## I. Weather Conditions

**Location:** Delhi / National Capital Region (NCR), India

Today was typical of Delhi’s monsoon spell in August: the morning began cool and humid, following light showers that continued until just past dawn. Temperatures climbed from 27°C in the early hours to a midday high near 36°C. Humidity remained between 65% and 80% throughout the day, contributing to a persistently muggy atmosphere. The wind was gentle, coming from the southeast at 6–12 km/h.

The early morning rain thoroughly moistened the garden beds, which lent a noticeable freshness to the air. However, this same dampness, combined with overcast skies by afternoon, means I must remain vigilant for fungal issues on susceptible plants. The thick cloud cover provided a restful break for shade-loving and indoor plants, protecting them from intense sunlight.

## II. Today’s Gardening Activities

### 1. Mindful Morning Walk

I began the day with a slow, observant walk through the garden just after sunrise, taking in the scent of wet earth lingering from the early rainfall. Birds were particularly vocal, and I paused to watch a pair of mynas hopping between the rose bushes. On the marigolds, I noticed several ladybirds crawling purposefully along the leaves—a natural reassurance against aphids. Where the soil was freshly turned near the jasmine patch, several small earthworm castings indicated active, healthy soil life.

This daily ritual grounds me and allows an opportunity to notice subtle changes—a shift in leaf color, increased insect activity, or the first sign of disease. The garden felt especially alive today, glistening with raindrops and cooled by the passing breeze.

### 2. Inspection of Tulsi and Marigold

#### Tulsi (Ocimum sanctum)
The tulsi plants in the courtyard’s northeast raised bed looked particularly vigorous this morning, exhibiting approximately 3 centimeters of fresh growth since last week. The leaves were deep green and robust to touch. When inspecting closely, I found two small clusters of aphids settled on the lower stems. The affected leaves were slightly sticky from aphid secretions. I handpicked and discarded those leaves, then misted the remaining stems with a dilute neem oil solution, following standard organic practice. The spicy aroma released by the tulsi was strong, a good sign of essential oil synthesis and plant health.

#### Marigold (Tagetes erecta)
The marigold bed on the southwest side, which receives full sunlight, seemed to benefit from the rain, with most plants upright and in full bloom. However, one plant was noticeably wilted, its stems weak and leaves slightly yellowed. A check of the soil confirmed it was damp, but not, thankfully, waterlogged. Suspecting mild root stress, I cleared some of the lower, crowded foliage to improve airflow, pruned spent blossoms, and added an extra layer of mulch to control weeds and reduce soil splash. I also took a moment to watch bees and butterflies busily visiting the flowers, a small reward for my efforts.

### 3. Repotting Indoor Ferns

With the weather relatively cool indoors, I decided it was a good day to repot my Boston and Maidenhair ferns. Both had become root-bound; the Boston fern on the primary north-facing window in the drawing room had crowded roots and some yellowing of the oldest fronds. The Maidenhair, occupying a spot on the study’s corner shelf, showed browning at the tips, a typical sign of moisture stress and restricted root growth.

I carefully transferred each into a slightly larger ceramic pot, using a sterilized mix of peat, compost, and added vermiculite for moisture regulation. After pruning away dead or damaged fronds, I watered both thoroughly, taking care not to saturate the roots. The plants are now positioned for indirect sunlight to help them recover from the repotting process. I felt a sense of satisfaction handling these delicate ferns—every tug and shake reminded me of their fragility and resilience.

### 4. Planting a New Jasmine Sapling (Jasminum sambac)

Later in the morning, I planted a new jasmine sapling along the east-facing garden border, an area that receives generous morning light. I took care to prepare a hole twice as wide as the root ball, mixing in aged compost to encourage early root development. The sapling’s roots were bright white and fibrous—a reliable indicator of health—and its leaves glossy and unmarred. Finding earthworm castings in the planting spot was extra assurance of soil fertility.

For stability, I lightly staked the plant and applied an insulating layer of coconut husk mulch around the base. Mr. Ramesh, our gardener, was present, and I reviewed with him the essential aftercare: water only in the mornings, remain cautious about overwatering during this muggy monsoon, and regularly check the stake to avoid any stem damage.

## IV. Reflections and Recommendations for Mr. Ramesh

### Personal Reflections

Today reinforced the importance of mindful, attentive observation in gardening. Small signs—like the slightly sticky leaves on tulsi or the wilting marigold—can indicate issues before they escalate. Timely intervention not only preserves plant vitality but also ensures less reliance on medicines or remedies later.

### Guidance for Mr. Ramesh

- **Tulsi and Marigold:** Check beneath leaves every couple of days for aphids or signs of disease. Continue gentle, organic pest management—neem oil is effective but should be used at the first sign of trouble, not as a routine spray. Introduce more ladybirds and lacewings to the garden for natural pest control.  
- **Fern Maintenance:** Only up-pot ferns when roots are clearly congested. Maintain moist but not soggy soil; both under- and overwatering can cause stress. Regularly wipe down fern leaves to keep them dust-free and promote healthy respiration indoors.  
- **Jasmine Care:** The new sapling will benefit from sturdy staking during its first few months. During the monsoon, only water when the topsoil feels dry to prevent root rot. Keep an eye on the mulch layer and replenish as needed to moderate soil temperature and moisture.  
- **Ethical Gardening:** Follow ICAR and NGT guidelines by using compost, organic mulches, and safe pest management options. Recycle all garden debris—including trimmed leaves, spent blooms, and pruned branches—into the compost pile to create a closed-loop of nutrient cycling. Maintain clean tools and hands before working with different plant species to minimize the risk of spreading disease.  
- **Garden Record-Keeping:** Make a habit of noting everyday changes, new blooms, pest sightings, or even shifts in plant color. These records will help track patterns, successes, and setbacks, allowing for better decision-making throughout the growing season.
