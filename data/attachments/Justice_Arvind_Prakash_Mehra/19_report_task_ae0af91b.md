## Observations

### Marigold Interplanting and Aphid Management

One of my ongoing experiments has involved interplanting marigolds (Tagetes spp.) among the vegetable beds. Today, I focused on the marigold borders, examining how this companion planting was faring against aphid infestations in the surrounding crops. Marigolds are widely recognized for their natural pest-repelling abilities—their strong scent and bioactive compounds, particularly thiophenes, tend to deter pests and attract beneficial insects. As I checked the leaves, I noticed relatively fewer aphids on vegetables closest to the marigold rows compared to those farther away, an effect supported by recent research. Lady beetles and lacewings seem more frequent in these sections, and their presence is reassuring given their appetites for aphids.

Supporting this, studies indicate that marigold intercropping can disrupt aphid host-finding by masking the visual and chemical signals of target plants, while simultaneously drawing in predators that keep pest numbers down [1]. Observing this process firsthand not only helps validate these claims but encourages me to continue expanding marigold use within the garden’s design.

**References:**  
1. Mkenda, P. A., et al. (2015). "Comparative Effects of Marigold Intercrops on Pest Incidence and Yield of Vegetable Crops." _International Journal of Pest Management._  
2. Sarkar, S., & Saha, T. (2018). "Role of Marigold in Pest Management." _Indian Journal of Entomology._

### Wildlife Encounter: Bulbuls Nesting in Hibiscus

While passing the hibiscus bush near the garden path, I paused to watch a pair of red-vented bulbuls (_Pycnonotus cafer_) busily weaving twigs into a nest. Their lively activity added a cheerful backdrop to the morning—one bird gathering material while the other guarded the site. In Delhi, bulbuls are a familiar sight, and I always welcome their presence; not only do they help naturally control pests and disperse seeds, but their nesting here is a sign the garden remains welcoming and healthy.

I was careful not to approach too closely, respecting both their space and their protection under the Wildlife Protection Act (1972), which safeguards nesting bulbuls from interference [2]. Watching them work reminded me that gardening supports biodiversity on many levels, from soil microbes to birds, and that every resident plays a role in the ecosystem.
