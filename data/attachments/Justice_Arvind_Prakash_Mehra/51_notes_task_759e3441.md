## Introduction

Tonight's streaming session brought our family together around the evocative artistry of Pandit Ajoy Chakrabarty, whose Hindustani classical recitals are rooted deep within the Patiala Gharana tradition. I arranged this listening experience not only as a way to unwind after a busy day, but also as an opportunity to strengthen our familial bonds through shared appreciation of cultural heritage and music. My spouse joined in with her insights from previous concerts, and both children contributed thoughtful responses during the recital. This time, our youngest member—my seven-year-old grandchild—listened in for the first time. Watching reactions across three generations underscored the power of music to connect us.

## Concert Program and Highlights

We selected a curated segment of the recital, representing a journey through mood and tradition:

| Composition Title                  | Raag         | Duration | Notable Features                                                                                                                      |
|------------------------------------|--------------|----------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Vilambit Khayal: Bandish in Ektal  | Raag Marwa   | 25 min   | Slow, meditative phrasing with subtle chromatic undertones. Raag Marwa is often performed as dusk settles, its introspective nature reflecting philosophical ideas about change and impermanence. Chakrabarty's approach revealed the raag’s emotional ambiguity and depth. |
| Drut Khayal: Bandish in Teental    | Raag Marwa   | 12 min   | Faster tempo, brighter melodic lines. Here, the energy of the performance marked a significant emotional shift, echoing the Gharana tradition’s structured transitions from contemplation to brilliance. |
| Semi-Classical Thumri: Bol Banaav  | Raag Khamaj  | 10 min   | Playful and romantic, this Thumri showcased the expressive variety of Hindustani classical music. Raag Khamaj’s resonances with love and longing were brought alive here, with hints of the courtly Lucknow tradition dating to the 19th century.|
| Bhajan: Vaishnav Jan To            | Raag Bhairavi| 8 min    | A devotional song, rendered with warmth and spiritual calm. Bhairavi is revered as a 'universal' closing raag in classical recitals, and the bhajan itself is woven into India's spiritual and national heritage.|

Many of these compositions belong to the public domain, protected and preserved through generations. Indian copyright law recognizes the classical bandishes and devotional songs used here as free for private, family use; pieces like “Vaishnav Jan To” and traditional thumris have long outlasted author-centric restrictions (see [4]). The Patiala Gharana lineage that Pandit Chakrabarty represents lent the entire recital an air of historical authenticity.

## Reflections: Mood, Wellbeing, and Family Connections

As dusk fell and the Marwa bandishes unfolded, I found myself enveloped by an unusual sense of calm—a feeling echoed by my spouse, who remarked on the raag’s ability to smooth the transition from day to night. The slow Vilambit section allowed us to decompress, while the quickening Drut energised the atmosphere, shifting us gently from introspection to vibrance. My daughter noted that the music felt “like a conversation between silence and light,” and even my son—usually reserved in his musical tastes—was drawn into the discussion.

The Thumri brought smiles and a lighthearted change of pace. Its playful lyrics prompted memories of earlier family gatherings, while the Bhajan inspired quiet reflection. Midway through “Vaishnav Jan To,” my grandchild asked what the song meant. This simple question opened a dialogue about devotional music, Gandhian values, and personal faith, sparking stories that linked our own family history to the wider Indian tradition.

Music’s capacity to assist in emotional regulation and recovery is well established ([5], [6]), and I witnessed this during our session. Tensions from the workday seemed to dissolve, replaced by gentle conversation and laughter. Sharing the performance in real time fostered a sense of togetherness—more than any planned activity could.

Family listening also serves as a vehicle for cultural transmission. By experiencing these ragas together, we honor oral storytelling traditions and make space for spontaneous learning. For the younger generation, these moments can nurture a lifelong curiosity, and for us, they represent continuity of values and experiences.

## Cultural, Legal and Wellness Contexts

We paid close attention to copyright rules, streaming the recital only through verified platforms and relying on works recognized as public domain ([4]). Indian Copyright Act (1957) is clear that sharing such music with family for private enjoyment is fully compliant with legal standards.

Beyond legality, our evening reflected broader cultural practices supported by bodies such as the Ministry of Culture and AYUSH ([7]). Wellness initiatives increasingly incorporate the arts—and classical music has featured prominently in community health strategies. Integrating these traditions at home feels both healing and culturally affirming.

The thematic choices in tonight's selections—spiritual devotion, introspection, celebration—echo values central to our family life. Each musical moment reinforced respect for heritage, the importance of mindful practice, and a shared sense of joy.

## Next Steps and Emerging Questions

The positive outcomes of tonight’s listening encourage us to continue this tradition. We plan to explore other twilight ragas—Puriya and Shree—in future sessions, comparing their emotional impact with Marwa's. Experimenting with additional semi-classical genres such as Dadra and Kajri also promises further variation; these forms bring their own flavors of lightness and storytelling.

I’m especially interested in learning how different ragas influence mood across generations. For example, would Bhairavi’s universal appeal evoke the same response in young listeners as it does in adults? And how are music-based therapies formally recognized in current healthcare and wellness guidelines in India?
