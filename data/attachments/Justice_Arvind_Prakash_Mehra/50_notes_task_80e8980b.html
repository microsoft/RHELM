Gardening Journal Entry

September 3, 2024

Time of Observation: 7:15 AM
Location: Home Garden

Weather Overview

Waking up this morning, I found the garden draped in a gentle mist, the overnight rain still lingering on every leaf. According to the Indian Meteorological Department, last night’s rainfall brought a steady 23 mm—enough to saturate the soil but not cause flooding. The air was heavy with humidity at 94%, and the temperature was a pleasant 24°C. With overcast skies and an occasional drizzle, the typical late-monsoon pattern continues: mild temperatures and moist air, both a blessing and a challenge for the garden. The rainfall this week has exceeded 120 mm, nourishing every inch of green while keeping me attentive to signs of excess moisture, especially around roots and dense foliage.

Garden Observations

Plant Name Detailed Observations Impact of the Monsoon Season
Hibiscus The hibiscus bushes are looking especially vibrant—a shock of glossy green leaves, topped with several big crimson blossoms. I noticed minor yellowing on two lower leaves, and a cluster of aphids gathering on some of the younger shoots. The ongoing rainfall is giving the hibiscus everything it needs for lush growth and prolific flowering. However, the minor leaf yellowing points to nutrients being leached out by constant rain, a common problem in prolonged wet spells. With high humidity, sap-sucking insects like aphids seem more active, and I know these damp conditions can spark fungal issues if I’m not watchful.
Marigold Marigold plants are standing tall, with strong stems and a burst of orange blooms. Two flower heads, though, have begun to brown and emit a faint musty smell around their base. The marigolds have clearly benefited from the consistent monsoon rains—full, vigorous, and brightly colored. But I could sense that the soil at their roots is almost too wet, a classic case during the late monsoon, leading to slight bloom decay and that ‘earthy’ odor I associate with root saturation. If the rain continues, I’ll have to be alert for root rot or damping-off, both of which threaten marigolds in soggy soil [2].
Jasmine I spotted a fresh new green shoot, about 5 centimeters high, sprouting from the base of the main stem. Although the number of flowers has dropped since last week, the leaves are healthy and bright. There’s a light dinner-moss coating clinging to some of the older stems. The high humidity and moist soil make this the perfect season for jasmine to send out new growth. That tender shoot is evidence of how well the plant is regenerating, while the rich green leaves speak to its overall health. Jasmine tends to pause flowering in the heaviest rains, focusing instead on leafy expansion. Moss on the old stems is usually harmless—just another reminder of constant moisture—but I keep a close eye for any sign of stem rot.

New Discoveries

This morning’s walk brought a welcome surprise: a new jasmine shoot emerging at the base of the old plant, standing just about five centimeters high. This kind of vegetative regrowth doesn’t happen every season. The abundance of rain has clearly triggered dormant buds, and the soft, aerated soil has given the young shoot a perfect start.

From a gardening perspective, these new shoots are encouraging—they show that the jasmine is responding well to consistent rainfall and the rich, moist soil of the monsoon. Such burst of growth also help the plant fill out and reinforce ground cover around its base, which can stabilize soil and provide shelter for tiny beneficial insects.

However, with thick new foliage and dense groundcover, I’m aware that airflow around the plant can become limited. If this lush growth continues, I may need to thin out some shoots to prevent mildew or fungal problems that thrive in these damp conditions.

Personal Reflections

This morning, I wandered through the garden, umbrella in hand, pausing at each bed to breathe in the softened air and listen to the rain’s steady patter through the leaves. Walking in the garden after a night of rain always has a calming effect—it’s as though the world quiets down, and all that matters are the immediate sights and scents around me. The fragrance of damp earth and soaked foliage carries a memory of childhood monsoons, reminding me why I started gardening in the first place.

There’s an intimacy in these moments—examining the subtle fade of green at a hibiscus leaf’s edge, or watching a new jasmine shoot push up through darkened mulch. Gardening gives structure to my days since retirement, offering a meaningful kind of physical routine and connecting me to the cycles of the natural world. I’ve come to value these small rituals of observation—kneeling by a marigold’s roots to test the soil, or gently brushing away aphids from a hibiscus stem—almost as deeply as the blooms themselves.

I also recognize, as research and my own experience confirm [2], that gardening brings psychological benefits: a lift in mood, a reduction in stress, and a reassuring sense of purpose. Every daily visit reinforces that rhythm of growth and change, balancing the comfort of the familiar with the intrigue of the unexpected. The assurance that something new—a bud, a leaf, a shoot—can emerge after the rain feels both grounding and quietly hopeful.

Summary

The monsoon’s reliable presence this year has made the garden particularly lush but also demands careful stewardship to manage excessive moisture and the risks of disease. Every morning walk is an invitation to notice small changes, to act as caretaker, and to find joy in the details of living things responding to their environment. The restorative power of these rituals feels like an investment—not just in the beauty of the garden, but in my own sense of well-being and vitality.
