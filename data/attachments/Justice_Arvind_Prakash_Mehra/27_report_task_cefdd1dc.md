## Introduction

This seminar examines the profound evolution of justice within Indian legal history, tracing its roots from the country’s colonial experience through the birth and continued development of the Constitution. The journey of Indian constitutional justice is illuminated by a series of pivotal Supreme Court decisions, as well as highly regarded scholarship that has influenced public and academic understanding of justice in India. Drawing upon Amartya Sen’s *The Idea of Justice*, the discussion navigates both philosophical groundwork and the practical realities shaping contemporary legal thought and practice. We will also assess recent advancements in constitutional interpretation and social justice initiatives, considering how India’s experience both mirrors and diverges from other international constitutional models.

---

## The Evolution of Justice in Indian Legal History

### Constitutional Foundations

Following independence, the Indian Constitution—adopted in 1950—emerged as a carefully crafted response to the injustices endured under colonial rule. Its Preamble declares justice—social, economic, and political—as central to national life, setting the moral compass for the country’s legal framework. Fundamental Rights, enshrined in Part III, particularly Articles 14, 19, and 21, are not merely legal provisions but represent an enduring commitment to individual dignity, equality, and liberty.[1]

Article 14 guarantees equality before the law and equal protection to all, laying the foundation for non-discrimination. Article 19 enumerates vital freedoms essential to democratic participation, while Article 21’s broad protection of life and personal liberty has become a cornerstone for safeguarding human rights. These provisions operate collectively to establish an environment where justice is not simply a constitutional promise, but a living practice.

### Landmark Supreme Court Precedents

The strength and adaptability of the Constitution have been shaped by landmark Supreme Court decisions, each expanding the meaning and scope of justice in India.

- **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973):** This transformative judgment introduced the “basic structure” doctrine, which asserts the inviolability of fundamental constitutional features—such as the rule of law, the separation of powers, and judicial review—even against constitutional amendments.[2] The ruling acts as a bulwark, ensuring that the spirit of the Constitution endures through political change.

- **Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978):** In this case, the Supreme Court broadened the interpretation of Article 21, insisting that any law affecting liberty must meet standards of fairness, reasonableness, and due process.[3] The decision marked a decisive shift from a narrow, procedural approach to a more substantive understanding of justice and individual rights.

- **Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018):** By decriminalizing consensual same-sex relations, the Court underscored constitutional morality as a guiding doctrine, expanding protections for marginalized groups and affirming the community’s right to dignity and autonomy.[4] This judgment reflects the judiciary's responsiveness to evolving societal values and the imperative of social justice.

These precedents collectively showcase the Court’s willingness to interpret the Constitution not as a static text, but as a dynamic instrument for delivering justice in a changing society.

## Engaging with Amartya Sen’s "The Idea of Justice"

Amartya Sen’s *The Idea of Justice* offers a compelling critique of justice as simply adherence to established institutional rules (*niti*), advocating instead for a vision of realized justice (*nyaya*) that emphasizes outcomes and people’s everyday experiences.[5] Sen challenges us to ask not just whether fair procedures exist, but whether they actually produce fair consequences.

The Indian judiciary’s evolving approach reflects this philosophy. Courts increasingly look past technical compliance and interrogate the real-life impact of constitutional guarantees, striving to bridge the gap between abstract rights and their actual realization. For instance, judicial interventions to promote access to education, healthcare, and protection from discrimination show an increasing concern with substantive, lived justice.

This orientation has led the courts to actively address persistent inequalities—whether along lines of caste, gender, or economic status—ensuring justice is tangible, rather than remote. Sen’s framework thus finds clear resonance in recent judgments that prioritize inclusion and meaningful rights over mere formalism.

## Recent Developments in Indian Law

### Constitutional Interpretation

The judiciary’s robust commitment to constitutional guardianship is most clearly expressed through the mechanisms of judicial review, especially under Articles 32 and 226, which empower citizens to challenge violations of their rights in the Supreme Court and High Courts respectively.[6] Recent rulings have demonstrated increasing responsiveness to contemporary challenges—digital privacy, freedom of expression in the age of social media, and recognition of new forms of family and relationships.

These decisions reflect an evolving constitutional interpretation that is firmly rooted in basic values, yet sensitive to social transformation and global developments.

### Social Justice and Access to Justice

Over the past decades, the Supreme Court and other courts have taken meaningful steps to realize social justice—not only through expansive readings of rights, but through direct interventions. Efforts include:

- Upholding affirmative action policies designed to redress historic disadvantage suffered by Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and Other Backward Classes.
- Recognizing privacy as a fundamental right, thereby reinforcing personal autonomy and dignity.
- Providing greater legal protection to marginalized groups, including women, children, and sexual minorities, as demonstrated in judgments like *Navtej Singh Johar*.

However, persistent challenges remain. Overburdened court dockets, uneven access to legal resources—particularly in rural and underserved regions—and low public legal awareness frequently obstruct the full and equal realization of justice. Addressing these systemic barriers is essential if constitutional principles are to have a transformative societal impact.

## Comparative International Perspectives

India’s constitutional framework shares notable similarities with post-war constitutional models in countries such as South Africa and Germany. Each of these constitutions affirms basic rights, rule of law, and the importance of judicial review to safeguard democratic governance.[7]

Yet, India’s approach is distinctly shaped by its unique history, cultural diversity, and intricate social realities. The adaptation of universal legal principles to fit local traditions and community needs has produced a constitutional system marked by both resilience and flexibility. Indian courts routinely blend global standards with indigenous practices, demonstrating an ability to innovate and reinterpret law in ways that resonate with the nation’s lived experience.

International comparisons provide valuable insights, but India’s exceptional blend of inclusiveness, judicial creativity, and social sensitivity set its legal landscape apart.

---

## Summary of Key Arguments

- Achieving constitutional justice demands both principled interpretation and practical implementation, balancing aspiration and reality.
- Judicial decision-making must be grounded in enduring constitutional values, resisting short-term political trends or popular pressures.
- Landmark Supreme Court precedents illustrate a bold engagement with the Constitution, often driving social change and expanding both the letter and spirit of the law.
- Comparative international analysis enriches legal understanding, but India’s historical experiences and social composition require thoughtful adaptation, not wholesale adoption.
- The continued realization of meaningful justice depends on upholding constitutional morality and securing the independent integrity of the judiciary amidst societal change.

## Conclusion

The trajectory of Indian legal history is characterized by a persistent quest for justice—a dialogue between constitutional principles, judicial courage, and the complex realities of Indian society. The judiciary’s independence and moral commitment remain crucial as new challenges arise and the nation continues to evolve. Looking ahead, strengthening fairness, inclusivity, and constitutional fidelity will be vital in ensuring that the promise of justice remains an active and effective force in people’s lives.
