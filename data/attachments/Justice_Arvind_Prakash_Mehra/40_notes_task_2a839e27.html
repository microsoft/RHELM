Music Journal Entry: August 14, 2024 – Evening Recital of Raga Bhimpalasi

Recital Overview

Date: August 14, 2024
Time: 7:30 PM – 9:00 PM IST
Duration: 1 hour 30 minutes

Performance Details

Tonight, I immersed myself in a sublime digital concert featuring Pandit Bhimsen Joshi’s interpretation of Raga Bhimpalasi. The session, accessed via the Saregama Classical platform, transported me back to a storied 1986 Mumbai Sangeet Samaroh recital—a meditative listening experience that enriched my evening and resonated deeply across our household.

Artists & Ensemble

  • Lead Vocal: Pandit Bhimsen Joshi (Kirana Gharana), whose commanding yet sensitive voice filled the room—unmediated by digital enhancements.
  • Harmonium: Pandit Tulsidas Borkar, playing a meticulously crafted Hiren Roy harmonium that supported and conversed with the vocal lines.
  • Tabla: Pandit Suresh Talwalkar, whose percussive artistry on tablas from Ustad Nisar Khan’s atelier set a rich, rhythmic foundation.

Instruments

Each instrument contributed its own timbral identity, yet blended seamlessly—a testament to the ensemble’s artistry and deep mutual understanding. The absence of electronic manipulation allowed each nuance to shine: Joshi’s earthy vocal texture, the harmonium’s warm reedy undertones, and the tabla’s crisp yet rounded tones.

Raga and Recital Structure

The performance explored Raga Bhimpalasi, an afternoon melody from the Kafi thaat, widely cherished for its poignant mood (viraha rasa—longing or separation). The musical journey unfolded in the classic Hindustani sequence: a slow, unmetered alap, followed by a grand vilambit khayal in Teen Taal, then a brisk drut khayal in Ektal, and culminating in exhilarating taranas. Each section was distinct yet formed part of a unified emotional arc.

I discovered this recording through the Saregama official classical archive, which helpfully preserved the original program order and artist information.

Musical Analysis and Observations

Structural Nuances of Bhimpalasi

Bhimpalasi, pentatonic in ascent (Arohana: Ni Sa Ga Ma Pa Ni Sa) and heptatonic in descent (Avarohana: Sa Ni Dha Pa Ma Ga Re Sa), demands sensitive treatment, especially around its signature phrases (Pa Ma Ga, Re Sa). Joshi explored these characteristics with purposeful restraint, never departing from the raga’s grammar. The meend—long, gliding motions between Ma and Ga—were delivered with remarkable clarity and atmosphere.

Pandit Joshi’s Approach

The evening’s alap was extended and introspective. Joshi savored the lower and middle registers, blending bol-alap and nom-tom phrases, letting each note unfold and resonate. His attention to microtonal shifts and dhrupad-inspired intonation, especially at the start, set a contemplative tone. The transition to the vilambit khayal revealed how Joshi weaves swara purity with incredibly emotive gamakas (ornamentations), allowing phrases to bloom organically.

When rhythm entered the equation, Pandit Suresh Talwalkar’s tabla punctuated the calm with gentle cadences, highlighting the sam (first beat) without overwhelming the melody. As tempo increased in the drut section, the interaction between tabla and voice became thrilling: swift taans (rapid melodic runs) soared across octaves, culminating in sparkling tarana segments. Each tihai provided closure to spontaneous explorations, while the harmonium echoed and embellished, creating a tapestry of melodic responses.

Comparison with Other Gharanas

Unlike the Jaipur-Atrauli Gharana’s latticework of angular taans and dense architecture, the Kirana style—exemplified tonight—favored smooth, unhurried unfolding of each swara. Joshi’s approach prioritized the long, expressive sustenance of notes, particularly on Ma and Pa, drawing listeners into a devotional realm. There was a robustness and open-heartedness here, less cerebral yet deeply moving—a marked contrast to, say, Kishori Amonkar’s introspective Bhimpalasi in the Jaipur tradition.

Technical Mastery

The session highlighted Joshi’s remarkable breath control, especially apparent in the taans and tarana. His sound in the upper octave (tar saptak) sustained its clarity and power, while the harmonium’s interplay offered gentle reminders of the melody’s home base. There was no trace of confusion between Bhimpalasi and its allied ragas like Patdeep or Bageshree; Joshi’s phrasing remained unwaveringly true to the raganga and its saturated mood of gentle yearning.

Family Reflections and Interactions

This recital became more than a personal study; it animated our living room with a sense of shared participation across generations.

Family Involvement

  • Priya (daughter, 14): Sat beside me throughout, intently sketching the unfolding alap and tarana passages. She asked about the subtle differences between the Kirana and Jaipur styles, showing a curiosity that made our listening communal and inquisitive.
  • Sunita (spouse): Listened with visible recall, sharing recollections of childhood baithaks where Bhimpalasi was often sung during summer dusk. Her memories enriched the emotional color of the evening.
  • Rohit (son, 17): Initially distracted, but as the tempo picked up during the taans and tarana, he leaned in, commenting on the complexity of the rhythmic cycles and recording a brief audio snippet of a phrase that caught his ear.

Intergenerational Dialogue

Our exchanges bridged diverse perspectives. Priya and Sunita related the raga’s motifs to festivals and family gatherings—a reminder of how music suture memories across time. Priya’s probing led us to discuss how gharanas evolve and the way contemporary artists juxtapose innovation with tradition. Rohit, inspired by his sister’s engagement, started bookmarking passages, carefully saving and annotating his favorite muraqaba (phrase) for later comparison and study.

Creative Artifacts and Family Traditions

Digital Keepsakes

I saved key segments—the alap’s exploration of the Pa–Ma–Ga motif, and the final tarana—through the Saregama app for future listening and study. Rohit’s 25-second voice note, preserved with his reflections, captured the excitement of discovery and the family’s growing investment in classical music.

Handmade Bookmark

Priya’s hand-drawn bookmark became a tangible memento of the night. She illustrated a stylized tanpura, its neck curving upward against a wash of watercolor waves that echoed the alap’s unfolding. At the base, she wrote “भीमपलासि” in graceful Devanagari, dating her art as a future keepsake. This bookmark now nestles in our family music journal—an emblem of our continuing reverence for tradition and the creative harmony between sound and image.

Closing Thoughts

This evening’s session was more than a solitary act of listening. The interplay of music, memory, and conversation fostered new connections—not just to the artistry of Pandit Bhimsen Joshi and his fellow musicians, but also within our family. Through Bhimpalasi’s yearning notes, we rediscovered how tradition and creativity, intellect and emotion, flow together—helping us weave the story of who we are, and where we come from.
