## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Case Background

The case at hand before the Supreme Court confronted a contentious executive decision that severely curtailed public access to digital platforms. This administrative order was issued without clear legislative backing and sparked a nationwide debate, reflecting the growing friction between governmental authority and personal autonomy within an increasingly digitalized society. The petitioners argued that the restrictions violated Article 21 of the Constitution, which guarantees personal liberty, and asserted that the digital age only amplifies the need for constitutional protections against arbitrary state power.

In reviewing this matter, I recognized how it encapsulated a modern constitutional challenge: as technology permeates every dimension of life, the possibilities for state action to impact personal liberty have multiplied. Our Court was called upon to test the resilience of enduring constitutional principles against the complexities of a rapidly evolving society.

### 1.2 Constitutional Issues Presented

Three interrelated constitutional questions emerged as the heart of this dispute:

- **Scope of Article 21:** Whether executive curbs on access to digital information constitute an unlawful deprivation of personal liberty.
- **Procedural Safeguards:** Whether the phrase “procedure established by law” in Article 21 demands robust requirements of fairness, non-arbitrariness, and proportionality, beyond mere adherence to statutory form.
- **Balancing Interests:** How to balance state interests in security and public order with the individual’s rights to liberty and privacy in a technology-driven society.

Each question called for deeper reflection on the adaptability of constitutional guarantees to new forms of state interference and the necessity that judicial interpretation remains responsive and principled.

### 1.3 Major Precedents

Supreme Court jurisprudence has consistently affirmed and expanded the scope of Article 21:

- **Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978):** The Court declared that any law depriving a person of liberty must be just, fair, and reasonable—not merely comply with formal procedures. This decision transformed the landscape of fundamental rights in India, infusing “procedure established by law” with substantive standards.[1]
- **K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017):** Our recognition of privacy as an inalienable component of personal liberty was a watershed moment. The judgment reaffirmed the primacy of informational autonomy, requiring that state-imposed restrictions withstand the test of necessity and proportionality, based on compelling public interest.[2]
- **Indian Express Newspapers v. Union of India (1985):** This precedent highlighted the critical role of judicial dissent in nurturing constitutional development and protecting minority viewpoints.[3]

Over time, these cases have laid a foundation emphasizing substantive justice and judicial vigilance, especially in the face of executive overreach.

### 1.4 The Importance and Legacy of Judicial Dissent

Judicial dissent has played a transformative role in Indian constitutional history. Dissents—such as that of Justice H.R. Khanna in ADM Jabalpur—have often planted the seeds for future reform, serving as a moral compass for both the judiciary and society.[4] To dissent is not merely to disagree with the majority, but to uphold the deeper spirit of the Constitution whenever fundamental rights are threatened. For me, the act of dissent is inseparable from the duties of a Supreme Court judge: it is an embodiment of fidelity to constitutional order and an assurance that the evolving concept of justice remains open to principled contestation, regardless of popular sentiment or immediate institutional pressure.

## 2. Dissenting Opinion

### 2.1 Article 21 and the Nature of ‘Personal Liberty’

Article 21 declares: “No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.” In my view, this guarantee is designed to shield every person from arbitrary exercise of State power. The Maneka Gandhi ruling made it clear that neither mere procedural formality nor ministerial compliance will suffice. Law must be substantive and rooted in fairness, and its application must be scrupulously just and reasonable.[1]

In the present case, the executive order restricting digital platform access bypassed legislative involvement, failed to prescribe objective criteria, and was unsupported by demonstrable necessity. Blanket digital restrictions, particularly on communication and access to information, strike at the heart of ‘personal liberty’ as envisioned in our Constitution. The right to liberty, as elucidated in K.S. Puttaswamy, encompasses not only physical movement but also privacy, autonomy of decision, and technological self-determination.[2] In the rapidly changing context of the digital age, these dimensions become inseparable from a person’s dignity and freedom. Administrative action that impedes such liberties—without procedural safeguards or proportional reasoning—undermines the constitutional architecture of Article 21.

### 2.2 Constitutional Reasoning and Judicial Duty

In reviewing the constitutionality of executive action, substance must triumph over mere paperwork. The doctrine of proportionality, propounded in Modern Dental College v. State of Madhya Pradesh, obliges the State to employ the least restrictive means in pursuit of legitimate objectives.[5] Restrictions must be necessary, evidence-based, and tailored so as not to eclipse the core of individual rights.

On examining the record, I found the State failed to reveal any clear legislative authority, specific threat, or compelling public interest justifying the sweeping restrictions placed on digital access. Neither fairness nor objectivity was evident in the measures adopted, nor were alternative, less intrusive remedies considered.

It is the judiciary’s solemn charge to maintain the sanctity of rights, especially when institutional pressures urge accommodation for executive expediency. As reflected in Indian Express Newspapers, dissent is often "the voice of the future," illuminating paths that the majority may overlook or reject.[3] My conviction is that judicial engagement must vigilantly protect constitutional values, even if it entails standing alone.

### 2.3 Analysis of Precedents and Comparative Jurisprudence

Throughout my career, I have found wisdom and guidance in comparative constitutional experience. The US Supreme Court in Griswold v. Connecticut famously insisted that privacy and personal choices demand the highest level of judicial scrutiny, setting strong limits on state interference.[6] In S v. Makwanyane, the South African Constitutional Court underscored the essential, non-negotiable aspects of life and liberty, arguing for robust safeguards against arbitrary deprivation by the State.[7]

These international precedents support my belief in the importance of interpreting Article 21 expansively, always erring on the side of freedom. Constitutional interpretation cannot be static; it must constantly respond to the transformation of society, technology, and the modes through which citizens interact with the State.

### 2.4 My Judicial Philosophy

For me, the role of a Supreme Court judge extends far beyond factual dispute resolution. It involves safeguarding those values the Constitution elevates above temporary politics or administrative expediency. The duty to dissent arises not from personal predilections but from an unwavering commitment to principle. Protecting unpopular rights or minority interests is often a solitary endeavor, but it is this very independence that keeps constitutional democracy vibrant and responsive.

In rendering this dissent, I am guided by the conviction that our constitutional promises must remain steadfast, resilient, and capable of weathering both the tides of technological change and the pressures of majoritarian politics. The executive action challenged here fails each constitutional test: there is neither lawful foundation, proportionality, nor procedural fairness. I believe the restrictions are incompatible with the expansive vision of liberty our Constitution demands. The right approach, even when inconvenient, is to set aside such measures and reinforce the protective boundaries the Constitution has drawn for each individual.
