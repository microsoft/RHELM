Session Discussion Summary

Analysis: Kesavananda Bharati Judgment

The session began with an in-depth discussion of Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala, a defining moment in India’s constitutional jurisprudence. Justice Mehra led the group through the context and reasoning of the judgment, emphasizing how the Supreme Court articulated the Basic Structure Doctrine—the idea that Parliament cannot amend the Constitution in ways that undermine its core principles. The dialogue included comparisons with earlier cases, notably Golaknath v. State of Punjab, which paved the way for a more assertive judicial stance on constitutional amendments.

Participants debated the scope of constituent power and the tension between parliamentary sovereignty and effective judicial review. The discussion referenced Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (NJAC Case), underscoring that judicial independence itself forms part of the Constitution’s basic structure. Justice Mehra highlighted practical examples of harmonization techniques in constitutional interpretation, encouraging mentees to reflect on how these methods influence the outcome of major constitutional cases.

Review: Recent Privacy Judgments

Attention then turned to the Supreme Court’s evolving approach to privacy:

  • Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (2017): The group reflected on the landmark expansion of Article 21 by the nine-judge bench, observing how the right to privacy now constitutes a central aspect of life and personal liberty. Justice Chandrachud’s reasoning—emphasizing dignity, autonomy, and informational privacy—was discussed at length.

  • Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020): The session analyzed the legal balancing act between state security interests and individual freedoms, specifically focusing on the procedural safeguards surrounding government-mandated internet shutdowns.

  • Aadhaar (Second Puttaswamy) Case (2019): The concept of proportionality, and its implications for state intervention in data protection and privacy, was carefully considered. Participants developed insights into how the Court calibrates necessity and reasonableness when assessing potential infringements of constitutional rights.

The discussion also explored the intersection of privacy rights with other constitutional guarantees, primarily Articles 14 and 19. The mentees drew connections between privacy jurisprudence and broader questions of necessity, fairness, and proportionality in state action.

Q&A: Evidence-Based Reasoning in Practice

During the interactive Q&A, mentees raised several practical questions:

  • Limits on Parliamentary Powers: What are the enforceable boundaries for Parliament when amending the Constitution, following Kesavananda Bharati?

  • Privacy Litigation and Evidentiary Standards: How do statutory protections and doctrinal limits apply to questions of surveillance, data usage, and privacy-sensitive evidence?

  • Ethical Obligations in Advocacy: What professional responsibilities do lawyers bear in privacy-related cases, particularly regarding evidence management and client confidentiality?

Justice Mehra responded with clear, detailed explanations. He discussed the relevance and application of formal legal reasoning, including the proportionality test and reliance on judicial precedent as codified under Article 141. He drew from comparative law perspectives and emphasized the ethical obligations set out by the Bar Council, reminding everyone that transparency and fidelity to facts must remain at the forefront of legal advocacy.

Key Takeaways and Professional Guidance

  • Judicial Review as Safeguard: The Supreme Court’s power of judicial review remains a bulwark for protecting fundamental rights.

  • Doctrinal Clarity and Analytical Rigor: Lawyers must master the scope and nuances of precedent to achieve clarity in argumentation and case analysis.

  • Ethical Standards: Ethical diligence is vital in handling privacy-centric cases, especially concerning client confidentiality and responsible advocacy.

  • Ongoing Learning: Session leaders encouraged mentees to continually engage with new developments in constitutional law, prepare well-researched briefs, and observe Supreme Court proceedings to enhance their professional competence.
