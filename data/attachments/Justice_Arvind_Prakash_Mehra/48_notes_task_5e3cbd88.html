Justice Arvind Prakash Mehra’s Classical Raga Playlist Journal

Date: 2024_09_01

Statement of Purpose

This entry marks the beginning of my music journal, assembled in the first week following my retirement from judicial service. As I step away from the rigor and responsibilities of the Supreme Court, I feel drawn to the serene discipline of Hindustani classical music—a tradition that, like law, offers both order and inspiration. My aim in curating this raga playlist is to harness the therapeutic, cognitive, and intellectual gifts of raga listening, helping me adjust to a slower rhythm of life while maintaining focus, inner balance, and creative engagement. The structured immersion in these timeless melodies is not merely recreational; it serves as a daily anchor, guiding me toward reflection, tranquility, and renewed purpose in this next chapter.

Analytical Commentary: Rationale for Playlist Choices

As a jurist, the discipline and complexity of classical ragas have always resonated deeply with me. Each raga in this playlist plays a deliberate role in shaping the emotional and mental contours of my daily routine.

Raga Yaman – Ustad Vilayat Khan (Imdadkhani / Etawah Gharana)

Yaman sets the perfect tone for my evenings. Its luminous phrases, marked by the sharp Ma (Tivra Madhyam), open up a spacious, contemplative mental field. When I listen to Vilayat Khan’s nuanced sitar interpretations, I notice a striking parallel with the logic and clarity demanded by the law—calm, poised, yet reaching for something beyond the ordinary. By immersing myself in Yaman as dusk falls and I settle in with a book or reflect on the day, I find my mind shifting from external obligations toward deeper self-awareness. Research suggests that Yaman is especially effective at reducing stress and promoting cognitive clarity during the transition from day to night.[3]

Raga Bhairav – Pandit Bhimsen Joshi (Kirana Gharana)

Mornings have always been my most sacred time. In this new phase, Bhairav forms the quiet backbone of my dawn ritual. The raga’s profound serenity and gravity, rendered so powerfully by Pandit Bhimsen Joshi, mirror the quiet determination I tried to bring to my courtroom every morning. I experience Bhairav as a call to stillness and order, gently sharpening my intent for the day ahead. Its somber beauty never fails to clear lingering anxieties, supporting mindfulness and daily renewal—essential in navigating the uncertainties of retirement.[5]

Raga Jog – Pandit Jasraj (Mewati Gharana)

My late evenings, once filled with last-minute legal briefs, now belong to the soothing, introspective flow of Jog. Pandit Jasraj’s expressive, devotional style brings to the raga a quality of intimate storytelling—inviting me to reflect not on outcomes, but on feelings and memories. The gentle tension between Jog’s pentatonic and hexatonic form feels like an echo of the nuanced reasoning in my former work, only here, it serves to unwind mental knots and welcome restful sleep. Each listening feels like a gentle bridge from outward activity to inward calm.

Raga Darbari Kanada – Ustad Amir Khan (Indore Gharana)

After dinner, with the day’s obligations behind me, I turn to the majestic solemnity of Darbari Kanada. Ustad Amir Khan’s profound, unhurried interpretation immerses me in a soundscape ripe for deep contemplation—much like the long, solitary walks I used to take after court sessions. The raga invites me to sit with ambiguity and complexity, which I now realize are not just features of legal argument, but of life itself. Listening to Darbari at night helps me cultivate acceptance, detachment, and emotional equilibrium.

Raga Shree – Pandit Ravi Shankar (Maihar Gharana)

Sunset is a time of transition, a natural boundary in the day. In the past, I often moved quickly through it; now I allow Shree to mark this passage. Ravi Shankar’s renditions blend innovation with deep tradition, embodying precisely the balance I seek in retirement: embracing change while honoring the familiar. Shree’s blend of solemnity and hopefulness resonates with my current stage of life, reminding me that every ending holds the promise of new beginnings.

Raga Multani – Ustad Rashid Khan (Rampur-Sahaswan Gharana)

Afternoons have always tested my focus, whether working through legal arguments or, now, reading for pleasure or taking notes. Multani, especially as sung by Rashid Khan, sharpens my mind without overstimulation—supporting sustained attention and contemplative study. The raga’s subtle yet energizing atmosphere makes it ideal for tasks requiring lucidity and patience. Drawing on scholarship linking mid-day ragas to cognitive performance, I have found Multani an effective partner for intellectual pursuits, old and new.[5]

Observed Effects on Mood, Cognition, and Well-being

The past few days of deliberate raga listening have already brought notable shifts:

  • Mood Regulation: I notice that raga sessions coincide with a smoother emotional rhythm throughout the day. The anxiety and restlessness that occasionally shadowed my retirement are tempered, replaced by a growing sense of groundedness and emotional resilience. The alignment of raga and routine has truly helped in this period of change.

  • Cognitive Clarity: The structural order in these compositions feels akin to preparing a legal brief—there’s space for both disciplined reasoning and creative insight. Particularly after listening to Yaman or Multani, my focus heightens, and I find myself able to sustain attention on longer reading or writing projects without mental fatigue.

  • Well-being and Ritual: Embedding music into my daily schedule has provided an anchoring sense of rhythm and purpose. This practice mimics the rituals of the judiciary—punctuating the day with moments of order and meaning. It has helped me stay connected to a sense of identity, even as I transition away from my judicial career.

  • Reflective Adaptation: Each raga’s distinct emotional world gently encourages me to explore new aspects of myself—not only as a judge, but as a listener, a student, and an individual. Inward stability and personal harmony are no less significant now than outward judiciousness once was.

My reflections draw on a combination of personal experience, classical treatises, Sangeet Natak Akademi’s analytical resources, and published studies on the therapeutic effects of Indian classical music (see sources below).
