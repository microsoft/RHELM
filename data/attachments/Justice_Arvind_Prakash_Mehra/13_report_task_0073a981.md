# Gardening Journal Entry: Planting Three Amaltas (Cassia fistula) Saplings  
### February 10, 2024 | New Delhi, India

---

## Date and Weather Conditions

**Date:** February 10, 2024  
**Location:** Private garden, South Delhi (adjacent to southern wall)

This morning, the garden was touched with the gentle warmth typical of early spring in Delhi. Temperatures ranged from a brisk 9°C at dawn to a comfortable 21°C by mid-morning, while the humidity hovered between 45% and 60%. The sky was clear, with bright sunshine filtering through a light, steady breeze from the northwest at 2–5 km/h. The UV index was low to moderate, making it pleasant to work outdoors. The soil surface was dry—no rainfall had occurred overnight or throughout the week, as confirmed by Indian Meteorological Department records [1].

These climate conditions proved ideal for transplanting saplings. The moderate temperatures and humidity created a gentle environment for roots to establish without the threat of waterlogging or pests. The absence of rain helped avoid soggy soil that can lead to fungal infections, and the mild wind encouraged healthy transpiration without causing stress to the freshly planted saplings.

## Planting Activities

**Time:** 8:00 to 10:30 a.m.

**Participants:**  
- Justice Arvind Prakash Mehra (lead gardener, documenting)  
- Mr. Ramesh (collaborator, hands-on planting and soil preparation)

The morning was cool and quiet—ideal for gardening before the heat of the day set in.

### 1. Site Selection and Layout

We began by carefully choosing three planting spots, each located 1.2 meters away from the southern boundary wall and spaced 2.5 meters apart. The layout followed established guidelines for urban planting of Cassia fistula [2], maximizing sunlight exposure (about 6–8 hours daily) while allowing the wall to shield the young trees from strong winter winds and the intense summer sun.

### 2. Soil Preparation and Compost Addition

Preparation began with digging three pits, each measuring 45 cm in diameter and 40 cm deep. We then mixed the removed soil with 8 kg of well-rotted farmyard manure for every pit, in accordance with horticultural standards [2], [3]. After composting, the resulting mixture felt noticeably more granular and friable, with a loamy-sandy texture and clear signs of organic matter. The pH measured about 7.2, and the moisture content rose to 28% by weight—an improvement that should support early root development.

### 3. Sapling Inspection and Planting

Three nursery-grown amaltas saplings were selected, all healthy and averaging 62 cm in height and 0.8 cm stem diameter. I inspected each root ball to verify integrity and moisture, pruning only minor damaged roots. The saplings were positioned upright in their respective pits, with root collars flush at the soil surface—a detail critical to preventing rot and encouraging robust growth.

### 4. Backfilling, Watering, and Mulching

After placing each sapling, we gently refilled the pits, firming the soil to remove air pockets. Each tree received 5 liters of tepid water, ensuring immediate contact between roots and moist soil. Once watered, I added a 2 cm layer of leaf litter around the base, intending to conserve soil moisture and buffer temperature swings.

### Live Observations

As we worked compost into the soil, its loose crumb structure became evident, with increased porosity compared to the original earth. No standing water appeared, and we spotted a few earthworms—always a reassuring sign of soil vitality. The saplings stood straight, their leaves turgid and vibrant; none exhibited signs of stress from transplantation.

## Soil and Composting Overview

**Compost Use:**  
For each planting pit, we blended 8 kg of farmyard manure with the garden’s natural loamy-sandy soil in equal measure. Cassia fistula thrives in well-drained, moderately fertile soil, and farmyard manure provides slow-release nutrients without overwhelming young root systems.

**Improvements Noted:**  
Post-composting, soil moisture surged from an 18% baseline to 28%, which should help roots survive the dry period common in Delhi’s late winter. The amended soil was more friable and structured, with an unambiguous boost in organic content and aeration. Notably, earthworm activity was present—an encouraging indicator of fertility and soil health.

**Nutrient Profile:**  
With manure fully integrated, nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium levels became moderately high, based on agronomic recommendations for local farmyard manure [4]. I deliberately avoided chemical fertilizers, relying on gradual nutrient release to support steady sapling growth.

**Previous Experience:**  
I’ve found in earlier plantings—particularly with hibiscus and neem—this blend of organic matter and native soil fosters strong establishment and early vigor, reducing transplant shock and supporting robust leaf and root development.

## Reflections

Today’s planting felt both purposeful and rewarding. The three amaltas saplings are positioned to take advantage of favorable microclimate and carefully amended soil. Past experience and local horticultural guidance point to rapid earliest establishment: shoots and roots should begin expanding within a month, and fresh leaves will likely emerge as early spring advances.

I’ll continue to monitor their progress through weekly visual inspections and monthly checks on height, leaf health, and soil moisture. The teamwork was a highlight—I appreciate Mr. Ramesh’s skillful handling of the root balls and his instinct for proper compost mixing. Our decision to work early in the day helped minimize transplant stress by avoiding high midday transpiration, and the proximity to the wall offers much-needed protection from Delhi’s wind.

Looking ahead, I intend to document the saplings’ growth with bimonthly journal updates and photographs, creating a record I hope will be useful for future plantings and community gardening projects.
