## II. Close Observation of the Rose Beds

### Aphid Infestation: Signs, Extent, and Contributing Factors

During my morning rounds, I spotted early signs of aphid activity on several of the rose bushes. Specifically, three out of the seven rose plants showed clusters of Aphis rosae—the small green aphids—mostly concentrated on the tender upper stems and newly forming buds. Thankfully, less than 10% of the overall foliage seemed affected at this stage.

I noticed a few telltale signs: the youngest leaves on affected stems were beginning to curl, some surfaces felt tacky due to the characteristic honeydew excreted by the aphids, and the blooms on one of my hybrid teas appeared less vigorous than usual. Given last night’s mild temperatures and high humidity, conditions were unfortunately ideal for aphid proliferation.

The foliage here in late winter is still quite dense, which creates little pockets of stagnant air where pests thrive. I also realized there was a conspicuous lack of ladybird larvae—usually reliable partners in controlling aphid populations. It seems I caught this outbreak in its earliest stage; experience tells me that timely attention now saves much trouble later.

## III. Neem Oil Treatment: Preparation and Procedure

Drawing on best practices from the Bureau of Indian Standards and guidance from the Neem Foundation [4], I prepared a traditional neem oil spray to tackle the aphids organically and safely.

### A. Preparation

**Ingredients:**  
- Neem oil (cold-pressed, certified): 5 ml  
- Pure, unscented liquid soap (emulsifier): 1 ml  
- Drinking water at room temperature: 1 litre

**Equipment:**  
- 1.5L pressure sprayer (checked in advance for leaks)
- Sterile, food-grade mixing vessel (2L capacity)
- Gloves (nitrile) and safety goggles (personal protective equipment)
- Syringe and measuring jug for accuracy

**Method:**  
Wearing my gloves and goggles, I first combined the neem oil and soap, stirring gently until a milky emulsion appeared. Next, I gradually added water—stirring all the while to keep the mixture uniform—then transferred the final blend into the sprayer.

All mixing and spraying was done outdoors, away from the children and the kitchen, to prevent any accidental inhalation or splashing. I was especially cautious to avoid contact with my skin or eyes. Any leftover mixture was properly disposed of, adhering to municipal waste guidelines, and all equipment was cleaned and dried immediately after use.

### B. Application

At 08:40, I began spraying the affected plants, starting with the most heavily infested stems. I focused on the undersides of the leaves, where aphids congregate, but made sure to avoid the flower buds to prevent unsightly oil spots. For the roses without visible aphids, I limited my application to the lower leaves as an extra precaution.

Once finished, I safely disposed of my gloves and gave the sprayer a thorough rinse outdoors. The entire process went smoothly, with no accidents or spills.

## IV. Introducing Marigold as a Companion Plant: Rationale and Plans

After discussing with Mr. Ramesh, our experienced garden supervisor, I decided to interplant marigolds (Tagetes spp.) among the roses as a further form of preventive pest management.

### Scientific Rationale

Marigolds are more than just cheerful additions to the flower beds. Their roots and leaves produce thiophenes—natural compounds known to repel many insect pests, including aphids. Over the years, multiple studies have shown that marigolds not only deter pests through chemical signals but also attract beneficial insects like ladybirds and lacewings, which prey on aphids.

Research by Staley et al. highlights the role of companion planting in biological control, including significant reductions in aphid levels [2]. Kumar & Singh’s study from the Indian context confirms the practical benefits of Tagetes as a pest deterrent around susceptible crops [3]. The distinctive scent of marigolds can also help mask the roses from aphid detection, adding another layer of defence.

### Implementation

Today, I began sourcing healthy marigold seedlings from the local nursery, planning to interplant them along the rose borders within the week. The aim is to establish these companions before the aphid season intensifies in March. I look forward to observing the changes this integration will bring—both to pest dynamics and the overall vibrancy of the beds.

## V. Progress Review and Reflections

### Immediate Outcomes

After carrying out the neem oil spray, I’m relieved that the aphid infestation remains localized and minor; no major damage so far, and the roses continue to show healthy new growth. I meticulously followed safety procedures, and all steps were executed without incident.

With marigold companion planting set in motion, I now have both an immediate intervention and a sustainable, longer-term cultural control strategy in place. Early detection has made all the difference—the infestation is not widespread, and with vigilant monitoring, I expect the issue to remain manageable.

### Assessment vs. Expectations

This morning's findings align closely with my initial hopes: spotting a nascent aphid problem, responding in a timely and measured fashion, and relying on organic measures. The situation has not escalated beyond the affected stems.

### Key Lessons

Regular, early-morning inspections of the garden are proving invaluable, not just for catching pests early but for fostering a closer connection to the changing needs of each plant. Combining organic sprays with companion planting strengthens the natural resilience of the garden. Most importantly, adherence to chemical safety standards is non-negotiable, ensuring the well-being of the household, the plants, and the local environment.
