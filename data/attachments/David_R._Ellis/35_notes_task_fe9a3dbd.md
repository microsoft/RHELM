## Overview

This report presents the plan and structure I created for a family-focused meal prep session with Monica, David, and Jasper. The goal for this session was to prepare a set of healthy, affordable meals together, and to organize ourselves efficiently while learning some new skills and enjoying the process as a group. The plan includes carefully chosen recipes that stretch our budget without sacrificing nutrition, clear division of responsibilities tailored to our individual strengths, and simple but effective strategies for food storage. I’ve also added reflections and educational notes, since these ideas can easily extend to workshops on budgeting or time management. As I designed this plan, I relied on guidance from USDA, EatRight.org, and the NHS nutrition and meal planning resources [1][2][3].

## 1. Meal Prep Plan

It always helps us to map out each dish in advance, listing the main ingredients and the exact steps. That way, everyone knows what we’re making, how to get started, and what’s coming up next.

| Dish                       | Key Ingredients                                              | Preparation Steps                                                                                                                                           |
|----------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Lentil Vegetable Soup**  | Dried lentils, carrots, celery, onion, canned diced tomatoes, garlic, olive oil, vegetable broth | 1. Rinse the lentils and chop all the vegetables.<br>2. In a large pot, sauté the onion, garlic, carrots, and celery in olive oil for about 5 minutes.<br>3. Stir in the lentils, diced tomatoes (with their juices), and broth.<br>4. Simmer for 30-35 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the lentils are soft.<br>5. Season to taste, let the soup cool, then portion for storage. |
| **Mason Jar Salads**       | Mixed greens, canned chickpeas, cherry tomatoes, cucumber, shredded carrots, whole-grain pasta (optional), vinaigrette | 1. Prepare whole-grain pasta if using, and let it cool.<br>2. In wide jars, layer ingredients in this order: vinaigrette (bottom), chickpeas and pasta, chopped veggies, then greens on top.<br>3. Seal tightly and refrigerate. |
| **Baked Chicken & Sweet Potato** | Boneless, skinless chicken breasts, sweet potatoes, broccoli, olive oil, herbs, salt, pepper | 1. Peel and cube the sweet potatoes, cut broccoli into florets.<br>2. On a sheet pan, toss sweet potatoes, broccoli, and chicken with olive oil and seasonings.<br>3. Bake at 400°F for 25-30 minutes, turning once.<br>4. Separate into portions once cooled. |

These dishes were chosen because they’re budget-friendly, balanced, and they hold up well in the fridge—making weekday lunches and suppers so much easier.

## 2. Our Roles: Who Does What?

To make things smooth (and keep everyone engaged), we divided up tasks in advance based on what each person enjoys or wanted to practice. Here’s how we split up the work, with an eye on skill-building and making sure everyone contributes safely.

| Task                          | Monica                | David                         | Jasper                                     | Skill Highlights                                  |
|-------------------------------|-----------------------|-------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|
| Grocery Prep & Organization   | Lead: Unpack and wash all produce | Assist: Gather pantry goods, check for missing ingredients | Shadow: Find items in bags, help rinse and wash vegetables | Organization, learning kitchen safety             |
| Chopping & Slicing            | Supervise knife use, chop onion and celery | Peel and cube sweet potatoes, slice carrots, cut broccoli | Tear washed greens by hand, measure pasta, rinse chickpeas | Safe knife handling, measuring, veggie prep        |
| Cooking & Sautéing            | Oversee stovetop soup, check chicken | Cook soup and chicken, watch oven | Stir soup (with help), observe cooking process | Safe use of heat, teamwork, basic cooking skills  |
| Salad Jar Assembly            | Double-check layers for freshness | Build up the jars—put dressing, beans, and pasta in first | Add veggies and greens to finish, seal jars | Assembly-line teamwork, food freshness, fine motor |
| Clean-Up & Storage            | Organize fridge, label containers | Wash pots and large dishes | Wipe counters, tidy up spills                | Good storage habits, shared cleanup               |

I found that when we match roles to our abilities and interests, everything goes more smoothly and we all get to learn or practice new things. Monica and David handled most chopping and heat tasks for safety, while Jasper enjoyed “helping” and learning by shadowing and doing small, safe tasks.

To keep things fresh, we rotate who does each job every week, so eventually everyone gets comfortable with the full routine.

## 3. Storing Our Meals & Keeping Track

Meal prep is only as good as how well you store the results! Here’s how we portioned and kept track of what we made, so nothing goes to waste and everyone knows what’s available for quick meals.

| Dish                          | Portion Size                     | Shelf Life (per USDA FoodKeeper)                    | Storage & Cost-Saving Tips                                                      |
|-------------------------------|----------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Lentil Vegetable Soup         | About 2 cups per container       | Fridge: 3–4 days<br>Freeze: Up to 3 months          | Cool completely, store in reusable/freezer-safe containers, and label with date. |
| Mason Jar Salads              | 1 quart/litre jar (meal size)    | Fridge: up to 4 days (dressing on bottom)           | Use wide-mouth jars for easy filling. Always keep dressing on the bottom, greens on top for freshness. |
| Baked Chicken & Sweet Potato  | 1 meal: 1 chicken breast, ½ sweet potato, 1 cup broccoli | Fridge: 3–4 days <br>(store chicken/veg separately if needed) | Portion immediately, use airtight containers, and keep proteins and veggies separate for best quality. |

**Extra Storage Strategies:**  
- Place oldest containers at the front of the fridge (so we use them first).
- Freeze leftovers in single-serve portions for grab-and-go lunches.
- Always label with date and contents (sometimes we get creative and add stickers, too).

We’ve learned these simple habits save us real money—less food forgotten at the back of the fridge, and fewer last-minute takeout runs when we’re tired.

## 4. Reflections, Tips, and Takeaways

### Family Reflection

After each session, we do a quick check-in:

- **What worked well?** Our salad jar assembly line was a big hit—everyone stayed busy and it felt oddly satisfying to see the fridge lined with colorful jars.
- **What took more time than expected?** Chopping veggies slowed us down—next time, I’d prep some ingredients the night before if possible.
- **How did everyone like the dishes?** David requested even more seasoning in the soup for next time, and Jasper wants to help measure out spices!
- **What would you tweak?** I’d have everyone taste and adjust seasoning together near the end, so we’re all happy with the flavors.

### Ingredient Swaps & Budget Tips

- Dried beans are cheapest, but canned are a big time-saver when on sale—we just rinse off the extra salt.
- When broccoli’s pricey, frozen peas or green beans work well in the chicken bake.
- Pasta can be swapped for bulk brown rice or barley (especially if we already have some on hand).
- For greens, we buy whatever’s freshest/on sale—sometimes a mix of baby kale and spinach lasts longer than spring mix.
- We throw veggie scraps (carrot tops, onion skins) into a freezer bag, and once the bag’s full, we simmer up homemade broth for soups.

### Making Meal Prep Fun & Communicative

- We start each session together, reviewing who does what, and set a timer for fun challenges (“Can we chop everything in 10 minutes?”).
- Music helps—everyone cleans faster to their favorite playlist.
- Rotating roles keeps it from getting boring, and lets us each learn new skills.
- For tricky jobs, we always pair up an experienced person with someone learning.
- Occasionally, we let each person “claim” their own meal with a color sticker—Jasper especially loves picking colors.

### Educational Extensions

When we use this plan in a workshop, or as a family learning tool, it’s helpful to:

- Figure out the cost per meal: just divide the grocery bill by total number of servings.
- Calculate our “meal prep wage”: compare the time and cost of homemade meals vs. takeout—amazing how much we save!
- For the kids, we sometimes guess, then weigh out how much food we prepped; it’s a real eye-opener about portion sizes and waste.

## 5. Making This Plan Work for Others

Everything here is easily adapted for small groups, workshops, or larger families:

- The tables work printed or on a tablet for reference while prepping.
- The task matrix shows a simple way to assign roles in any collaborative project—cooking or otherwise.
- Including reflection questions encourages everyone (kids and adults!) to think about what went well and what changes could make next time even better.
- The storage and substitution tips double as lessons on nutrition and smart shopping—no expert needed.
- Sharing this plan has made it easier for new participants in workshops to jump in, ask questions, and leave with practical ideas they can use at home.
