## Reflections on Food Spoilage and Budget Impact

### What Happened and Why

Looking over our kitchen this week, I couldn’t ignore the reality: we lost food, money, and nutrition to spoilage. It was frustrating to toss out fresh romaine, strawberries, and a nearly untouched tub of Greek yogurt. These moments always feel like more than just wasted dollars—they’re reminders that our planning fell short somewhere.

A few things stood out:

- **Buying Perishables Without a Clear Plan:** We grabbed strawberries and lettuce earlier in the week, quickly, thinking we’d work them into salads or snacks. Instead, our week got hectic, dinners at home got skipped, and those items sat untouched.
- **Improper Storage Mistakes:** The Greek yogurt, sitting at the back of the fridge where temperature tends to fluctuate, spoiled faster than expected. We realized that our refrigerator isn’t organized for easy visual inventory, and items sometimes get lost or pushed aside.
- **Last-Minute Schedule Changes:** Both Monica and I ended up working late and choosing to eat out on nights we didn’t anticipate. Our intentions for home-cooked meals didn’t match our actual week.

### What It Cost Us

We tallied up the direct expense for these spoiled items:

- Romaine Lettuce: $3.49
- Strawberries: $4.99
- Greek Yogurt: $5.99  
**Total Lost to Spoilage This Week: $14.47**

But the impact isn’t just about grocery receipts. Those missing ingredients could have been part of at least three homemade meals, which would easily rival or beat the $72 we spent dining out (estimating $12 per person, six meals total). We’re also missing out on the health benefits fresh fruits and greens offer, and that’s harder to put a price on.

### Why It Matters

After talking it through together, Monica said she felt really frustrated—those wasted groceries weren’t just a hit to the wallet, but also to our efforts to eat better and make healthy habits stick. I felt the same. It’s easy to buy lots of produce with good intentions, but unpredictable workweeks and variable Nashville produce quality make it tough to find the right balance. We’re trying to prioritize health, but our schedule doesn’t always cooperate.

## What We’re Committing To Next Week

After our review (which admittedly got a bit passionate), we set down a few concrete action steps to get our household grocery habits on track:

### 1. **Weekly Meal Prep Planning**
We agreed to carve out 15–20 minutes every Sunday for a meal plan session. Mapping out dinners, lunches, and snack options before leaving for the store should help us avoid those “I’ll just grab extra… just in case” moments that lead to waste. We’re pulling inspiration from the NEFE’s “Smart Shopping” initiative, which emphasizes planning purchases with clear intention [3].

### 2. **FIFO Organization**
Inspired by the “First In, First Out” (FIFO) method recommended by the USDA [1], we’re reorganizing the fridge, so older items are front and center. Monica found a set of clear bins that make it easier to track what needs to be eaten soon.

### 3. **Shared Digital Grocery List**
We’ve started using Google Keep to share and update grocery lists in real time. This keeps both of us in the loop and means fewer duplicate purchases (no more coming home to find we’ve both bought eggs). This strategy is encouraged by financial education programs, which note that shared responsibility and communication help prevent waste [4].

### 4. **Midweek Inventory Checks**
A five-minute review every Wednesday night is now on our shared calendar. It’s a quick glance to see what’s left, what needs to be eaten soon, and whether we need to adjust our meal plan. It’s a small habit that can make a big difference over time.

### 5. **Buy Produce More Often, in Smaller Amounts**
We’re shifting away from big, once-a-week fruit and veggie hauls. Instead, we’ll try picking up fresh produce twice a week, reducing the chance that anything spoils before we get to it.

This week reinforced how much visible planning and honest communication matter—not just for our budget, but for the health and wellbeing of our household. If we stick to these changes, we’ll save money, spend less time worrying about spoiled food, and get more fresh, healthy meals on the table. We’re looking forward to seeing how these new habits help us keep our groceries—and our goals—on track.
