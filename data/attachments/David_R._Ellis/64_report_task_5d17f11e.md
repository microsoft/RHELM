## Workshop Objectives

This interactive workshop is designed to give middle school students a foundational understanding of budgeting concepts and practical skills for managing money. By the end of the session, students will:

- Clearly define essential budgeting terms such as income, expenses, saving, and the difference between wants and needs.
- Apply an age-appropriate budgeting framework to track simulated financial activities.
- Collaborate in small groups to set budget priorities through discussion and consensus-building.
- Engage in a hands-on Lemonade Stand simulation to make, record, and debate financial choices.
- Demonstrate their knowledge through group presentations and reflective feedback.

**Measurable Outcomes:**

1. Completion of a budgeting worksheet with at least 80% accuracy.
2. Active participation and consensus in a group budgeting exercise.
3. Presentation of a balanced, group-developed budget.
4. Thoughtful reflection on budgeting decisions in a post-workshop feedback form.

## Activity Descriptions

### Welcome & Icebreaker

The facilitator opens the workshop by reviewing group norms and setting a friendly, inclusive atmosphere. Students are encouraged to share a memorable experience, habit, or story about money—maybe an allowance, a savings goal, or a small purchase they’re proud of. This sharing helps everyone feel seen and builds connection within the group.

### Essential Budgeting Concepts

A brief, lively presentation introduces students to the basics: income (money coming in), expenses (money going out), savings (setting aside funds for the future), and distinguishing wants from needs. Using relatable, real-life examples, like deciding between buying ice cream or saving for a new book, engages students and grounds discussion in everyday choices.

After each concept, students participate in interactive scenarios. For instance, when deciding if a new video game is a ‘need’ or a ‘want,’ students explain their reasoning and mark off the term on their worksheets to reinforce learning.

### Lemonade Stand Simulation Introduction

Next, the facilitator introduces Jasper’s ‘Lemonade Stand,’ a visual scenario featuring a simple lemonade stand and prompts for financial choices—startup expenses, anticipated income, and unexpected costs. Working in small groups, students analyze the illustration, debate how they’d allocate startup funds, and discuss strategies for covering sudden expenses. Simulation cards present realistic challenges, prompting students to weigh and reconsider their group’s priorities.

These contextual activities take inspiration from research-backed approaches, as outlined by organizations like the Jump$tart Coalition and NEFE, which highlight the effectiveness of hands-on financial simulations for developing real-world skills ([1],[2]).

### Group Activity: Building Your Budget

Each group receives a set of scenario cards and a budgeting worksheet. They work together to build a workable budget for their lemonade stand, discussing and debating spending, saving, and pricing. The task requires consensus: teams must agree on each line item and be prepared to defend their choices.

The facilitator circulates, listening in, asking probing questions, and encouraging quieter students to share ideas. When teams face conflicting opinions—for example, how much to invest in marketing versus ingredients—they must negotiate, compromise, and decide together. This process surfaces the challenges of budgeting and develops teamwork skills.

Random income and expense cards add unpredictability and realism, requiring quick adjustments and illustrating the need for flexibility in financial planning.

### Progress Review & Group Reflection

Groups pause mid-workshop to complete progress checklists, marking which budget milestones they’ve met and identifying any stumbling blocks. The facilitator leads a discussion in which groups share successful strategies and challenges—what worked, and what would they try differently next time?

These conversations reinforce reflective thinking and help students connect budgeting decisions to real-life outcomes.

### Group Budget Presentations

Groups take turns presenting their finished budgets to the class. Each team explains their key decisions, balancing needs and wants, and outlines how they agreed (or disagreed) on major choices. Presentations are assessed using a structured rubric focused on clarity, consensus, accuracy, and justification of choices.

After each presentation, both peer and facilitator feedback highlights strengths and offers suggestions for improvement, reinforcing the value of collaboration and collective reasoning.

### Debrief & Reflective Self-Assessment

To close the workshop, students fill out a feedback form, summarizing what they learned, describing challenges their group faced, and reflecting on how disagreements were resolved. The facilitator reviews completed rubric sheets, giving personalized feedback on individual and group performances.

Combining formative feedback (during group work) and summative assessment (through presentations and checklists) supports students’ growth and encourages them to take ownership of their learning.

### Closing & Q&A

The facilitator wraps up by reviewing key takeaways and answering final questions. Additional resources for continued learning, such as links to the Council for Economic Education, are distributed to empower students and their families to apply budgeting skills beyond the classroom.

## Assessment Methods

### Formative Assessment

- Facilitator records observational notes on group participation and collaborative problem-solving.
- Students and teams use progress review checklists to monitor their own learning.
- An exit ticket—“Name one budgeting tip you’ll use at home”—encourages individual reflection and practical application.

### Summative Assessment

- Presentations are graded with a clear rubric covering accuracy, clarity, group consensus, and reasoning.
    - **Accuracy**: Budget totals are realistic and mathematically correct.
    - **Clarity**: Decisions are clearly communicated by each team.
    - **Consensus**: Evidence that all group members contributed and agreed on outcomes.
    - **Justification**: Teams provide logical reasons for each budget decision.

**Rubric Sample:**

| Criteria       | Excellent (3)        | Satisfactory (2)   | Needs Improvement (1) |
|----------------|---------------------|--------------------|-----------------------|
| Accuracy       | All calculations accurate | Minor errors       | Major errors identified |
| Clarity        | Decisions explained clearly | Some confusion     | Explanation unclear     |
| Consensus      | Full group agreement       | Partial agreement  | No clear consensus     |
| Justification  | Thorough reasoning        | Partial reasoning  | Little or no reasoning |

Students also complete a reflective feedback form, describing their learning journey and sharing how their group worked through budgeting disagreements.

## Best Practices for Facilitating Interactive Group Workshops

- Start by building rapport and setting a supportive, respectful atmosphere.
- Use real-world, scenario-based simulations for maximum engagement ([1],[2]).
- Scaffold vocabulary and concepts so all students, regardless of background, can participate fully.
- Facilitate group discussion and debate, guiding teams to consensus while encouraging respectful disagreement and compromise.
- Commit to ongoing, multifaceted assessment, enabling students to receive actionable, timely feedback ([2],[3]).
- Integrate opportunities for self-reflection and peer feedback throughout the workshop, promoting a growth mindset and self-directed learning.
