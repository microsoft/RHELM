# Mentorship Call with Dr. Samuel Greene  
**Date:** April 19, 2024

---

## Overview

On April 19, I joined a mentorship call with Dr. Samuel Greene to discuss strategies for advancing financial literacy advocacy in Nashville, TN. Our conversation centered on practical, research-driven approaches to improving educational policy, engaging parents, and empowering students throughout the city’s K-12 schools. Our shared goal: to close the financial literacy gap and foster a model of sustained success that is both replicable and equitable.

We focused on four core objectives:
- Raising financial literacy levels among students using proven pedagogical methods.
- Actively involving parents and guardians in financial education, using family engagement as a driving force.
- Equipping students with real-world budgeting skills and the confidence to make informed financial decisions.
- Establishing collaborative partnerships and policy recommendations to support long-term community impact.

The vision is a Nashville where every student—regardless of background—builds a strong financial foundation, supported by their families, educators, and wider community.

---

## Key Focus Areas and Discussion Highlights

### Evidence-Based Strategies for Teaching Financial Literacy

Dr. Greene emphasized the importance of aligning curriculum with recognized national standards, such as those from Jump$tart and the Council for Economic Education. These frameworks prioritize core topics including budgeting, saving, credit management, and investing. We discussed ways to breathe life into these concepts—moving beyond textbooks by integrating hands-on activities, student-led financial projects, and interactive workshops for parents. Technology is a vital tool here: apps, games, and simulations actively engage students and can foster parent-student collaboration at home. For example, using budgeting games in class and then extending that learning through family challenges makes financial education both relevant and fun.

### Best Practices in Budgeting Education

To meet Tennessee's state requirements and ensure the curriculum is both skills-focused and accessible, we explored several action steps:
- Launching a parent-student budgeting challenge, piloting the idea with a group of at least 20 families. This fosters open, transparent discussions about household finances and co-designs actionable budgeting plans.
- Developing curriculum modules that reflect the diversity of Nashville’s student population. Providing materials in multiple languages and incorporating cultural relevance ensures that all families have equal opportunity to participate and benefit.
- Anchoring learning in real-world scenarios and problem-solving, which increases student engagement and retains long-term knowledge.

### Policy Advocacy and Professional Development

Dr. Greene stressed the necessity of systemic change through advocacy. Key actions include:
- Pushing for dedicated funding and district-wide policy mandates for comprehensive financial literacy education in all Nashville public schools.
- Supporting ongoing professional development so teachers remain confident and well-versed in financial literacy instruction—this is critical for program quality and sustainability.
- Implementing ongoing program assessment—regular data tracking and outcome reporting enable continuous improvement and accountability.

### Building Community Partnerships

Progress depends on collaboration. We outlined approaches to deepen partnerships with organizations such as the Nashville Public Education Foundation, local banks, and non-profits. Engaging student advisory groups, PTAs, and policymakers throughout the design and implementation process ensures everyone’s voice is included. Community events, like an annual “Family Finance Night,” can elevate collective awareness and encourage more families to participate. These events create opportunities for stakeholders to share resources, celebrate successes, and identify new needs.

## Next Steps

Moving forward, the following actions are planned for the coming months:

1. **Pilot Program Launch and Implementation**
   - Finalize and distribute budgeting challenge materials; begin family recruitment during April and May.
   - Align introductory parent/educator/student workshops to district calendar events.
   
2. **Broader Stakeholder Engagement**
   - Host roundtables with policymakers, school leaders, and community representatives in May and June to coordinate efforts.
   - Create accessible feedback channels, such as short surveys and regular check-ins for participating families and educators.

3. **Ongoing Evaluation and Program Improvement**
   - Conduct baseline and post-program surveys to measure progress in financial literacy, engagement, and confidence (June–August).
   - Present interim results to all stakeholders, using insights to refine the initiative as needed.

4. **Continuous Communication and Advocacy**
   - Share updates, stories, and impact data via newsletters and public events, promoting transparency and encouraging broader support.
   - Work closely with local partners to advocate for sustained funding and further policy advancements.

**Timelines:**
- Preparation and stakeholder onboarding: April–May 2024
- Program launch: June 2024
- Initial evaluation and reporting: August 2024

**Evaluation Strategies:**
- Track improvements in financial literacy competencies using pre- and post-surveys.
- Monitor attendance and participation rates at workshops and events.
- Gather qualitative feedback from families and educators to identify strengths and areas for growth.

## Reflection

This mentorship call exemplified the power of a collaborative, research-backed approach to closing the financial literacy gap in Nashville. By integrating inclusive curriculum design, authentic family engagement, and consistent stakeholder collaboration, the initiatives set forth aim to elevate both student and parental confidence in navigating real-world financial challenges. The aspiration is not only improved financial competency among students but also a durable, city-wide model for equitable economic and educational outcomes.

Programs like the parent-student budgeting challenge tap directly into family dynamics and encourage open conversations about money, bridging generational gaps. Sustained professional learning for educators ensures that instruction remains relevant and impactful. As these efforts take shape, continued evaluation and adaptation will be key, driven by candid feedback and transparent data sharing. Nashville stands to become a leader in financial literacy advocacy, with ripple effects reaching families and communities well beyond the classroom.
