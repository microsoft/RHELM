Meeting Purpose & Objectives

Our meeting focused on developing dynamic and effective financial literacy workshops for middle school students. We set out to evaluate instructional strategies that foster strong engagement, with particular attention to interactive learning methods. As a core project component, we reviewed the concept and logistics of an interactive budgeting game. Finally, we outlined steps toward establishing a practical, research-driven plan for the initial rollout and ongoing development of the workshops.

Discussion Highlights

Workshop Structure and Curriculum Design

We agreed the curriculum should be organized into discrete modules—each focusing on a key aspect of personal finance appropriate for grades 6–8. The proposed modules include topics such as budgeting, saving, distinguishing needs from wants, and basic entrepreneurship. Each module will incorporate real-world case studies and financial scenarios tailored for this age group. For example, case studies might present choices about how to allocate an allowance, or simulate saving for a personal goal.

To enhance student ownership and relatability, we discussed integrating peer-led sessions. Older students or recent alumni would take part in facilitating specific activities, serving as role models and making the learning experience more engaging. This peer modeling is supported by research indicating that students are more receptive to their peers when learning life skills.

We also examined strategies for involving families in financial learning. Activities such as take-home budgeting challenges or interactive tasks on digital platforms can extend learning beyond the classroom. Students and their families could explore spending decisions together and share reflections, reinforcing core concepts and supporting ongoing learning at home.

Engagement and Instructional Strategies

To build a sense of excitement and motivation, we outlined several teaching methods:

  • Gamified Activities: Students accumulate points or rewards as they participate, with progress visibly tracked. This approach can significantly boost sustained engagement, as students enjoy friendly competition and tangible recognition.
  • Scenario-Based Role Play: Assigning students roles (such as income earner, spender, saver, or entrepreneur) allows them to manage fictional finances, face decision-making dilemmas, and see consequences unfold in real time.
  • Collaborative Group Work: Working in small groups, students tackle financial challenges—such as constructing a simple household budget or solving unexpected expense scenarios. These group activities are designed to enhance teamwork, communication, and critical thinking.
  • “Real World” Simulation Days: At the end of each workshop cycle, students participate in a hands-on simulation—managing transactions such as shopping for groceries, receiving a salary, and paying bills. This cumulative exercise solidifies concepts and provides an authentic, memorable learning experience.

Interactive Budgeting Game: Review and Implementation

An interactive budgeting game emerged as a central pedagogical tool. David R. Ellis emphasized that “students internalize financial habits best when they experience consequences and rewards in a safe, simulated environment.” The game fosters critical thinking, problem-solving, and supports active learning—adaptable for various skill levels through adjustable difficulty settings.

We discussed practical requirements for successful implementation: - The game must be available in digital format but also adaptable for paper-based play, ensuring accessibility for schools with different resources. - Effective teacher facilitation is essential; we need to provide professional development materials so educators understand both the technical and instructional aspects of the game. - Embedding formative assessment features within the game interface will help monitor student comprehension and guide differentiated instruction in real time. - For added impact, we’ll explore parental access to game elements, encouraging family reinforcement of financial literacy skills.

Dr. Samuel Greene concluded, “A budgeting game is central to student engagement. We should prioritize making it flexible, accessible, and relevant to students’ lived experiences.” We reached full consensus on integrating the game as the program’s cornerstone.

Consultation, Feedback, and Evidence Base

We agreed on the importance of gathering frequent, structured feedback from both teachers and students throughout pilot testing. This iterative approach will guide curriculum refinement and ensure practical relevance. Additionally, we intend to compile recent academic research on game-based learning and financial literacy and maintain a centralized resource bank for all stakeholders. Our commitment to evidence-based practice and open collaboration will underpin every stage of workshop development, with consensus-building before any district-wide implementation.
