# Field Report: My Vegetarian Meal and Hydration Routines for June 10th Desert Fieldwork

## Overview

This report documents my routine for meals and hydration during a full day of desert conservation fieldwork on June 10th. After a recent shift to vegetarian food choices—driven by both ecological concerns and a commitment to sustainable living—I wanted to optimize my nutrition and hydration for long hours in the heat. My approach emphasizes simple, field-ready meals made from locally sourced and minimally packaged, plant-based ingredients. I’ve also begun experimenting with cold herbal teas for hydration, hoping to boost my comfort and mental performance under harsh field conditions. By sharing my daily methods and observations, I aim to help other conservation teams adapt sustainable and effective strategies for desert work.

---

## 1. Meals: Breakfast, Lunch, and Dinner in the Field

Each meal was planned to be nutritious, easy to pack, low in waste, and suited for desert logistics. Here’s how I handled my food for the day:

### Breakfast

For breakfast, I stuck with what’s become a reliable foundation for me in the field:

- **Ingredients:** Rolled oats (organic, bought in bulk locally), chia seeds (from the local health store’s bulk section), dried apricots (from the nearby farm), almond butter (from the co-op, in a glass jar), and unsweetened soy milk (carton, minimal packaging, local brand).
- **Portion:** I mix 60g oats, 15g chia seeds, and 30g dried apricots in a reusable bowl. I add 250ml soy milk to hydrate it, then top with 20g almond butter. The proportions keep my pack light but my calorie intake solid.
- **Prep:** I usually prep the mix right at camp—not much fuss. When I want to save fuel, I soak the oats overnight in cool water for a no-cook breakfast. Sometimes I swap apricots for dates, which are even more drought-friendly and locally available.
- **Nutrition:** This bowl gives lasting energy through complex carbs, omega-3 fats, plant protein, and plenty of fiber. It’s hydrating, too, with the soy milk base. I find it keeps me steady through the early hours before the sun ramps up.
- **Ecological Notes:** Keeping it vegetarian means a lighter food footprint, especially with local sourcing and bulk packaging. Almonds and oats work well because they’re available regionally and require less water than animal products.

### Lunch

Lunch is a key refueling moment in my workday, and I focus on freshness and hydration:

- **Ingredients:** Whole wheat tortillas (from a local bakery, with minimal packaging), homemade hummus (using bulk chickpeas, local olive oil), sliced cucumber and bell pepper (from desert-adapted farms), sunflower seeds (bulk, local), and a handful of leafy greens—usually spinach or arugula.
- **Portion:** I wrap up two tortillas (50g each) with about 60g hummus, 80g sliced veggies, 15g sunflower seeds, and 30g greens. I adapt the volumes based on field conditions—if the heat’s intense, I up the veggies for added hydration.
- **Prep:** I assemble the wrap at camp or right on site, packing pre-sliced veggies to save time. The hummus can be a dried mix I rehydrate if there’s a water constraint. Sunflower seeds give crunch and extra nutrition.
- **Nutrition:** This lunch balances carbs, protein, and healthy fats, plus magnesium, vitamin C, and potassium. The high water content of the veggies is a subtle but noticeable help with hydration. I feel clearer-headed and less sluggish in the midday sun after this meal.
- **Ecological Notes:** Legumes and seeds are low-impact compared to animal protein. I prioritize bulk purchases and reusable containers, avoiding single-use plastics. If permitted, I sometimes forage wild edible greens, which enrich both flavor and sustainability.

### Dinner

Dinner needs to be restorative and simple, especially after a long day traversing desert terrain:

- **Ingredients:** Whole wheat couscous (locally supplied), quick-cooking lentils (pre-cooked when time is short), freeze-dried peas or carrots (for minimal weight and packaging), olive oil (from a local press), za’atar spice blend (bulk, from a traditional market), and a wedge of local lemon.
- **Portion:** I prepare 70g couscous, 50g lentils, 40g freeze-dried veggies, 15ml olive oil, 5g za’atar, and a lemon wedge for a recovery-focused dinner. These proportions are enough to replenish my glycogen stores and keep hunger at bay.
- **Prep:** At camp, I combine everything in a single pot, pour in boiled or solar-heated water, and let it sit covered for about 10 minutes. I finish it with olive oil, za’atar, and lemon for both flavor and morale. Using a solar water heater or insulated flask helps cut down fuel use, which feels good in terms of impact.
- **Nutrition:** This combination offers complex carbs for endurance, lentils for protein and iron, and veggies for fiber and vitamins. Olive oil packs in extra calories, and za’atar adds both taste and minor bioactives. I’ve noticed dinner really helps me recharge physically and mentally before nightfall.
- **Ecological Notes:** Every ingredient is plant-based, chosen for low resource usage and local availability. Freeze-dried veggies keep the meal light for carrying but still offer solid nutritional value. I use reusable containers and pack out any waste for composting.

## 2. Hydration: Integrating Cold Herbal Tea

Staying hydrated in the desert heat is a relentless challenge, so I’ve recently started testing an herbal tea strategy instead of just plain water.

### My Hydration Protocol

- **Gear:** I use a 2L BPA-free hydration bladder (Platypus model, with an insulated sleeve) to keep drinks cool in my pack. A small cloth bag holds tea infusions, and I keep a reusable cup handy for breaks.
- **Prep:** Each evening, I steep peppermint tea overnight in cool water at camp, then fill my bladder with the cold tea in the morning—sometimes adding ice from our cooler when there’s enough. This holds the chill for several hours.
- **Adaptations for Desert:** Insulating the bladder slows warming in my pack. The cloth tea bags are lightweight and compostable, which fits my zero-waste approach.
- **Teas:** My standard is peppermint (for its cooling, alertness effects), but I rotate with hibiscus (for antioxidants and blood pressure support) and lemongrass (minor anti-inflammatory, mood lift). I source all in bulk from local botanicals—never sweetened or artificially flavored.
- **Hydration Practice:** I aim for 2L of tea during a half-day in high heat, sipping regularly according to thirst, sweat volume, and urine color. I refill around midday whenever possible.
- **Effects Observed:** Cold herbal tea brings a distinctly refreshing feeling, especially during the hottest stretches. I’ve found my perceived exertion drops after drinking—it’s like my mind resets and refocuses, particularly after long, rocky crossings. My urine stays pale and clear, and I haven’t experienced any dehydration symptoms. I also notice sharper cognitive performance and fewer memory lapses during species logging.
- **Environmental Routine:** All tea is packed in bulk cloth or reusable jars; hydration gear is cleaned daily with biodegradable soap. Plant-based tea waste goes into compost, and I generate zero landfill waste through this system.

## 3. My Personal Dietary Philosophy and Motivation

Switching to a vegetarian field diet has become much more than just a practical adjustment—it’s a commitment rooted in my experience, values, and the demands of desert conservation work.

- **Why Plant-Based:** My main motivation is ecological. Every meal I eat in the field is a small chance to respect and protect the ecosystems I work in. Prioritizing food with lower water and land use helps me align my personal choices with the region’s needs and my conservation goals.
- **Health and Performance:** I’ve read several studies and noticed firsthand that plant-based meals support better hydration, improved electrolyte management, and help me bounce back from oxidative stress after long days in the heat. My energy stays level, and I no longer suffer midday slumps.
- **Pack Logistics:** Plant meals are lighter to carry, leave less trash, and make rationing easier for multi-day fieldwork. It’s freeing to not worry about spoilage and bulky packaging.
- **Reflection:** I feel lighter physically and mentally after meals. Irritability fades in the heat, and I stay focused on data logging and transcription. Sometimes, I think of how desert plants survive by conserving water and thriving on minimal resources—it feels right to model my field diet on those same principles.

### Continual Adaptation

I’m always looking for ways to refine my routine. I want to explore more regionally specific crops (dates, native grains) to lower the ecological cost of food imports and better support local networks. If permitted by protocol, I plan to try more wild-foraged greens or sun-dried local rations. I’m also experimenting with powdered versions of plant milks to reduce packaging even further, and with solar-cooking to minimize fossil fuel use. Feedback from my team on ration effectiveness helps shape each revision.
