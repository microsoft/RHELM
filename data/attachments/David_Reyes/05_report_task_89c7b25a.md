## Location Overview

- **Site:** Ridgecrest Water Recharge Basin  
- **Coordinates:** 35.6412° N, 117.6784° W  
- **Elevation:** 703 meters (2,306 feet)  
- **County:** Kern  
- **Habitat Description:**  
  The Ridgecrest Water Recharge Basin is an engineered network of ponds designed for groundwater recharge, located within the heart of the Mojave Desert. This site features a mix of semi-permanent open water, sandy and alkaline flats, scattered patches of cattail and rush, and is surrounded by extensive creosote bush scrub typical of desert uplands.

- **Recent Management Activities:**  
    - Initiated managed flooding in December 2023 to replenish water levels following autumn drawdown.
    - Mechanically removed invasive Phragmites along the northwest margin in early January.
    - Installed new riprap on the north dam face for erosion control.
    - Ongoing water quality monitoring since December has detected minor turbidity increases, but no chemical treatments have been applied.

#### Detailed Account: Northern Shoveler (*Anas clypeata*)

- **Observed Count:** 38 birds (23 adult males, 13 adult females, 2 immatures)
- **Key Behaviors:**  
  The largest duck concentration during this survey. Flocks were highly organized, actively participating in synchronized spinning and straining behavior typical of filter-feeding in turbid conditions. Courtship was conspicuous, with multiple pairs engaged in lateral head-pumping, shallow diving, and wing flicking displays. At resource-rich inflow points, shovelers displaced Gadwall, but overall interspecific aggression was minimal. The observed male-to-female ratio (~2:1) aligns with prior regional wintering trends. Immature birds remained at pond margins and did not participate in displays.
- **Ecological Context:**  
  The notable shoveler presence corresponded directly to the recent rise in water levels and increased turbidity post-inflow, providing optimal conditions for this species’ unique feeding strategy.

## Field Notes: Highlights and Rarity Documentation

- **Spotted Sandpiper:**  
  A single adult verified in basic (non-breeding) plumage, observed persistently foraging along new mudflats. This species is seldom present in winter at this site, making the record notable. Supporting photo and annotations submitted for state-level rare bird review.

- **Eared Grebe:**  
  One adult sighted, diving alone with no associates. This constitutes a rare mid-winter record for the basin. Given the transient nature and infrequent detection, this may reflect both genuine rarity and sporadic use of the site as water conditions shift.

- **Green Heron:**  
  Presence in January is highly unusual; this species typically departs in late autumn. Today’s record possibly reflects the moderating influence of engineered, reliably unfrozen open water. Such records may be increasing as local winter water remains more consistently available.

- **Loggerhead Shrike:**  
  Now considered a regional species of conservation concern, the shrike was observed actively hunting from fencing and creating prey impalement caches (“larders”). Diminished local populations make each sighting at the basin noteworthy and valuable for monitoring.

- **Absence of Vagrants:**  
  Despite recent reports elsewhere in the region, no vagrant waterfowl (such as Eurasian Wigeon or Greater White-fronted Goose) were observed.

## Ecological Summary and Interpretation

This January survey at the Ridgecrest Water Recharge Basin highlights the powerful influence of hydrologic management on winter bird communities within a desert context. The prompt response of dabbling ducks—especially the substantial flock of Northern Shovelers—to increased water levels and turbidity underscores the basin’s value as a dynamic foraging site. The prevalence of courtship and pairing behaviors among numerous duck species further illustrates how quickly avian life capitalizes on new resource pulses following managed inflow.

The emergence of fresh mudflats around the rising water supported an array of shorebirds, including both expected and unusual winter records, such as Spotted Sandpiper. The detection of rare or otherwise seasonal visitors like Eared Grebe and Green Heron emphasizes the basin’s function as a regional wetland refuge—critical as natural wetland acreage diminishes and weather patterns fluctuate. The reappearance and documented predatory activity of Loggerhead Shrike, a species in regional decline, further reflect the basin’s ongoing conservation significance.

Continued monitoring at Ridgecrest, integrating systematic eBird-compliant checklists with detailed management records, is essential to guide adaptive stewardship. Data from such visits will clarify longer-term trends in species composition, rare bird occurrence, and phenological events across the Mojave bird community, informing both local conservation priorities and broader regional understanding of avian responses to engineered water resources.
