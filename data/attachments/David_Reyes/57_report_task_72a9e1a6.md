# Autumn Birding Workshop Outline & Volunteer Schedule  
## For Conservation Practitioners in the Mojave Desert Region  
**Event Date:** October 14–16, 2024  
**Location:** Mojave Desert Land Trust Field Station, Joshua Tree, CA  

---

## Executive Summary

The Autumn Birding Workshop is a three-day, interactive event dedicated to advancing bird conservation efforts across the Mojave Desert. By bringing together conservation practitioners, volunteers, and Indigenous Elders, the workshop emphasizes cutting-edge survey techniques, Indigenous (Cahuilla) ecological knowledge, and effective collaboration between community partners. 

Participants will deepen their skills in identifying both migratory and resident bird species, conducting habitat assessments, and entering data for citizen science platforms. These efforts directly align with ongoing initiatives led by the Mojave Desert Land Trust and Audubon California.

A key aspect of the workshop is the integration of Cahuilla perspectives, offering participants opportunities to learn from Elders whose oral traditions and place-based environmental knowledge enrich our collective understanding of desert ecosystems. Co-led sessions foster mutual learning, supporting more adaptive conservation strategies grounded in both science and Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK).

Highlighted activities include guided dawn field surveys, workshops co-facilitated by Cahuilla Elders and ornithologists, and the launch of a citizen science project focused on mapping key Mojave bird habitats. 

**Conservation Objectives:**
- Expand local data on both migratory and resident birds during the 2024 season, fulfilling state and federal monitoring priorities.
- Contribute to the Mojave Desert Land Trust’s main conservation goals—invasive species (like Sahara Mustard) control, Joshua Tree woodland preservation, and protection of critical migratory corridors.
- Develop and pilot frameworks for integrating TEK into ongoing bird habitat restoration projects.

---

## Workshop Objectives

### Learning Objectives
- Train participants in accurate identification of Mojave Desert avifauna through both visual and auditory cues, covering migrants, year-round residents, and species of special concern.
- Provide hands-on practice with rapid avian survey methodologies, electronic data reporting (using eBird and Calflora), and large-scale habitat evaluation.
- Deepen understanding of how bird presence, activity, and distribution signal broader ecosystem health, particularly in the context of ongoing climate change.

### Conservation & Stewardship Objectives
- Create opportunities for two-way learning with Cahuilla Elders focused on seasonal changes, source water protection, and birds that hold particular cultural significance.
- Promote models of cooperative stewardship that integrate scientific rigor with place-based Indigenous knowledge—emphasizing careful observation, phenology, and communal care for the land.
- Establish a pilot community science monitoring team from among workshop trainees, laying groundwork for year-round local bird monitoring.

## Cahuilla Elder Reference Notes  
### Insights Shared by Julia Banegas, Cahuilla Elder & Community Steward

- **Seasonal Awareness:**  
  “Our stories teach us that when certain birds arrive, the season is turning. Listening for the ‘téwet’ (cactus wren) or the ‘hísil’ (ash-throated flycatcher) teaches you about both the birds and the well-being of the desert itself.”

- **Connections to Water & Habitat:**  
  - Springs, seeps, and desert oases (“pawíka”) serve as gathering points—vital for wildlife and people. Their protection is essential for life to continue across generations.
  - Traditional knowledge identifies specific microhabitats—mule fat thickets, mesquite groves—that provide secure nesting or important stopover habitat during migration. These locations often align with biologists’ survey hotspots, reflecting the strength of TEK.

- **Traditional Stewardship Practices:**  
  - “We do not just observe, we listen and tend—clearing invasive weeds, leaving seed plants for quail.”  
  - The use of fire and selective clearing historically created a landscape mosaic supporting both food plants and bird nesting needs—a strategy now being reconsidered by land managers as part of habitat restoration.

- **Integration with Science:**  
  - Where possible, Cahuilla bird names are preserved in records alongside scientific names. This dual recognition enriches both community identity and scientific data.
  - “When we map birds today, we walk with scientists, pointing out what our grandparents told us—now we have the numbers to confirm the observations gathered over many generations.”

**Conclusion:**  
Cahuilla ecological traditions contribute crucial insights to timing, habitat selection, and restoration planning. Integrating this knowledge alongside scientific data strengthens conservation outcomes and fosters deeper community stewardship.

## Outreach Progress Update

- **Participant Engagement:**  
  Workshop registration reached 34 as of August 10, 2024, with attendees representing local communities, regional schools, and park service staff (target: 40). Social media promotion via Instagram and the Mojave Desert Land Trust website generated strong interest, with 3,800 individual accounts reached and 174 post shares. Bilingual flyers were distributed at local community centers and swap meets in Joshua Tree and Twentynine Palms, ensuring accessibility for Spanish-speaking residents.

- **Stakeholder Partnerships:**  
  Engagement with Cahuilla community councils and Mojave Desert Land Trust members has already led to five formal partnership offers to expand monitoring activities beyond the event itself. Feedback from partners and school groups underscores the importance of “learning our birds in our language” and calls for more transparent data-sharing.

- **Feedback, Challenges, and Adaptive Measures:**  
  Early registration was initially slow, likely due to a lack of bilingual outreach. Volunteers responded promptly by creating translated flyers and conducting in-person outreach. Participant feedback from previous years highlighted difficulties with overlapping fieldwork seasons. To address this, the current schedule now includes dawn start times and a mid-October window to maximize bird activity while avoiding peak desert heat. COVID-19 precautions are in place for all food stations and group activities, and the workshop footprint has been expanded to prioritize outdoor learning spaces.

- **Continuous Improvements:**  
  Following the workshop, there will be a virtual citizen science Q&A session supporting ongoing participant learning and bird monitoring efforts. Planning is underway to recruit more volunteers from local high school environmental clubs, increasing youth involvement and building local conservation capacity for the future.
