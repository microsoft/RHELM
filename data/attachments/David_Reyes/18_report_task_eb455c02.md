# Field Journal Log: Bird Survey at Joshua Flats, Indian Wells Valley

## Survey Overview

**Date:** 2024-02-24  
**Location:** Joshua Flats Trailhead, Indian Wells Valley, Kern County, California  
**Estimated GPS Coordinates:** 35.6125° N, 117.6452° W  
**Habitat Description:**  
Nestled in the western Mojave Desert, the Joshua Flats area is characterized by dense stands of Joshua tree (Yucca brevifolia), dotted with creosote bush (Larrea tridentata), saltbush (Atriplex sp.), and occasional Mojave yucca (Yucca schidigera). The terrain consists of sandy washes, scattered rocky outcrops, and ephemeral grass patches, creating a mosaic of shrubland and open desert. These diverse features provide critical habitat for a range of desert-adapted bird species.

**Observers:**  
- David Reyes (Principal Observer)  
- Emily Tran (Partner Observer)  

**Survey Time:** 06:15 AM – 1:30 PM  
**Weather:**  
Morning began calm, under gentle golden light. By midday, the sky remained clear and bright, with temperatures rising smoothly from 43°F at dawn to 68°F by early afternoon, as confirmed by historical National Weather Service data from Ridgecrest, CA. There was no precipitation and very little wind throughout the survey (under 5 mph), creating optimal conditions for auditory and visual detection.

## Methods

### Survey Route and Protocol  
Our survey route began at the Joshua Flats main trailhead (see GPS above), heading east for approximately 2.4 km. Along the way, we made several short excursions into adjacent washes, open desert flats, and a boulder field. The return followed the same primary path, ensuring we encountered microhabitats both outbound and inbound, which helped maximize species diversity detection.

We followed the Standardized Area Search protocol as outlined by California Partners in Flight for Mojave Desert bird monitoring. This included placing 10-minute stationary point counts at each significant habitat transition and maintaining continuous observation while traversing the main transect. When mixed flocks or noteworthy avian activity were encountered, time was extended at those locations to ensure thorough observation.

**Observation Methods:**  
- *Visual Detection:* Utilized Leica Trinovid 8x42 binoculars for scanning and a Canon EOS 90D paired with a 100-400mm lens for photographic documentation.
- *Auditory Detection:* Passive listening was employed, with careful notation of vocalizations. Playback was used sparingly and only for LeConte’s Thrasher, in accordance with California Department of Fish and Wildlife guidelines.
- *Data Recording:* Observations were logged in waterproof "Rite in the Rain" notebooks. To supplement and verify data, time-stamped entries and GPS waypoints were collected via the eBird mobile app operating offline. Audio recordings of uncertain calls were captured using a Tascam DR-05X recorder for later review.  
- *Adaptive Sampling:* When encountering mixed species flocks—particularly along wash edges—stationary observation periods were extended. Occasionally, we detoured into boulder outcrops in response to increased activity from Phainopepla or Black-throated Sparrows.

## Gear and Field Methods Log

**Optics and Cameras:**  
We relied primarily on a Canon EOS 90D (32.5MP, APS-C, weather-sealed) paired with a Canon EF 100-400mm f/4.5–5.6L IS II USM lens. Camera settings were generally set to Av mode at f/7.1, ISO 500-800, and a 1/2000s shutter to capture active birds. The lens hood was kept on throughout the day to reduce flare and guard against wind-blown dust.

A midday gust prompted a thorough lens check and cleaning, using a LensPen and rocket blower to remove sand accumulated during boulder field traverses. While minor grit was detected in the lens zoom action afterward, no immediate impairment resulted; a complete cleaning will be performed post-trip. A slightly loose rubber lens grip—worn from prior outings—remained functional.

Emily’s use of a monopod before sunrise added stability for low-light shots, and burst mode on the camera captured crucial moments, especially during the quail flush. When midday heat shimmer reduced sharpness, we shifted observation posts to shaded areas for clearer photos.

We also used Leica Trinovid 8x42 binoculars and a Tascam DR-05X audio recorder, particularly valuable for verifying tricky vocal identifications.

**Field Documentation:**  
All observations were logged in waterproof notebooks and backed up digitally with eBird app entries (offline mode, due to spotty service). GPS waypoints were marked at each significant observation site.

**Gear Recommendations:**  
Frequent brief cleaning intervals are essential in dusty conditions. Use of lens hood and routine checks throughout the day helped prevent both image degradation and damage to optics. Assigning explicit roles (one observer on photography, one on data entry during peak activity) improved documentation quality and thoroughness.

## Reflective Summary

Our time at Joshua Flats highlighted both the quiet drama and ecological subtlety of the Mojave’s winter avifauna. Dawn revealed LeConte’s Thrashers in silent washes—a species whose pale cryptic plumage and shy behavior made even a brief sighting especially rewarding. We watched, motionless, as the pair foraged along the sand, tails dipping, giving only a muted response to brief playback. This encounter set the tone for a morning where patience rather than pursuit yielded the best results.

The fruiting mistletoe near 07:16 created a focal point for activity, with Phainopepla drawing in, their vocalizations weaving through the air, joined soon after by mixed sparrow flocks. Even in winter, mistletoe’s bounty shaped the patterns of movement and interspecies interaction along the transect. Emily noted the subtle differences in male and female plumage, while I recalled previous Phainopepla observations from Joshua Tree National Park for reference.

Cactus Wrens, never ones for subtlety, presented an animated duet atop a cholla, their harsh calls and synchronized trills as lively as ever. Their performance prompted a brief reflection—if only all fieldwork partnerships showed the same coordination and spirit. Emily’s quail call mimicry wasn’t as successful in going unnoticed, unintentionally flushing the covey but yielding a burst of laughter and rapid-fire photographs.

Throughout the morning and into early afternoon, adaptation proved critical. We extended stationary counts at saline shrub edges when sparrow activity spiked, then used those pauses to hone in on group foraging behaviors—including detailed note-taking and photography as moments unfolded rapidly. The open flats, though lively in the morning, became quieter toward midday, reinforcing the value of time-partitioned study to capture full species activity.

**Ecological Patterns:**  
The bird community closely reflected the microhabitats encountered: LeConte’s Thrashers remained restricted to wide sandy washes, while sparrows flocked at shrubby saline edges. Rock Wrens kept to the boulder-strewn terrain, and raptors such as harrier and kestrel patrolled the more open, unbroken flats. Joshua tree clusters, particularly those interspersed with ephemeral grass or fruiting mistletoe, concentrated both noise and avifaunal diversity.

**Recommendations for Ongoing Monitoring:**  
- Continued use of multiple detection methods—visual, auditory, photographic, and app-based—is key for maximizing detection rates in this varied landscape.
- Deploying passive acoustic recorders overnight and at dawn, especially in sensitive or low-density habitats (e.g., for LeConte’s Thrasher), would enhance survey completeness.
- Structuring surveys with split morning and afternoon sessions would help document temporal changes in activity, particularly to fill observed gaps during midday quiet periods.
- Scheduling regular gear maintenance pauses protects equipment and ensures continued data quality, particularly under harsh, dusty desert conditions.
- Assigning data and photography roles dynamically, in response to activity surges, improved both the richness and reliability of our records.
