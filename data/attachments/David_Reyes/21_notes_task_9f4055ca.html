Daily Meal Plan for David Reyes, Research Ecologist

March 3, 2024

Overview

I'm David Reyes, and as an ecologist who leads a conservation project, my daily life revolves around balancing fieldwork demands with health, environmental sensitivity, and curiosity. I've put together this daily meal plan—not just as something to keep me fueled, but as a reflection of my commitment to wellness and to conservation science. Each meal centers local and sustainable ingredients, while also factoring in my sinus allergies and my interest in connecting culinary choices with the eco-systems I study. These tables and notes help me track, adapt, and share my routines with colleagues—feel free to suggest modifications.

Breakfast

Local Quinoa & Fruit Bowl

Time 7:30 AM (before heading out for field prep)
Meal Name Local Quinoa & Fruit Bowl
Ingredients - Quinoa (local, organic, low water use)
- Blueberries (seasonal, from nearby regenerative farm; high antioxidants)
- Diced apple (heirloom, from IPM farm)
- Pumpkin seeds (regional, dry-farmed)
- Almond milk (organic, water-efficient, sub oat milk if needed)
- Honey (raw, local wildflower apiary)
- Chia seeds (local when possible, fair trade)
Dietary Notes Sinus-friendly: No citrus, chili, or excessive spice. High in antioxidants for immune support.
Allergen caution: Check for nut tolerance, oat milk as alternative. Vegan option available.
Instructions Rinse quinoa and cook in water or unsweetened almond/oat milk until fluffy. Cool briefly and layer in a bowl with fruit and seeds. Finish with a drizzle of honey; serve chilled or warm.

Thoughts & Rationale

I start most mornings with a light, energizing bowl that’s gentle on my sinuses and packs in antioxidants to support my immune system, especially during allergy season. Quinoa is a mainstay—low water use and grown close to home, a crop that mirrors the drought tolerance I respect in our native flora. Blueberries and apples add fiber, while pumpkin seeds and chia round out the texture and provide lasting energy. Choosing honey from local wildflower hives feels right; it supports bee populations, so essential to our local biodiversity.

Why these ingredients?
My focus is on minimizing inflammation and maximizing slow-release energy without irritants like citrus or chili. Everything in the bowl is sourced with an eye toward conservation: integrated pest management for apples, dry farming for seeds, pollinator-friendly honey. This meal isn’t just food—it’s a small daily act of stewardship.

Lunch

Sinus-Friendly Vegetarian Stew

Time 12:30 PM (after morning fieldwork)
Meal Name Sinus-Friendly Vegetarian Stew (Reduced Chili)
Ingredients - Local potatoes (low-irrigation)
- Carrots (regional, organic)
- Zucchini (regenerative culture)
- Spinach (locally grown, pesticide-free)
- Lentils (organic, nitrogen-fixing)
- Onion (just a touch, sautéed)
- Bay leaf (native plantings)
- Cumin/coriander (small amount, sustainably sourced)
- Vegetable broth (homemade, low salt—no MSG)
- Olive oil (local, drought-tolerant grove)
- Sea salt (local, small-scale)
- Chili: omitted for sinus recovery
Dietary Notes Vegetarian and sinus-supportive: minimal irritants, rich in vitamin C and plant protein. Gluten-free.
Instructions Sauté onions in olive oil until translucent. Add diced carrots, potatoes, zucchini, and lentils. Stir in bay leaf, cumin, coriander, and broth. Simmer covered for 30–40 min until tender. Season to taste, toss with fresh spinach just before serving.

Thoughts & Rationale

By midday, after hours outside, I’m always craving something warm and nourishing. I keep my stew simple—lots of root vegetables and lentils for protein. Lentils are a staple; their soil-enriching qualities echo the restoration work we do in the field. The little bit of onion I use is sautéed until sweet, to soften any harshness. I keep the seasoning light and skip the chili—my sinuses thank me for it.

Conservation connections:
Every choice reflects the same principals I bring to my professional work: dry-farming methods, local sourcing, nitrogen-fixing crops (lentils, chickpeas) for soil health. Even the olive oil comes from groves where water conservation is a top priority. As I eat, I think about the journey these ingredients took to reach my table and the landscapes they support.

Dinner

Desert-Inspired Roasted Veg & Wild Grain Salad

Time 7:00 PM (after fieldwork and data synthesis)
Meal Name Roasted Veg & Wild Grain Salad
Ingredients - Wild rice (regional wetlands, sustainably harvested)
- Sweet potato (local, dry-farmed)
- Eggplant & bell peppers (low-input, regional)
- Chickpeas (organic, nitrogen-fixing)
- Parsley (native/water-wise herb)
- Lemon juice (minimal, based on sinus tolerance)
- Olive oil (as above)
- Pumpkin seeds (regional, dry-farmed)
- Sea salt/pepper (local)
Dietary Notes Sinus-friendly: minimal lemon. Gluten-free, vegan, high fiber and prebiotic content.
Instructions Cook wild rice and rinse. Roast sweet potatoes, eggplant, and bell peppers in olive oil at 400°F (200°C) until caramelized. Combine grains with roasted veg, chickpeas, parsley, pumpkin seeds, a drizzle of oil, and a squeeze of lemon (only if tolerated). Season and serve warm or chilled.

Thoughts & Rationale

Evenings are when I reflect. After documenting field data, a meal that honors the desert is grounding—wild rice from regional wetlands is particularly meaningful, as its harvest is intricately tied to wetland health and indigenous stewardship. I roast local sweet potatoes, eggplant, and peppers for their rich flavors and satisfying complexity, then finish the salad with protein-rich chickpeas and a few pumpkin seeds for crunch.

Ecological connection:
Wild rice is not just food; it’s an indicator of ecosystem balance, and its role is analogous to keystone species in the habitats I study. The whole dinner is rooted in conservation—seasonal, resource-conserving ingredients, low-impact farming, and a deep respect for local ecology.

Daily Nutritional & Wellness Summary

  • Nutritional Balance:
    My meals are packed with plant-based proteins: quinoa in the morning, lentils at lunch, chickpeas and wild rice in the evening. This diet keeps my energy steady and my mind clear for fieldwork and analysis. Antioxidants from berries and greens reduce inflammation and support immune defenses. With plenty of fiber, virtually no processed sugars, and minimal saturated fat, my body feels resilient in the face of long days outdoors.

  • Sinus & Allergy Care:
    I design every menu to skip known triggers—no chili at lunch, only a little onion, and lemon only at dinner if my sinuses allow. Hydration is foundational, so I drink water and eat plenty of soup, fruits, and greens throughout the day.

  • Conservation Values:
    Choosing crops that are local, dry-farmed, or water-efficient makes my meals an extension of my professional commitment to sustainable land use. Lentils and chickpeas, as nitrogen-fixers, improve soil health and limit fertilizer runoff. Sourcing honey from wildflower hives supports our native pollinators and ties directly to my work in regional biodiversity.

  • Professional Reflection:
    For me, food is a tangible daily connection to the concepts I apply as an ecologist—drought resilience, resource efficiency, species stewardship, and regenerative agriculture. Each ingredient reminds me in subtle ways of the interconnectedness of human habits and ecosystem health. Sharing this plan with colleagues makes space for collaboration and invites ongoing improvement, blending scientific rigor and daily living.
