## Project Overview

The Mojave Desert Field Ecology Project is dedicated to advancing the understanding and conservation of the region’s arid land ecosystems, with a special focus on preserving the unique biodiversity that defines the Mojave. The project’s primary objectives are as follows:

- Conduct a quantitative analysis of population trends among keystone and indicator species.
- Monitor shifts in phenology and habitat quality in the context of ongoing drought and increasing climate variability.
- Identify sources of ecological disturbance, assess the impact of invasive species, and provide actionable recommendations for mitigation.

### Ecological Focus

This season’s field campaign emphasizes population dynamics for the Mojave Desert tortoise (*Gopherus agassizii*), barrel cactus (*Ferocactus cylindraceus*), and primary avian pollinators such as the Phainopepla (*Phainopepla nitens*). Adaptive management practices, supported by real-time data collection, allow for swift refinement of conservation strategies throughout the project. The integration of new sensor technologies and data analytics enhances decision-making, ensuring timely responses to changing field conditions.

### Current Desert Conditions

Field teams this week observed that the Ridgecrest sector has received only about 30% of average seasonal precipitation so far, leaving soils dry and reducing both the availability of drinking water for wildlife and the extent of flowering among native plants. Morning temperatures have varied between 6°C and 14°C, and wind gusts up to 25 km/h have been regularly recorded at sunrise, impacting both animal and surveyor activity. Notably, several ephemeral washes have shown increased levels of surface disturbance, much of it linked to recent off-road vehicle traffic, which has disrupted sensitive ground cover and microhabitats critical for native seedlings.

## Recent Field Observations & Proposal Development

### Key Ecological Trends

Recent field data highlight several concerning trends. Early morning activity levels around established tortoise burrows have dropped by roughly 30%, likely due to diminished surface moisture and lower availability of plant forage. Vegetation transects reveal delayed and reduced blooming in barrel cactus populations compared to previous years, a likely consequence of ongoing water stress. Off-road vehicle activity has noticeably increased in key ephemeral washes, resulting in significant damage to cryptobiotic soil crusts and reducing seedling recruitment for native plants. These ecosystem disruptions not only threaten resident species but may also degrade habitat resilience to future climate variability.

### Upcoming Research Proposals

In addition to ongoing fieldwork, the team is preparing a pre-proposal for the National Science Foundation’s “Ecology and Evolution of Infectious Diseases” program. The proposed research will focus on how chronic drought stress may influence disease susceptibility and transmission in Mojave tortoise populations. Discussions have also begun with colleagues at the University of California, Riverside, regarding a joint proposal that would examine the resilience of pollinator networks in the face of climate change-driven shifts to plant communities and habitat structure.

Immediate next steps for proposal development include completing and visualizing preliminary data analyses, scheduling a concept call with targeted collaborators, and drafting detailed budget justifications for submission in the upcoming funding cycles.
