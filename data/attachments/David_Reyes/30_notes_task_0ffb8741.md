## 2. Stew Preparation: Workflow and Real-Life Adaptations

### Getting Started

The first step, as usual, was sorting and soaking the dried chickpeas. I started the soak the night before, but on occasion I use canned or pre-cooked chickpeas—especially during particularly dry spells when I need to conserve water. One habit I’ve gotten into, living in the desert Southwest, is to reserve the soaking or cooking water to use later as a broth base. It feels satisfying to avoid waste, even in small ways.

#### Chopping and Mise en Place

Once I was ready to cook, I put on music and got out the cutting board, grouping the carrots, onions, zucchini, and garlic together to save time and cut down on washing knives and boards. The chopping itself felt almost meditative this evening, but I still made sure to keep a bowl nearby for scraps—those always end up in the compost, unless I’m making broth. Sometimes chopping vegetables in bulk like this reminds me of batching tasks at work: being organized up front pays dividends later.

#### Sautéing and Building Flavor

With all the vegetables prepped, I heated olive oil and tossed in onions and garlic, using a moderate flame and placing the lid on the pot to trap heat. This not only conserves energy (useful when it’s already hot in the house), but also keeps the kitchen from warming up too much—a trick I’ve leaned on heavily during Southwestern spring heatwaves.

When the aromatics turned translucent, I added in the carrots, zucchini, and soaked, drained chickpeas—there’s always something comforting about this stage: everything smells rich and savory.

Next, I introduced the cumin, paprika, and bay leaves, measuring them all out in advance. I briefly toasted them in the oil. This step is easy to rush, but I’ve learned that taking the time here really deepens the flavor. Occasionally, distractions have made me skip a spice, so now I set out all the jars in a row before I start—a small change that’s saved a few stews from being bland or overly spicy.

#### Liquids and Simmering

Because I was out of broth, I deglazed the pan with a bit of the reserved chickpea water and poured in the canned tomatoes. I increased the spices slightly and made sure to cover the pot, letting everything simmer gently. Covering helps reduce evaporation and shortens the cooking time, which felt especially important tonight since I wanted to keep the kitchen cool. While the stew gently bubbled, I sliced some bread to serve alongside and tidied the counters. I checked the stew every so often, stirring to prevent sticking.

Just before serving, I tasted and salted the stew, as I’ve learned to do only at the very end—especially since tap water can vary in mineral content here, and over-salting is hard to undo. I plated the stew hot and topped it with chopped herbs from the windowsill planter. Leftovers went straight into containers so I could enjoy them for lunch the next day (and avoid waste).

## 3. Distractions, Focus, and Kitchen Realities

### Disruptions in the Evening Routine

Tonight, I ran into an especially annoying distraction: the Bluetooth speaker kept disconnecting from my phone, derailing my podcast every 10 or 15 minutes. Each time this happened, I’d lose my train of thought, needing to stop and reconnect the device. I actually forgot to toss in the bay leaves at first—something that usually never happens—and had to work backwards to see what I’d missed.

These interruptions cost me at least five minutes overall, but what affected me more was the loss of flow and rhythm. After three drops in a row, I gave up on the Bluetooth, grabbed a pen, and jotted down the prep steps and a quick checklist on paper. Switching to a local radio station in the kitchen helped, too. I could focus again, moving methodically through the last steps of cooking.

### Managing Cognitive Load

Repeated small distractions don’t just slow me down—they also lead to double-checking more steps (was the cumin in? did I chop all the carrots?), and break up the satisfaction of getting ‘in the zone’ while cooking. There’s research showing that task switching and digital interruptions hurt overall efficiency and increase the odds of making mistakes ([3]). I definitely noticed that effect tonight.

#### Tried and True Fixes

Using a physical checklist helped me stay on task. I set out all the spices in advance, so even if my head wandered, I wouldn’t forget any crucial flavor. Muting notifications and letting the radio run in the background—simple as it sounds—made the kitchen routine much smoother. I’ve made a mental note to check my tech setup before cooking sessions from now on, much as I would check field gear before heading out for ecological work.

#### Sidebar: Parallels to Work and Field Projects

Tonight’s minor chaos in the kitchen drove home how easily small tech hiccups throw off more than just dinner. It felt a lot like fieldwork situations where dropped GPS units, failing tablets, or dead batteries disrupt plans and force you back onto pen, paper, and routines. The cooking checklists, ingredient pre-staging, and fallback to radio I used tonight are basically the same resilience strategies I rely on in field research: have low-tech backups, batch prep where possible, and document the issues for next time.

Some habits I plan to maintain:
- Relying on analog entertainment (radio, for example) if I want background noise.
- Doing a simple tech check ahead of time.
- Always pre-measuring spices and laying out ingredients before I start.
- Keeping a record of what worked, what didn’t, and what distracted me, for future improvements.

## 5. Summary and Recommendations

Looking back, tonight’s stew preparation highlighted just how much smooth workflow and conscious ingredient choices affect the experience—not just for time or taste, but for sustainability and personal satisfaction, too. When I pre-stage everything and use a written checklist, cooking feels less stressful and more enjoyable. It’s worth making these steps a habit, especially since interruptions are almost inevitable with modern devices.

From an ecological standpoint, sticking to local, seasonal produce—and making creative use of scraps—has become second nature. Choosing bulk and minimally packaged options not only feels right, it genuinely cuts down on waste. I’ll keep documenting where ingredients come from, both to help guide shopping and to track my environmental impact over time.

Managing cognitive load is crucial. By minimizing digital distractions and incorporating simple analog tools—like a pen-and-paper checklist or background radio—I keep the cooking process focused and pleasant. Recording these process hiccups and my changes each time also helps me refine both kitchen routines and project workflows in other parts of my life and work.

#### Practical Steps to Keep Improving

- Prep and organize all ingredients before starting
- Use written checklists for both recipe and grocery tracking
- Batch tasks when possible (chop all veggies at once, soak beans ahead)
- Shop local and bulk to support sustainability
- Minimize digital interruptions by checking tech before starting, and rely on low-tech alternatives if possible
- Keep a log of what works (and doesn’t) for adjustment in future sessions

I plan to keep iterating on this foundation, making both my dinners and my day-to-day routines more resilient, efficient, and sustainable.
