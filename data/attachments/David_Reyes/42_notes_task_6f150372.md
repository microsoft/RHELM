# Comprehensive Field Journal Entry: Sunrise Hike in the Mojave Desert, Ridgecrest Vicinity

## Date and Location

**Date:** June 18, 2024  
**Location:** Ridgecrest Area, Mojave Desert, Kern County, California  
**GPS Center:** 35.6567° N, 117.6476° W (Ephedra stand, survey epicenter)  
**Elevation:** Approximately 770 m (2,526 ft)

## Weather and Landscape Overview

**Observation Period:** 05:15–07:30 PDT

Arriving on site before dawn, I found the ground temperature a comfortable 21°C at 05:15. By mid-morning (07:30), it had climbed rapidly to 27°C, a shift that was especially notable in exposed areas. In contrast, shallow draws under creosote and Ephedra retained both cool air—remaining at 18–19°C—and higher patches of overnight dew. Relative humidity at sunrise was measured at 8% according to my Kestrel 3500, a typical reading for this time of year. Surface winds were calm at the outset, but by 07:00, light southwesterly breezes had picked up (5–7 km/h), gently stirring the shrub tops. The sky was mostly clear throughout, interrupted only by the occasional strand of cirrus, and the UV index increased quickly after sunrise, underscoring the advantage of early fieldwork in this region.

The substrate here comprises well-drained, gravelly alluvium, with frequent and well-developed cryptobiotic crusts—an indicator of undisturbed desert soils. Topographically, the area is characterized by rolling alluvial fans, dissected by shallow washes and interspersed with stands of Larrea tridentata (creosote bush) and Ephedra nevadensis (Mormon tea). Mojave aster (Xylorhiza tortifolia) blooms clustered conspicuously on the mid-slopes, adding color to the otherwise muted landscape.

Early in the morning, I observed marked cooling in microhabitats sheltered by shrubs and shallow depressions—areas that also hosted the morning’s highest concentrations of birds and arthropods. Such microclimatic variation appeared critical for both foraging and shelter at the start of a desert day.

## Detailed Observational Log

### 05:20 — Le Conte’s Thrasher Pair, Ephedra Stand

**Location:** 35.6569° N, 117.6473° W  
**Microhabitat:** Ephedra nevadensis thicket, edging a low sandy rise with adjacent Larrea–Ambrosia association

Shortly after first light, a pair of Le Conte’s thrashers (Toxostoma lecontei) were actively foraging at the edge of an open Ephedra canopy. The birds moved methodically, alternating between the ground and low Ephedra branches, using short 2–4 meter sally flights to cover ground. I documented vocal duetting—particularly the male’s sharp “whit” and series of descending notes—which elicited an immediate response from the presumed female nestled among neighboring shrubs. The behavioral exchanges and joint movement patterns suggested strong pair cohesion and active defense or assessment of a breeding territory. Notably, the surrounding Ephedra was healthy and fully vegetative, but not yet in bloom.

### 05:50 — Mojave Aster Bloom Survey

**Location:** 35.6573° N, 117.6458° W

At the center of a mid-slope alluvial fan, I recorded peak flowering in Xylorhiza tortifolia (Mojave aster). Approximately 72% of mature plants displayed full anthesis—vivid lavender ray florets and prominent yellow disc florets drawing a steady flow of pollinators. Halictid bees were consistently present on the newly opened flowers; I also observed two Danaus gilippus (queen butterflies) briefly nectar foraging. While many plants were in pristine bloom, a few at the fan base showed corolla wilt, hinting at microclimatic differences along the slope. No fruiting structures—neither achenes nor dehiscent fruits—were apparent, placing the stand firmly in a pre-seed set, peak floral phase. Mojave asters here mingled with Ambrosia dumosa (white bursage) and Baileya multiradiata (desert marigold), both in mixed phenological states ranging from vegetative to partial bloom.

### 06:20 — Noteworthy Plant-Bird Interactions

**Location:** Slope margin, 35.6562° N, 117.6465° W

Bird activity intensified as sunlight struck the upper slopes. A loggerhead shrike (Lanius ludovicianus) caught my attention as it used the commanding height of a creosote bush to scan for prey, eventually making a swift aerial dive to snatch a medium-sized orthopteran. The shrike transferred its prey from the shrub to the ground—a behavior typical of this species.

House finches (Haemorhous mexicanus), traveling in small parties, were focused on pecking Ambrosia seeds from the ground and probing the stem bases of Mojave asters. Judging by their behavior and the moist condition of the stem bases, these foraging bouts likely targeted both nutritional seeds and the limited water held in herbaceous tissues.

Nearby, two pairs of black-throated sparrows (Amphispiza bilineata) foraged close together, moving between creosote bush interspaces and the edges of cryptobiotic soil slabs. Their focus was on invertebrates in the cool, damp soil, where overnight condensation presumably made prey more accessible.

### 07:10 — Herpetofauna and Mammal Signs

At this later stage in the survey, reptile activity became evident. I spotted an adult side-blotched lizard (Uta stansburiana) basking at the interface between a cryptobiotic soil patch and a flat rock (35.6568° N, 117.6478° W), taking advantage of the early sunlight. Parallel to the survey route, I found fresh kangaroo rat (Dipodomys spp.) tracks—clear prints and distinct tail drags threading into an Ephedra thicket. Several new burrow entrances appeared overnight, though no live mammals were visible during my time on site.

## Reflections on Ecological Context and Conservation

Surveying at dawn in the Ridgecrest sector of the Mojave desert offers rare access to the full tempo of activity in an environment notorious for its extremes. Cooler morning microclimates provide a critical opportunity for wildlife—birds, reptiles, and small mammals—to forage, interact, and occupy spaces that will later feel inhospitable. I was struck by the density and diversity of life, especially around persistent shrub clusters and wash margins.

The Le Conte’s thrasher pair, occupying a patchwork of mature Ephedra and Larrea, underscored this landscape's potential as viable habitat for sensitive and range-restricted species. Their persistent territory display, active foraging, and vocal duets anchor the area as a functioning stronghold for the species. With their current IUCN Near Threatened status, sustained sightings like these become essential data points for habitat management and adaptive restoration planning.

Equally compelling was the Mojave aster bloom, which delivered a pulse of resources that benefitted a suite of insects and birds. The constant movement of finches and sparrows among the aster and bursage stands highlighted the intertwined dependencies present in even a small plot of intact desert scrub. These forbs, though ephemeral, reinforce community structure and trophic connectivity during their brief explosion of color and productivity.

The importance of native shrub structure—especially Ephedra and Larrea—was reemphasized by the clustering of animal tracks, bird activity, and the persistence of cryptobiotic crust. Casual signs of herpetofaunal activity and the emergence of new kangaroo rat burrows confirmed the area’s continued ecological function. Such patterns remind me that even subtle shifts in management or climate could ripple through this complex system.

Thorough, timestamped documentation of these species and ecological interactions not only supports regional conservation projects but also builds the case for sustained, science-based stewardship in sensitive desert landscapes.

## Field Equipment Assessment

- **Garmin eTrex 32x GPS:** Maintained accurate satellite lock throughout survey; no drift or waypoint errors noted.  
- **Kestrel 3500 Weather Meter:** Reliable and precise measurements for temperature, relative humidity, and wind; no device malfunctions.  
- **Vortex Diamondback 8×42 Optics:** Provided crisp, condensation-free views from pre-dawn onwards.  
- **Field Data Collection:** Waterproof notebook and Rite-in-the-Rain pen proved essential for jotting real-time observations—materials remained reliable even amid low humidity.  
- **Mobile Survey App (Survey123 for ArcGIS):** Allowed in-field digital entry, with seamless data integration upon return to base (occasional desert signal loss required post-field syncing).

In summary, all equipment operated effectively under warm, dry conditions. Daily digital backup of georeferenced data minimized risk of information loss, while the combination of electronic and manual methods afforded flexibility and redundancy. Reliable optics and climate readings contributed to robust, repeatable bird and plant surveys. Integrating analog and digital workflows is recommended for extended, multidisciplinary projects in arid environments.
