Discussion Summary

1. Integrating TEK into Plant Stewardship and Monitoring

Scientific Foundations

Dr. Priya Rao presented an in-depth review of established plant monitoring protocols used in the Mojave desert, referencing guidelines from the California Department of Fish and Wildlife (CDFW) and Bureau of Land Management (BLM) ([1], [2]). Standard practices rely on quadrat and transect sampling, most often using 10x10m plots set at systematic random intervals, repeated annually to monitor temporal changes. Dr. Rao emphasized the growing utility of GPS and GIS technologies to map species distribution and habitat quality, offering the potential for more precise, landscape-scale trend analysis.

Traditional Knowledge and Cultural Context

The Cahuilla Elder shared critical insights from the perspective of traditional stewardship, highlighting plants such as Yucca schidigera (Mojave yucca), Larrea tridentata (creosote bush), and Prosopis glandulosa (honey mesquite) as central not only for ecological functions—shelter, water management, wildlife forage—but also for their cultural relevance, food sources, and use in traditional practices. The Elder stressed the importance of minimizing disturbance through non-extractive monitoring, such as visual counts and participant observation rather than collection. Phenological cues like flowering and fruiting hold additional meaning, marking seasonal events and guiding resource gathering. For example, the first bloom of brittlebush ("Encelia farinosa") signals the shift in wildlife activity patterns and is intertwined with ritual timing.

Importantly, the Elder insisted on recognition of cultural protocols, such as offering a song or words of blessing (“mihawiš”) before entering sensitive areas. These actions foster respect and reciprocity with the land and, moving forward, will be formally noted in survey logs in recognition of their significance.

Pathways for Integration and Adaptive Management

All participants affirmed the value of integrating TEK and scientific practices. The group agreed that collaborative, adaptive management is essential for responding to ongoing environmental changes—including episodic drought and habitat fragmentation—while also upholding cultural priorities. Practical measures discussed include: - Jointly developing monitoring protocols, - Adjusting sampling frequency and timing in response to both environmental signals and traditional knowledge, - Iterative feedback between researchers and Elders, - Transparent documentation of protocol modifications and cultural observances to ensure both auditability and mutual respect.

Reference materials such as the USFWS Adaptive Management Handbook ([3]) were highlighted for their relevance to this evolving approach.

2. Refinement of Mojave Ground Squirrel Survey Transects

Current Protocols and Field Realities

David Reyes reviewed existing CDFW protocol for ground squirrel monitoring, which involves systematically spaced line transect surveys (typically every 500 meters, stratified by habitat) and GPS-based location data collection (Garmin Instinct, WGS84 datum, decimal degrees). The process is designed to ensure representative site coverage while maintaining data quality and replicability.

Transect Adjustments Guided by TEK and Field Experience

Building on TEK contributions and recent field observations, several transects were shifted to honor active Cahuilla gathering areas and ephemeral water sources historically recognized as wildlife "sharing sites." Key criteria guiding these changes included: - Protection of undisturbed plant stands and culturally significant sites, - Avoidance of locations associated with annual ceremonies, - Realignment to more accurately reflect recent ground squirrel activity, - Exclusion of GPS-challenged areas such as steep-walled arroyos where satellite signal is unreliable.

Every adjustment was recorded with clear rationale, reference to either TEK or field data, and agreement among scientific and cultural observers.

Addressing GPS Data Capture Issues

Both Dr. Rao and David Reyes reported multiple instances—especially in arroyos—of GPS drift (20–60 meters) and lost (“NULL”) waypoints. Field notes highlighted the persistent challenges of variable terrain, where satellite coverage can fluctuate. The Cahuilla Elder observed that desert topography changes dynamically with weather and water, underscoring the need for both digital and manual recordkeeping and for flexibility in technology-dependent methods.

To address these challenges, the team will: - Annotate all suspect or missing GPS points directly within GIS layers, referencing hand-written field logs as verification, - Update metadata to capture calibration details, device accuracy, and presence or absence of cultural protocols for each survey event, - Schedule post-survey data audits and revisit field sites where GPS uncertainty remains, ensuring team presence of both scientific and TEK representatives.

GPS Data Capture Issues and Actions

Identified Issues: - Several waypoints logged as “NULL” or showing significant drift (20–60m) in deep arroyos, affecting original locations at revised TR-08. - Device accuracy at some points did not exceed ±15m during the March survey window. - One instance of accidental datum change, with correction immediately entered into logs. - Some field logs initially omitted documentation regarding performance of cultural protocols at survey points; this process is now standardized.

Corrective Actions: - All anomalous GPS points will be annotated within the GIS database and cross-referenced with field notes. - Sites affected by GPS uncertainty will be reviewed during a scheduled post-survey audit, with additional site visits if required. - Updated metadata fields will record: calibration status, device accuracy, and cultural protocol observance for every entry. - Appendices to final documentation will include both digital and hand-logged records to ensure a complete audit trail.
