Field Safety Guidelines

Thorough preparation for the Mojave Desert’s unique environment is critical. The following guidelines are emphasized during onboarding and reviewed with Maria before deployment:

Mojave Desert–Specific Hazards

  • Extreme Heat: The Mojave routinely experiences temperatures above 40°C (104°F) during summer. All volunteers must be vigilant for heat-related illnesses such as heat exhaustion and heat stroke. Frequent hydration, scheduled breaks in shade, and strict sun protection measures are mandatory.

  • Dehydration: Volunteers are required to drink more than 3 liters of water daily, supplemented with electrolyte solutions during extended fieldwork. Every individual must carry backup water supplies at all times.

  • Wildlife Encounters: The region is home to potentially dangerous species, including rattlesnakes, scorpions, and Gila monsters. Volunteers are trained to scan for wildlife, avoid reaching into concealed spaces, wear protective boots, and follow established distancing procedures. First aid protocols for bites and stings are reviewed before each field day and re-communicated as needed.

  • Navigation Challenges: The landscape’s monotonous appearance and sparse natural landmarks can pose serious navigation risks. Volunteers must demonstrate proficiency with GPS devices and compass-based orientation. Implementation of a buddy system (minimum team size of two) is strictly enforced to reduce the risk of disorientation and improve emergency response.

Essential Equipment and Communication Protocols

All volunteers are required to carry the following throughout each field day:

  • GPS device with spare batteries
  • Fully programmed field radio (team and emergency channels)
  • Comprehensive emergency medical kit
  • Minimum 3 liters water per person, plus reserves
  • Wide-brimmed hats, high-SPF sunscreen (SPF 30+), sunglasses
  • Emergency whistle and high-visibility vest

To ensure safety and rapid response capacity:

  • Teams must check in regularly at pre-established intervals via radio.
  • Satellite messengers are issued for use at remote or high-risk sites.
  • Locations of the nearest medical facilities and detailed emergency evacuation procedures are communicated and accessible to all field staff.

Data Collection and Record-Keeping Standards

  • All observations must be documented using agency-provided datasheets or approved mobile applications, with precise GPS coordinates recorded for each observation.
  • At the end of each shift, Maria and her field supervisor must review and sign all forms and digital records to maintain a clear chain of custody for data.
  • Any field incidents—including injuries, near-misses, or identified hazards—must be reported within two hours using the agency’s formal incident reporting protocol. These immediate reports are critical for prompt action and continuous safety improvements.
