## 3. Field Impact Notes

### Survey Timing and Adjustments

The day began with a 15-minute delay as I worked to manage early sinus discomfort. This pause let me adjust to conditions and check all personal protective gear—absolutely worth the short wait, since heading out prematurely would have compromised both my focus and the day’s observations.

During the morning, high-dust transect exposures were intentionally kept brief. Whenever possible, I paused at natural windbreaks—shelterbelts or larger shrubs—letting the worst gusts subside before resuming work. Just before midday, I altered the planned route to detour around a particularly exposed open flat, sticking instead to southern shelter belts. While this meant a minor reduction in planned sample points, the overall data quality was stronger thanks to the better working conditions.

### Documentation and Equipment Challenges

At mid-morning, I had to pause documentation briefly when condensation fogged my camera lens after mask removal for water. A quick cleaning solved the issue, but revealed the kind of small but real challenges created by managing both self-care and scientific gear simultaneously. Later, during one of the more intense sinus pain episodes around 11:45, my concentration slipped and a few macro plant images were slightly less sharp than usual. Reviewing the images with field notes and voice memos allowed me to correct and clarify important data, ensuring no information was lost.

### Ecological Data Quality

Overall, I managed to capture all critical observations for the day. However, I did notice that symptom flare-ups, especially around mid-day, led to some shortened field sketches and less detailed phenology notes. I made it a point to recheck and flesh out these notes during afternoon rest breaks, using audio memos and quick reference sketches to fill in any gaps.

### Team Dynamics and Adaptive Work Strategy

Maintaining clear communication with my field team was essential. I let everyone know when I was experiencing more severe symptoms so they could provide backup, particularly when fine detail work or data entry was needed. We also agreed to push lichen sampling to later in the afternoon—by then, my symptoms and the environmental conditions had both improved, making it safer and more efficient to tackle that task.

## 5. Best Practices for Health and Field Journaling

- **Systematic, Timestamped Tracking:** Detailed logs of symptoms, triggers, mitigation actions, and preventive measures provide a strong foundation for both occupational safety and scientific rigor in the field.
- **Environmental Context Integration:** Consistently recording situational details—weather, dust, sun exposure—adds valuable context for both personal health management and for interpreting ecological data patterns.
- **Reflective Evening Assessments:** Taking time each evening for structured self-reflection, reviewing both biological care and mental state, supports continuous improvement of field methods and promotes psychological resilience.
- **Team Communication and Adaptability:** Openly sharing health constraints and adapting work strategies with the team preserves both data quality and safety, modeling best practices for field-based research in harsh environments.

This record structure aligns with professional guidance for personal health and safety in fieldwork and the best practices of ecological research journaling, particularly under arid desert conditions (see sources below).
