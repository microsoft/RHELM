Field Exercise Log – David Reyes

Morning Routine at Short Canyon East Rim, Kern County, CA – July 3, 2024

Overview

This report documents my structured morning exercise routine on July 3, 2024, at the east rim of Short Canyon in Kern County, California (GPS: 35.6135° N, 117.8917° W). The routine is tailored for physical readiness in desert fieldwork and conservation activities. Below, I detail the activities performed, biomechanical notes, gear setup and maintenance, as well as environmental conditions and how I managed hydration and health throughout the session. Drawing on both hands-on experience and established field guidelines, I also share recommendations for future work in similar arid environments.

Activity Rationale and Biomechanics

Dynamic Stretches:
I started with 10 minutes of dynamic stretching, targeting hips, legs, and shoulders—essential for flexibility before fieldwork in rough terrain. This routine always feels vital for reducing muscle stiffness and lowering my risk of injury during demanding conservation tasks [1]. Actively moving through stretches helps energize me and gets my mind focused for the session.

Push-ups:
Three sets of 15 push-ups at a moderate pace really engage my upper body, especially shoulders and triceps, which I rely on for lifting gear and scrambling over rocks. I pay close attention to keeping my elbows at about 45°, wrists straight, and core tight—it makes a noticeable difference in shoulder stability [2]. The granite slab here provides a stable base, and the lack of gloves improves grip. I always make sure to apply sunscreen just before starting since the sun comes up fast.

Planks:
Plank holds (45 seconds per set, three sets) on rough ground are a staple for my core stability. Setting my forearms on the Osprey pack provides extra comfort and keeps my lumbar spine aligned even on uneven canyon terrain [3]. This simple tweak helps maintain good form when conditions aren’t ideal.

Cool-Down Stretches:
After the working sets, I move through five minutes of slow static stretches, focusing on my quads, calves, and lats. I take deep, controlled breaths to relax my body and ensure smooth recovery [4]. These last stretches always bring my heart rate back down and leave me feeling refreshed.

Equipment Maintenance:
A mesh pocket on my Osprey Atmos AG 65 backpack showed signs of wear. During my break, I used Oscar’s patch and my multitool for a quick fix. I always prioritize equipment repairs immediately to avoid bigger problems—they can make or break a day in the desert.

Equipment and Gear Assessment

  • Osprey Atmos AG 65: This pack stands up well to desert conditions; its AirSpeed™ mesh back panel is especially helpful for keeping my back cooler and drier [6].
  • Oscar’s Patch: Field repairs like patching a backpack mesh pocket need to be fast and reliable; Oscar’s patch did the job on the spot, which means less time lost.
  • Sunscreen: SPF 50 gets applied before stepping out, again mid-session, and post-cool-down. High UV indices here are non-negotiable, and I closely monitor reapplication frequency [7].
  • Hydration Gear: I carry both a 2L hydration reservoir and a backup insulated bottle, and always draw water from a filtered spring after checking total dissolved solids (TDS) for safety.

Weather Observations & Environmental Challenges

The July desert morning was clear and warming rapidly, with temperatures starting at 74°F and climbing to 81°F (23–27°C) over the session. Relative humidity dropped from 17% to 13%, and there was a slight W/NW breeze (2–4 mph).

  • Managing Heat: An early start is the best way to avoid the worst thermal stress. By finishing before 8:00 am, I avoid the peak heat risk, which ramps up sharply after mid-morning.
  • Solar Exposure: The sun cresting the ridge by 06:20 signals ramped UV levels—my skin feels the difference, and I’m always alert to reapply sunscreen as needed.
  • Hydration Needs: Low humidity and strong sun speed up sweating and respiratory moisture loss. Throughout, I monitor and adjust my hydration, trusting recommendations for desert fieldwork (0.7–1L per hour) as a guideline [8].

Hydration Strategy

I drank about 1.1 liters of water during the routine, starting with 250 mL before stretches, 100 mL after each push-up/plank set, and another 250 mL during cool-down and post-repair—timed with activity segments. All water came from a spring I filtered on-site, and I kept sterile bottled water as a backup.

On days when my field sessions go longer, I include electrolyte tablets. Regular, segmented water intake matches guidelines for hot, dry fieldwork and helps avoid cramps and headaches later in the day [8].

Observations on Health Through the Routine

  • Cardiovascular: My heart rate peaked around 120–140 bpm during push-ups and returned to calm levels during stretching and the maintenance break. I didn’t experience any dizziness or abnormal fatigue [9].
  • Musculoskeletal: Mild soreness cropped up in triceps and core—expected after a full set of push-ups and planks—but joints stayed pain-free. Skin did feel dry by the end, so I applied moisturizer after cooling down.
  • Hydration/Heat Stress: No signs of dehydration or heat stress—no headache, confusion, or muscle cramping. Urine output was clear and adequate, which I always check before packing up.
  • Respiratory: Breath control was steady throughout and quickly recovered post-session.
  • Overall Fitness: I felt “field ready” after the routine, energized and confident for a full day of work.

Recommendations for Desert Fieldwork Practice

  1. Start Early: Morning routines before peak heat (ideally done by 8:00 am) dramatically reduce heat stress and fatigue.
  2. Hydrate Frequently & Consistently: Stick to regular, segmented water intake. Use electrolyte supplements for sessions lasting more than one hour.
  3. Gear Maintenance: Immediate field repairs—patches, multitools—should always be close at hand to keep gear functional and morale up.
  4. Sun Protection: Use SPF 50 sunscreen, with frequent reapplication tracked; high UV is a given in these settings.
  5. Monitor Health Continuously: Track cardiovascular and musculoskeletal responses to spot any warning signs early. Keep routine logs to compare across field days and seasons.
  6. Standardized Logs: Consistent, structured logs allow effective tracking—personal health management and fieldwork strategy both improve with good data.
  7. Preparedness and Maintenance: Reliable field performance depends on preparedness and self-care. These logs don't just support personal health—they are an essential piece of successful conservation work.

Closing Thoughts

Balancing structured exercise, proper hydration, sun protection, and diligent equipment maintenance is the foundation for safe and effective desert fieldwork. I find that keeping a formatted log helps me and my colleagues assess readiness, adapt our strategies, and stay resilient in Kern County’s challenging landscape. These practices are central not only to personal health but to the long-term success of conservation efforts in arid habitats.
