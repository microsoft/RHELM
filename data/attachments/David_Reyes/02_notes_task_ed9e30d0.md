## Introduction

Living and working as a research ecologist in Ridgecrest, on the edge of the Mojave Desert, has shaped not just my approach to fieldwork and conservation, but also my daily choices—including the way I buy my groceries. My professional commitment to sustainability and resource stewardship guides even routine tasks like shopping. Over the years, I’ve developed a system for sourcing food, personal care, and field supplies that support my health in harsh conditions while aligning with my environmental values.

This report documents my most recent online grocery order. It’s not just a list, but a reflection of what it means to shop thoughtfully in the desert—balancing nutrition, practicality, sustainability, and personal comfort. I hope these details offer insight into one ecologist’s attempt to align everyday life with a larger conservation ethic.

## Sustainability and Practicality: Core Principles

Every item I order has to earn a place both for its usefulness and its environmental footprint. In my climate and field routine, I focus on:

- **Choosing Organic and Recycled:** When available, I pick products certified organic or 100% recycled (like Seventh Generation bath tissue) to avoid excess chemicals and support better farming practices.
- **Low-Waste and Reusable:** The shift to bar soaps, silicone bags, menstrual cups, and beeswax wraps has cut my trash output dramatically. I keep an eye out for glass, metal, or compostable packaging to avoid plastic.
- **Local and Regional Sourcing:** I favor crops from California or neighboring states—reducing transport emissions and supporting regional resilience. Items like rice, carrots, and dates all come from nearby farms.
- **Field-Tested Essentials:** The desert sun is relentless, so I swear by mineral sunscreens, hydration mixes, and rugged snacks like trail mix or Kind bars for busy days outdoors.
- **Minimalist Field Hygiene:** Personal care staples like solid shampoo bars and refillable hand sanitizer pull double duty—effective in the wild, gentle on the land.

---

## Reflections and Insights

My ordering habits double as a form of research. Tracking what I buy, how fast I use it, and what creates waste helps me identify real opportunities for impact in my own life. Documenting these purchases in a log or field journal also lets me see changes and recurring needs—for instance, how much sunscreen or electrolyte mix I go through on multi-day surveys, or which snacks hold up best in summer heat.

Over time, I’ve seen my waste volume drop, especially as I replace single-use plastics with truly reusable alternatives. I’ve also noticed that investing in quality (for example, higher-end olive oil or bar soap) means supplies last longer and I make fewer repeat purchases. Compared to when I started fieldwork years ago, today I feel more confident that my personal habits genuinely align with my professional beliefs.

---

## Looking Ahead

I plan to keep refining both what I buy and how I track it. Log sheets for consumption and waste, plus simple satisfaction notes (which jerky packs best, which oat milks survive the ride to the survey plot) help me adapt both my personal and professional routines for a healthier, lower-impact lifestyle.

Integrating this information into broader fieldwork reporting could eventually help our team quantify the sustainability benefits of personal choices—even in extreme environments like the Mojave. Sharing what works (and what doesn’t) benefits the whole community of researchers and conservationists facing similar daily realities.
