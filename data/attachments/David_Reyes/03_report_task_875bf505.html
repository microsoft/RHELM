Comprehensive Grant Review Summary

Mojave Desert Climate Adaptation Project – Partial Review
Reviewer: David Reyes
Review Date: June 2024

Grant Objective

The Mojave Desert Climate Adaptation Project is designed to strengthen the resilience of desert ecosystems and support the wellbeing of local communities as they face increasing climate variability. The project focuses on several core strategies:

  • Restoring and Enhancing Native Habitats: By revitalizing arid land ecosystems, we aim to provide natural buffers that help mitigate the impacts of extreme temperatures and prolonged drought conditions so common in the Mojave region.

  • Implementing Adaptive Management for Key Species: Adaptive strategies, informed by ongoing ecological monitoring and scenario modeling, will be put in place to protect priority species and ensure that crucial ecological processes remain stable, even as conditions change.

  • Optimizing Water and Resource Management: Recognizing water scarcity as a growing challenge, the project will adopt efficient management techniques that consider current and projected climate conditions, supporting both ecological and community needs.

  • Engaging Mojave Communities: Meaningful engagement is central. Through sustained dialogue and co-development of strategies with Indigenous groups and other vulnerable community members, the project strives to ensure adaptation efforts are inclusive and generate equitable outcomes.

These priorities mirror both regional and national adaptation frameworks, such as the U.S. National Climate Adaptation Strategy and the Open Standards for the Practice of Conservation. The design is well-tailored to the Mojave’s unique ecological and socio-economic landscapes, addressing vulnerabilities identified in previous assessments.[1][2]

Field Conditions and Personal Challenges

Fieldwork throughout June 2024 presented continual physical and operational challenges. Air quality regularly dipped well below safe thresholds due to persistent dust storms and high particulate levels.

Sinus Irritation and Dust Exposure:
From June 3 through June 7, my field journal notes almost daily interruptions in survey work. Even with routine use of N95 respirators, sinus discomfort quickly set in, causing significant fatigue and, at times, requiring early stoppage—interrupting not only data collection, but also energy for evening desk review.

Productivity and Concentration Impacts:
Days with elevated ozone and PM2.5 (EPA AIRNow data shows frequent readings over 50 µg/m³) corresponded closely with headaches and diminished focus during the late afternoons and evenings. Data integration duties, which require prolonged concentration, often had to be postponed or divided into shorter work segments.

Operational Disruptions:
Dust storm advisories added unpredictability to an already complex schedule. We frequently made abrupt changes—sometimes with only an hour’s notice—resulting in canceled surveys or missed stakeholder interviews. Maintaining even basic contact with some community partners became especially challenging during these periods.[5]

Recommendations and Immediate Actions

To stay on track and adaptively manage under these challenging conditions, I recommend the following prioritized, evidence-based actions:

1. Complete Outstanding Ecological Data Collection

  • Deploy portable rain loggers at all remaining sites and ensure immediate data upload for drought and hydrology modeling. Cross-reference field instruments with NOAA precipitation data[3] for calibration.
  • Shift indicator species monitoring to staggered field teams, dispatching during projected windows of low dust and favorable weather, as outlined in the Open Standards for Conservation[1].
  • Expedite soil moisture sensor repairs and work collaboratively with USGS[4] for rapid integration of all available soil data.

2. Strengthen Stakeholder Engagement and Data Collection

  • Relaunch community adaptation survey simultaneously online and at in-person gathering points to widen participation.
  • Schedule dedicated dialogue sessions with Indigenous and other underrepresented partners. All facilitated engagement should follow USFWS Adaptive Management guidelines[2] to encourage direct input and co-design of adaptation strategies.
  • Establish a standardized process for logging event attendance and community feedback, with support from partner NGOs and local liaisons.

3. Operational and Health Adaptation

  • Institute real-time air quality monitoring for all field staff, integrating health risk thresholds into daily operational decision-making. Field staff should rotate duty schedules and adhere to rest cycles during high pollution events.
  • Develop a surge plan, enabling rapid redeployment and intensification of data collection once weather conditions improve, ensuring gaps are filled without overburdening team members.

4. Promote Iterative Learning and Documentation

  • Hold quarterly "pause and reflect" sessions with all core team members to synthesize lessons, refocus priorities, and document adaptive changes, as recommended by Open Standards[1].
  • Create a centralized digital log to record all project adaptations—including fieldwork interruptions and problem resolution measures—to inform both current adaptive management and future grant reporting.
