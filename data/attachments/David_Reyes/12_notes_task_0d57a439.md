## Freeform Notes: Vegetation, Microhabitats, and Adaptive Strategies

### Vegetation & Microhabitats

The dominant vegetation at my study sites included creosote bush (Larrea tridentata), bursage (Ambrosia dumosa), saltbush (Atriplex spp.), and Mojave yucca (Yucca schidigera). Animal activity was consistently concentrated in the shrub understory zones, where added cover provided safety and foraging opportunities. Sandy arroyo stretches supported high burrow density, while the more exposed, gravelly outcrops were favored for basking. Caliche outcrops were noticeably quiet in terms of burrow activity, likely due to the substrate's increased hardness.

### Abiotic Factors

Throughout the day, I observed a pronounced temperature gradient—up to 15°C difference at midday—between shaded sand and exposed gravel. Shifts in wind and humidity seemed to drive changes in behavior, with subtle cues like soil moisture or sun angle affecting foraging patterns. Microclimate dynamics were especially visible during periods of rapid weather change.

### Ecological Adaptations & Behavioral Impressions

White-tailed antelope squirrels frequently used tail-flagging displays when exposed on open ground. In high-heat microhabitats, body flattening and synchronized group movements appeared to function as both thermoregulatory tactics and deterrents to aerial predators. Fast, coordinated retreats following alarm calls highlighted the strength of social communication within squirrel groups. Particularly striking was the use of communal tail-flagging during alarm events (notably observed at 11:40), which may represent a novel group anti-predator strategy worthy of deeper investigation. I also observed temporal niche separation between squirrels and lizards, with peak activity alternating between these taxa over the day, reflecting fine-scale habitat partitioning.
