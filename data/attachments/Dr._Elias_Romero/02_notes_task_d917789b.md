## 3. Narrative Reflection and Critical Self-Evaluation

Whenever I shop at Mercat de Sant Antoni, especially with public health and sustainability goals in mind, I’m reminded how interconnected food, health, and environmental choices are. On today’s trip, I set out feeling determined to buy mostly local and organic produce, reuse packaging, and reduce waste wherever possible. Most vendors know I prefer to use my cloth bags and refillable bottles; still, real life crept in—I forgot my main grocery tote, so I ended up using a plastic bag for the fish, and had no choice but to accept the strawberries in their standard plastic punnet.

The moment that defined today’s shopping experience—and tested my food safety judgment—was the sea bass. Even though the vendor spoke confidently about its freshness, when I got it home, a sharp, almost sour fishy odor filled the kitchen as soon as I unwrapped it. The skin had a sticky, unpleasant sliminess—clear indicators from my training and experience that freshness was in question. No matter how much I disliked the idea of wasting food (and money), I knew it wasn’t safe to eat. I threw it out, feeling a mix of frustration, disappointment, and a lingering guilt about the wasted animal and wasted resources. Yet, I felt reassured in my decision to protect my health.

Most of my other purchases went according to plan: juicy local tomatoes, crisp just-picked spinach, and still-warm bread that made the whole bag smell like a bakery. Packing these items into my cloth bags and seeing the minimal plastic in my cart gave me a small sense of pride, but the handful of unavoidable single-use packaging reminded me how market systems still set the boundaries of what I can do as an individual.

Adapting my meals was a test of resilience. Losing the fish meant improvising a vegetarian dinner—and though I missed the protein, I added more vegetables and made sure to use up leftover bread. It wasn’t the meal I originally pictured, but I felt lighter and still satisfied. Throughout the day, I stuck mostly to what I’d planned to eat, and the structure helped me eat more mindfully—even small changes, like adding tomatoes to my lunch stir-fry, felt positive and spontaneous rather than disruptive.

As I reflect, I notice a mix of emotions running beneath my day: satisfaction at sticking to my values and building healthy, mostly plant-based meals, but guilt about both the wasted sea bass and the stubborn persistence of plastic, especially in produce packaging. I’m glad for the small connections with food vendors—the baker sharing his bread-saving tricks, and the produce seller suggesting I use bell pepper trimmings for stock. Those moments make the process feel more personal and reinforce the importance of community in eating well and sustainably.

Today’s experience underscored a few points that keep coming up in my public health nutrition research:
- Sometimes protecting food safety means accepting food waste, even when it’s wasteful and distressing.
- Despite my best intentions, environmental sustainability is limited by what’s available or structurally possible at the local market.
- Meal planning supports dietary goals, but flexibility is indispensable—especially when faced with unpredictable food quality.
- My own feelings—from pride and connection to guilt and disappointment—powerfully shape the way I shop, eat, and plan for the future.

Every shopping trip is part learning process, part daily routine. I leave today with renewed motivation to double-check my reusable bags, to keep advocating for better packaging alternatives, and to be forgiving with myself when the ideal and the real world don’t align.
