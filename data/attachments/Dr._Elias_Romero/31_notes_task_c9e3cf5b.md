## 1. Introduction: Site, Date, and Context

On August 18th, 2024, I carried out a batch meal preparation in my home kitchen on Carrer d’Astúries, right in the heart of Barcelona’s Gràcia neighborhood. I specifically chose this kitchen for its proximity to Mercat de l’Abaceria—a source of fresh, seasonal ingredients—and because I can easily maintain a high standard of food safety in this familiar environment. The main goal for this session was to prepare two dishes—Mediterranean Farro Salad and a Grilled Vegetable Medley—demonstrating both practical applications of nutrition principles and how to make the most of peak-season Mediterranean produce. This kitchen session also served as a pilot for collecting data on nutritional content and acceptability for future teaching demonstrations.

## 3. Stepwise Recipe with Practical and Nutritional Annotations

### Mediterranean Farro Salad

#### Ingredients
- Farro, cherry tomatoes, cucumber, red onion, basil, mint, extra-virgin olive oil, lemon, sea salt, black pepper, and (optional) feta cheese.

#### Preparation

1. **Farro Preparation**  
   I rinsed 400g farro thoroughly under cold water and cooked it in 1.5L of boiling water, seasoned with sea salt, for roughly 28 minutes until pleasantly tender. This method, consistent with whole grain best practices [1], yields a chewy, flavorful base with a lower glycemic load than refined grains.  
   *Note*: Barley, spelt, or whole-grain rice are viable alternatives for those with gluten intolerance.

2. **Vegetable and Herb Prep**  
   While the farro simmered, I halved the cherry tomatoes, diced the cucumber, and sliced the red onion very fine for balance and color. I waited until just before combining to chop basil and mint, preserving both flavor and nutrient integrity [2]. Sourcing these locally in-season maximized vitamin C and polyphenol content. On past occasions, I’ve used spring onion instead of red onion, or swapped parsley for the herbs if supplies run short or dietary needs require it.

3. **Mixing the Salad Base**  
   Once the cooked farro cooled to room temperature, I combined it in a large bowl with the vegetables, chopped herbs, sea salt, and pepper. The grains soaked up the flavors beautifully.

4. **Dressing**  
   In a small bowl, I whisked together 60 ml of extra-virgin olive oil and the juice of one fresh lemon. Olive oil’s healthy monounsaturated fats play a key role in nutrient absorption and cardiovascular health [3].

5. **Finishing and Optional Feta**  
   I dressed the salad, mixing gently to distribute the oil and lemon evenly. Just before serving, I sprinkled crumbled, pasteurized feta on top for extra creaminess and flavor—a step I recommend delaying until the last minute, especially if the salad is to be stored.

6. **Food Safety Throughout**  
   I maintained strict cold chain for all perishables, keeping everything under 5°C when not actively prepping [4]. Utensils and work surfaces were sanitized, and contact between raw and ready-to-eat ingredients was rigorously avoided.

---

### Grilled Vegetable Medley

#### Ingredients
- Eggplant, zucchini, red and yellow bell peppers, garlic, extra-virgin olive oil, sea salt, black pepper.

#### Preparation

1. **Prepping and Salting**  
   I sliced all the vegetables to about 1cm thick, giving particular attention to the eggplant. After salting the slices and letting them sit for 10 minutes, I rinsed and patted them dry to draw out bitterness—a trick that leaves the texture silky and the flavor sweet.

2. **Seasoning and Tossing**  
   I tossed the vegetables with 30 ml olive oil, minced garlic, and a liberal sprinkle of salt and black pepper until evenly coated.

3. **Grilling**  
   I preheated two grill pans on high to keep up with batch production. Laying the vegetables in a single layer, I grilled each side for 4–5 minutes, flipping once, until dark grill lines formed and the vegetables softened. Quick, high-heat grilling helps preserve water-soluble nutrients [5].

4. **Serving**  
   I arranged the vegetables on a large platter and scattered a handful of fresh herbs on top, which added both aroma and vibrant color.

### Additional Notes: Substitutions and Nutrition

- **Gluten-free variation**: Quinoa or brown rice works perfectly in place of farro for those avoiding gluten.
- **Plant-based alternative**: The salad is delicious without feta, or with a plant-based cheese.
- **Food safety**: For both dishes, I only combined cheese or cooked vegetables with the salad base immediately before serving to minimize microbial risk.
- **Seasonality and Sustainability**: Using peak-season local produce was not just a nutritional decision, but also about supporting local growers and sustainability.
- **Dietary Compliance**: The recipes were kept within recommended limits for sodium, saturated fats, and fiber per Spanish and European public health guidelines [6].

## 4. Preparation and Storage Protocols

From the moment I finished prepping, I cooled all cooked components rapidly (to ≤5°C within two hours) using shallow stainless-steel trays. Each batch was packed into clearly labeled, airtight BPA-free containers, noting the date of preparation, dish name, and a “consume within 72 hours” advisory. Both digital and handwritten preparation logs were updated in line with research requirements. To prevent any risk of cross-contamination, I sanitized all surfaces throughout the process and assigned separate utensils for raw vegetables and cooked items.

## 5. Sensory and Process Observations

### Sensory Assessment

Tasting the final dishes is always one of my favorite moments. The farro salad had a refreshing, herbal fragrance thanks to the mint and basil, while the farro itself brought a satisfying chewiness, perfectly balanced by crisp cucumbers. The dressing was light but flavorful, accentuating the sweetness of the tomatoes without overwhelming the grains.

The grilled vegetable medley was beautifully smoky and caramelized on the outside; the eggplant was tender and rich, zucchini sweet, and the peppers added bright color and sweetness.

### Process Reflections

Using a mandoline in batch prep made slicing faster and more consistent, which contributed to even cooking and great texture. I also found that blanching the sliced red onion briefly in acidulated water reduced its harshness, making the salad more widely acceptable. Each portion—about 250 g for salad and 120 g for grilled vegetables—was hearty and balanced, as intended for teaching and research samples.

## 6. Batch Cooking Methodology 

To streamline workflow, I prepped vegetables for grilling while monitoring the farro, maximizing efficiency. I cooked the farro in a large pot and used two grill pans simultaneously, allowing me to scale production without overloading any one phase of the process.

Following mass meal preparation standards, I cooled cooked farro in a layer less than 5 cm deep and grilled vegetables in single layers to ensure even cooking and safe, rapid cooling. I kept all procedures in line with EFSA and Spanish health authority guidelines [4][6], and followed HACCP principles to minimize health hazards at any critical point.

## 8. Limitations and Recommendations

### Ingredient and Process Variability

Using local markets means flavor and texture in herbs and vegetables do vary with season and producer, occasionally requiring substitutions like parsley for basil which could slightly shift the nutrient profile. Despite scaling up prep by using dual grill pans and large pots, home kitchen size restricted batch capacity. For industrial or large-scale teaching events, I recommend commercial prep space, standardized slicing equipment, and pre-arranged ingredient traceability to farm or cooperative level for full transparency.

### Documentation and Traceability

Market receipts were collected at purchase for provenance, but for research requiring full traceability, direct communication with suppliers or farm-level certification would be essential.

### Future Steps

Going forward, OCR transcription of handwritten notes should be added for searchability and ease of sharing. For teaching demonstrations or research requiring larger batches, investing in commercial-grade prep equipment and expanding sourcing procedures are crucial next steps.
