## 3. Methodology and Rationale

### 3.1. Original Cooking Method (Lucía's Recipe)

1. Sauté onion in olive oil until translucent.
2. Add chopped zucchini and potato; cook until softened.
3. Pour in vegetable stock; simmer until all vegetables are tender.
4. Blend the cooked vegetables with mint, salt, pepper, and lemon juice.
5. Chill thoroughly; garnish each serving with Greek yogurt.

This method yields a classic creamy, cold soup high in summer appeal and approachable flavors, balancing starch, healthy fat, and fresh herbs.

### 3.2. Modifications and Evidence-Based Rationale

Each step of the recipe was carefully reconsidered with nutritional goals, culinary quality, and public health implications in mind:

1. **Reduction of Olive Oil**  
   Using half the olive oil compared to the original, I incorporated a water-sauté technique. This significantly lowered the overall fat and energy content while maintaining a satisfactory mouthfeel—olive oil’s depth is still present but more restrained.

2. **Onion Adjustment**  
   Downsizing the amount of onion lightened the flavor and reduced FODMAPs, improving the soup’s digestibility. This change expanded its suitability for individuals prone to digestive discomfort.

3. **Elimination of Potato**  
   Removing the potato reduced both glycemic impact and caloric density. The soup became a lighter, lower-carb option, while still offering satisfying texture through careful blending and yogurt addition.

4. **Stock and Salt Revisions**  
   The volume and type of vegetable stock remained, but added salt was significantly reduced. Extra lemon juice and black pepper provided depth and brightness, balancing the flavors despite the lower sodium content.

5. **Boosted Mint and Lemon Juice**  
   I leaned into fresh mint and extra lemon juice, both to intensify the soup’s herbal-citrus profile and to increase the content of phytonutrients and vitamin C. This move enhanced both the nutritional and sensory experience.

6. **Use of Nonfat Skyr**  
   Replacing the whole Greek yogurt with a smaller amount of fat-free Skyr reduced saturated fat, calories, and further raised protein content. The result is a creamy finish with a more favorable nutrient profile.

7. **Enhanced Blending and Chilling**  
   Using an immersion blender produced a finer, more aerated texture. Rapid chilling of the soup over an ice bath minimized microbial growth—an essential safety step for cold soups [7].

8. **Final Presentation**  
   Each bowl received a swirl of Skyr and a garnish of mint and microgreens. Microgreens contributed visual interest and added micronutrients, raising both the nutritional and aesthetic value.

Overall, each adjustment was made to align with health-focused guidelines, without compromising flavor integrity or traditional appeal.

## 4. Sensory Evaluation and Critical Appraisal

### 4.1. Sensory Experience

Upon tasting, the revised soup exhibited a vibrant, pale green color, visibly freshened by the extra mint and the removal of the potato, which had previously dulled the hue. The aroma was markedly herbal and invigorating, with the mint's presence more prominent and the citrus from the lemon coming through cleanly.

The texture felt slightly lighter and less dense—expected after omitting the potato—yet still creamy thanks to the blending technique and Skyr. The flavor profile was balanced: herbaceous, refreshing, with a noticeable brightness from the increased lemon juice, compensating for the lower salt. The change in olive oil quantity was perceptible, with a less intense richness, but didn't detract from the overall harmony. The black pepper, added more assertively, contributed nuance and warmth.

In the mouth, the soup was cooling and refreshing, fitting for summer menus. The dollop of Skyr provided a satisfying, protein-rich creaminess on top, while fresh mint and microgreens elevated the presentation and fresh flavors.

### 4.2. Public Health and Culinary Considerations

From a nutritional standpoint, these modifications resulted in a soup lower in total energy, saturated fat, and sodium, with increased lean protein and phytonutrients. By focusing on strong flavors from herbs and acidity rather than sodium or fat, the soup maintained high palatability, illustrating that flavor need not be sacrificed for healthfulness.

Culinarily, the modifications illustrated a flexible approach to recipe adaptation: thoughtful use of herbs, acid, and modern cooking techniques can reinforce—or even improve—flavor while following dietary recommendations. This adaptation process showcases valuable concepts for use in nutrition education, such as practical methods to reduce salt, saturated fat, and refined carbohydrate content at home, without alienating those accustomed to traditional recipes.

Implementing such changes on a wider scale supports public health aims. Recipes like this one provide accessible templates for balancing taste, tradition, and evidence-driven health concerns, both in formal nutrition teaching and within community outreach programs.

## 6. Conclusions and Application to Nutrition Practice

The step-by-step modification of Lucía’s chilled zucchini and mint soup provides a practical framework for translating nutrition science and public health principles into everyday cooking. Strategic reductions in sodium, saturated fat, and total calories were achieved without compromising the essence or enjoyment of the dish. Leveraging the natural flavors of mint, citrus, and pepper, along with a higher-protein garnishing, resulted in a soup that is both nourishing and flavorful.

This approach demonstrates that health-promoting adaptations do not have to come at the cost of culinary satisfaction or cultural resonance. Such techniques are readily applicable in dietetic practice, nutrition education, and culinary workshops, bridging the longstanding divide between theory and the realities of the home kitchen. This recipe now stands as a compelling, evidence-based case study for using recipe modification as a public health strategy.
