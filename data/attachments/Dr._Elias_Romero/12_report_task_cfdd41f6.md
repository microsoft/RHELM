### 2. Preliminary Data Presentation and Discussion

#### a. Study Design and Data Collection

Dr. Romero summarized the research design. The team conducted a randomized controlled field trial over four weeks within university dining outlets. The sample included 580 students and staff aged 18–65, recruited via stratified sampling to ensure adequate representation by gender, faculty, and self-reported health status.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups:

- **Intervention group:** Exposed to color-coded nutritional labeling (green, yellow, red), which was integrated into food displays to reflect the healthfulness of each item according to established nutrient profiling.
- **Control group:** Received standard black-and-white nutrition labels showing only numerical information, with no color coding.

Choices at the point of sale were recorded and supplemented with anonymized electronic surveys assessing participants’ understanding and recall of nutrition information.

#### b. Participant Demographics

Data collected showed a balanced distribution across several key dimensions:

- **Gender:** 54% female, 46% male  
- **Age:** 38% aged 18–25, 42% aged 26–40, 20% aged 41–65  
- **Faculty Affiliation:** Health Sciences 35%, Humanities 25%, Sciences 22%, Other faculties 18%

These demographics provide a strong sample for evaluating impacts across diverse subgroups.

#### c. Outcomes and Statistical Analysis

The main aim was to assess whether color-coded labels led to a higher proportion of healthy food choices compared with standard labels. The primary outcome measured was the percentage of participants’ selections that featured a green (healthiest) label.

- **Effectiveness of Labels:**  
    - In the intervention group, participants selected healthy (green-labeled) items 57% of the time, versus 41% in the control group—a substantial increase of 16 percentage points (χ² = 14.8, p < 0.001).
    - Logistic regression analyses, adjusting for age, gender, and baseline nutritional knowledge, confirmed the effect: the odds of choosing a healthy option were more than double with color-coded labeling (OR = 2.1, 95% CI: 1.5–2.9, p < 0.001).

- **Nutrition Knowledge and Self-Reported Intentions:**  
    - Surveys administered after menu exposure found knowledge retention scores of 78 out of 100 in the intervention group, compared with 63 in the control group (t(578) = 5.6, p < 0.001).
    - Intention to choose healthier foods also appeared higher in the intervention group, as reflected in the survey feedback.

- **Subgroup Analyses:**  
    - No statistically significant differences were observed according to gender (p = 0.24).
    - A modest trend suggested color-coded labeling was especially effective among the youngest age bracket (18–25), although this did not reach standard significance levels (p = 0.07); further analysis is ongoing.
  
Dr. Romero illustrated the label effect by comparing it to the way traffic lights aid decision-making on the road: a quick visual cue can guide food choices, especially in busy canteen settings where decisions are made rapidly.

#### d. Limitations and Considerations

Several considerations were highlighted. While participant recruitment was robust, voluntary completion of electronic surveys may have introduced some selection bias. More granular analyses by faculty, prior nutritional knowledge, and socio-economic status are still in progress to clarify if the observed effects hold true across subgroups. Collection of open-ended qualitative feedback is ongoing, which should provide richer insight into participants’ experiences and perceptions.

### 3. Policy Implications and Policy Brief Planning

Transitioning from findings to practical recommendations, Dr. Alvarez led a discussion on the policy implications of the data. Both participants agreed that the evidence strongly supports color-coded nutrition labeling as an effective strategy to encourage healthier food choices in university catering environments.

Key points for the policy brief will include:

- A clear summary of the evidence base demonstrating increased selection of healthy items with color-coded labeling.
- Concrete recommendations for adopting color-coded labeling across university and potentially other institutional food outlets.
- Communication strategies targeting stakeholders (e.g., food service managers, students, faculty) to ensure messages are clear, accessible, and resonate with diverse audiences.
- Emphasis on the simplicity and intuitive nature of the labels as a central strength.

Dr. Alvarez volunteered to prepare the initial draft of the policy brief for review at the next meeting.

**Clarifying Note:**  
An effect size (e.g., +16%) here represents the improvement in healthy choices due to the intervention, much like noting how faster traffic moves when signals are clearer. The low p-values (<0.05) confirm that the observed differences are unlikely to be due to random chance.
