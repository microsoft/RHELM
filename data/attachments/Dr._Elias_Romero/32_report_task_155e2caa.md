## Executive Summary

This update synthesizes key findings from my recent review session focusing on food environment disparities in Spain and the wider European Union. Central to the discussion are two recent, influential publications: one by Dr. Ingrid Keller, which highlights the spatial and policy-driven dimensions affecting food access, and another by Dr. Tomasz Nowak, whose analysis delves into the socioeconomic gradients shaping food equity. Both studies employ robust methodologies and provide critical insights directly relevant to current Spanish and EU policy goals.

Dr. Keller's research demonstrates how urban and rural environments diverge in food access, largely influenced by differences in local policy and urban planning. Her geospatial approach uncovers pronounced gaps, especially in fresh food availability, emphasizing the ways in which policy interventions can either mitigate or exacerbate these divides.

In parallel, Dr. Nowak applies multilevel statistical modeling to reveal persistent socioeconomic disparities in access to affordable, healthy food—both in Spain and across the EU. His focus on vulnerable groups such as low-income households, immigrants, and rural residents sheds light on the structural barriers these populations continue to face.

Together, these studies reinforce the urgent need for policy innovation, stronger monitoring systems, and coordinated multisectoral interventions to address the root causes of dietary inequality in Spain. Their findings provide compelling evidence to support integrated public health nutrition strategies that align national and European objectives.

## Comparative Analysis: Research Methodologies and Findings

| Element                | Dr. Ingrid Keller (2024)                                                        | Dr. Tomasz Nowak (2024)                                                        |
|------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Study Design**       | Cross-sectional, mixed-methods; ecological and spatial analysis                  | Quantitative, multilevel regression analysis                                    |
| **Data Sources**       | GIS-based spatial mapping, Spanish National Nutrition Survey, local food outlet registry | Harmonized EU-SILC data, Spanish National Food Retail Data, regional statistics      |
| **Analytical Strategy**| Spatial clustering, regression adjusted for socioeconomic status (SES) and urbanicity  | Multilevel regression with propensity score matching                           |
| **Study Settings**     | Urban, peri-urban, and rural regions across Spain                                 | Selected provinces in Spain and EU countries, with a focus on urban and rural settings |
| **Population**         | 15,500 Spaniards representing diverse SES backgrounds                             | Low-income, immigrant, and rural groups in Spain and the EU                    |
| **Key Results**        | Wide urban-rural differences in fresh food access; policy moderates but does not resolve disparities | Strong association between low SES and both limited access and affordability of healthy food |
| **Main Recommendations** | Regional adaptation of policies; integrated urban planning for food equity      | Targeted interventions to enhance affordability; EU-level harmonization and benchmarking |

This comparative overview highlights the complementary strengths of each study. Dr. Keller's spatial mapping provides a nuanced understanding of geographic disparities, while Dr. Nowak's multilevel modeling reveals how socioeconomic factors intersect with these spatial divides. Both underscore that while some progress has been made through policy, significant gaps remain—particularly for Spain’s most vulnerable groups.

## Project Notes: Synthesis, Critical Appraisal, and Research Gaps

Both studies distinguish themselves by employing comprehensive, high-quality data sets, ensuring findings are robust and generalizable not only for Spain but also for similar contexts across the EU. The integration of advanced quantitative and geospatial analysis techniques allows for a precise diagnosis of both geographic and socioeconomic determinants of healthy food access.

However, the cross-sectional design common to both studies limits our ability to draw conclusions about causality or how disparities evolve over time. This is particularly pertinent given that food environments—and the policies governing them—are dynamic and subject to change.

Underrepresentation of deeply rural areas and the most marginalized populations, including certain ethnic minorities and recent migrants, remains a notable limitation. These groups often face the greatest barriers, which means current estimates of inequality may not capture the full extent of the problem.

Potential sources of bias also persist. The completeness of retail outlet registries, for instance, can impact GIS analyses, while unmeasured variables such as household food literacy or reliance on informal food markets can confound observed outcomes. There is also an evident gap in the literature when it comes to assessing how these disparities affect children, the elderly, and recently arrived migrants—groups that often have unique needs and challenges.

Both Dr. Keller and Dr. Nowak underscore the growing necessity for longitudinal studies and natural experiment designs, which would allow us to evaluate how policy changes affect food environments and population health over time. They also point out the alignment of their findings with broader strategic frameworks, such as the EU’s Farm to Fork strategy and Spain's national food security policies. Despite this strategic alignment, the translation of evidence into tangible policy improvements has been uneven, with disadvantaged regions often lagging behind.

## Action Items: Recommended Next Steps

1. **Systematic Review of Longitudinal Evidence**  
   I plan to initiate a comprehensive review focused on longitudinal and experimental studies of food environment interventions in Spain and the EU. This effort aims to strengthen causal understanding, clarify which interventions achieve tangible improvements, and support evidence-based policy recommendations.

2. **Pilot Natural Experiments**  
   It is crucial to design and closely monitor natural experiments—for example, by tracking dietary outcomes after the opening of new supermarkets or the closure of traditional markets in diverse regions. These pilots will help us understand real-world impacts and build the case for scaling successful interventions.

3. **Enhance Data Collection and Mapping**  
   I will collaborate with data scientists to expand GIS analyses using open-source and crowd-sourced mapping data. This should increase the precision and coverage of local food environment assessments, especially in rural and peri-urban areas that are often overlooked in national surveys.

4. **Broaden Population Focus**  
   Going forward, dedicated sub-analyses will address the needs of groups that are underrepresented in existing research—specifically, children, the elderly, and migrant communities. Understanding the distinct barriers these populations encounter is essential for targeted policy design.

5. **Engage in Policy Partnerships**  
   Continued collaboration with public authorities at local and national levels is essential. Joint efforts will focus on adapting and implementing the key recommendations from these studies within current policy frameworks, while building in mechanisms for ongoing evaluation and learning.

6. **Benchmark Progress Against EU Standards**  
   Regular benchmarking against other EU countries using harmonized, transparent indicators will help track Spain’s progress, identify transferable best practices, and highlight areas that require additional attention or investment.

7. **Close Data and Surveillance Gaps**  
   Lastly, I will advocate for stronger, more flexible surveillance systems at the national and regional levels. These systems should track not only changes in the food environment but also the resulting health outcomes—ensuring that progress is measurable and that policy adaptation remains responsive to evolving challenges.
