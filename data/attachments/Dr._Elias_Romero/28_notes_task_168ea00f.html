1. Structured Summary: Advancements in Community Mapping and Engagement

Following the project meeting on July 27, 2024, our approach was significantly strengthened through the implementation of revised methodologies and strategic priorities, informed by Javier’s thorough feedback. These enhancements are in line with leading global standards for community-engaged public health research, firmly centering participatory practices, detailed socio-demographic analysis, and robust collaboration with neighborhood associations throughout Barcelona.

Enhanced Community Mapping Strategies

  • Integrated Geo-Socio-Demographic Mapping:
    The project’s mapping process has evolved to include layered geographic and socio-demographic data, enhancing our understanding of targeted neighborhoods such as Sants, El Raval, and Sant Andreu. By merging open municipal datasets with insights from localized focus groups, we now capture nuanced elements—age distributions, ethnicity, household income, and food insecurity indicators—that shape food environments. This integration allows for more precise identification of community needs and assets, reflecting guidance from the WHO’s urban food environments policy brief (WHO, 2023).

  • Participatory GIS Workshops:
    We have introduced mapping workshops where community members collaborate directly with researchers to chart local nutrition resources, barriers, and opportunities. Co-facilitation by local association representatives and university staff has already helped foster trust and improved both the relevance and quality of collected data. This practice is guided by the International Association for Public Participation’s standards for meaningful public engagement (IAP2, 2022), ensuring that community voices are central in shaping our understanding.

  • Shift to Asset-Based Community Development (ABCD):
    Responding to Javier’s recommendation, our analysis now places greater emphasis on identifying and harnessing existing nutrition-related assets—such as community kitchens, youth groups, and women’s associations—rather than focusing solely on deficits. This asset-oriented perspective aligns with trends in Spanish and broader European public health research and has proven effective in building sustained capacity and motivation among community stakeholders (Gaceta Sanitaria, 2023).

Insights from Neighborhood Associations

Through ongoing engagement with neighborhood associations, several key patterns have emerged:

  • Sants:
    The neighborhood’s significant migrant population has expressed a clear preference for peer-led healthy eating initiatives. Their feedback highlights the importance of culturally relevant outreach and building on existing informal support networks.

  • El Raval:
    Elevated levels of child food insecurity have come to the forefront. The local Islamic women’s association has shown strong interest in co-creating nutrition interventions that honor cultural dietary preferences and family structures.

  • Sant Andreu:
    With active senior centers and high intergenerational interaction, there is substantial readiness to collaborate on nutrition education programs that bridge younger and older residents.

  • Strengthening Engagement Mechanisms:
    To maintain momentum and transparency, regular participatory forums have been instituted, complemented by quarterly meetings—both in person and virtually—with association leaders. These feedback loops now form a core component of our process, reflecting the participatory action research (PAR) model (Cargo & Mercer, 2008), and supporting shared decision-making at all stages.

3. Upcoming Priorities: Methods, Partnerships, and Ethics

Advancing Methodological Rigor

  • Finalization of a participatory GIS mapping toolkit is underway, incorporating guidance from neighborhood associations to ensure accessibility and relevance for all stakeholders.
  • A pilot phase for asset-based mapping sessions will specifically focus on communities historically underrepresented in public health research, improving equity of participation.
  • The research team is incorporating structured reflexive practice, maintaining detailed logs to systematically review power dynamics and ensure equitable participation throughout the project cycle.

Strengthening Community Partnerships

  • Recruitment of resident “nutrition ambassadors” is moving forward, with these individuals to play pivotal roles in co-facilitating workshops and community events, enhancing peer-led capacity.
  • Formalizing collaboration through Memoranda of Understanding (MoUs) with at least three neighborhood associations ensures sustained and clearly defined partnerships.
  • Plans are in place to facilitate exchange visits between neighborhoods, giving local leaders direct opportunities to observe and learn from nutrition interventions in different contexts.

Upholding Ethical Standards

  • Consent protocols are being revised in close collaboration with multilingual community representatives to ensure clarity, inclusiveness, and cultural appropriateness.
  • New data stewardship guidelines, co-developed with participants, will protect privacy and reinforce community data sovereignty, consistent with the Spanish Data Protection Law.
  • Internal review meetings will be conducted after each major data collection phase, providing regular assessment points for identifying and proactively addressing ethical challenges.

All adaptations are aligned with current international and Spanish standards on participatory community research (WHO, 2023; Gaceta Sanitaria, 2023), ensuring both methodological integrity and participant protection.

4. Reflective Synthesis: Strategic Pathway, Challenges, and Anticipated Impacts

Strategic Direction

The project is now firmly oriented towards establishing lasting, participatory action at the neighborhood level. Our model is structured around power-sharing between university researchers and community organizations, fostering mutual accountability and ownership. By investing in asset-based strategies, we aim to reinforce community identity and reduce the risk of participation fatigue. The iterative nature of our process ensures interventions remain relevant and responsive to community feedback.

Key Challenges

  • Balancing Diverse Priorities:
    Neighborhood associations each bring distinct priorities, requiring ongoing dialog and flexibility to align project objectives and approaches.

  • Maintaining Engagement:
    There is a risk of participation fatigue, particularly if project milestones require extensive community input. To address this, we are phasing activities, recognizing community contributions, and ensuring clear communication of each step’s value.

  • Managing Data and Ethical Complexities:
    Maintaining robust informed consent, protecting privacy, and respecting data ownership are increasingly complex in participatory frameworks. Regular training, transparent protocols, and collaborative oversight will be critical to address these evolving ethical dimensions.

Anticipated Public Health Outcomes

  • Development of practical, adaptable models for community-driven nutrition engagement, suitable for implementation in other urban areas.
  • Strengthening of local advocacy networks and enhanced community capacity to sustain healthy food environment initiatives.
  • Provision of robust, community-informed evidence that can inform and shape municipal nutrition policy.

Alignment with Best Practice

These changes reinforce alignment with global standards in community engagement and participatory research, as detailed in recent publications from Cargo & Mercer (2008) and IAP2 (2022). Javier’s contributions have directly bolstered our methodological rigor and enhanced the potential for lasting, equity-focused public health impact.
