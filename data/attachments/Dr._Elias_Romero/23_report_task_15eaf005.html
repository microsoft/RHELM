Discussion Summary

1. Progress Update: Cross-European Urban Nutrition & Public Health Study

Recruitment and Data Collection:
We have successfully completed recruitment of a diverse, multi-site adult cohort (N=4,200), evenly distributed across Barcelona, Amsterdam, Warsaw, and Vienna. All phases of dietary data collection—comprising 7-day food diaries and standardized Food Frequency Questionnaires—are finished. These assessments captured both dietary diversity and detailed micronutrient profiles.

Environmental and Biological Assessment:
Each participant’s home location has been geo-coded to assess proximity to green spaces, walkability, and accessibility of healthy food outlets. Biospecimen collection for a targeted subsample is over 85% complete, enabling analysis of nutritional status and cardiometabolic markers.

Methodological Innovations:
The study combines quantitative surveys, objective biosampling, and comprehensive GIS mapping. In addition, qualitative interviews across demographic groups have provided richer insights into lived experiences of food access and urban environmental wellbeing. We piloted wearables for four weeks in two cities to objectively monitor participants’ physical activity and sleep. Environmental exposure data is being analyzed using NDVI (satellite-based vegetation indices) and ground-level air pollution metrics.

Preliminary Findings:
Participants who regularly use urban green spaces report higher dietary diversity, more frequent fruit and vegetable intake, and lower consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages. Strong associations are evident between mental health status (measured by GHQ-12) and frequency of green space engagement. Notably, frequent green space users (controlling for income, education, and local walkability) tend to have lower mean BMIs. Patterns of fresh produce consumption vary by city, and correlate with both geographical proximity to markets and perceived neighborhood safety. Manuscript writing is underway as data analysis continues.

Public Health Implications:
The emerging evidence supports policy efforts aimed at promoting equitable access to green spaces in urban planning. The findings also point to the necessity for integrated urban strategies that unite environmental design, nutrition policy, and mental health promotion. The cross-city comparative approach is proving valuable for revealing context-specific factors influencing urban health outcomes.

2. Personal Experience: Marathon Training – Lessons for Human Performance and Public Health

Training Journeys:
We shared our experiences from recent marathon training rounds in spring 2024. I averaged about 60 km per week, preparing for a full marathon, while my colleague focused on the half marathon with a weekly average of 35 km. We both confronted typical challenges: balancing job demands, tailoring nutrition for the demands of long-distance running, and ensuring adequate recovery—all within the constraints of a bustling urban environment.

Interplay with Urban Setting:
We both found that quality and accessibility of local green corridors and parks made a significant difference to the consistency and enjoyment of our training. Air quality and safe, well-maintained pedestrian routes were key for keeping up motivation, especially on long runs or after stressful workdays. Running alongside others—either formally through clubs or informally—enhanced our adherence; the sense of social support was palpable and directly impacted our ability to sustain our training programs.

Link to Broader Public Health Themes:
Reflecting on our experiences, we discussed how thoughtfully designed green infrastructures not only support physical activity but foster broader benefits: stress relief, community building, and even heightened awareness of healthy eating habits (for example, the draw toward nutritious recovery meals post-run). Our discussion reinforced the idea that “active populations” could serve both as models for urban health initiatives and as priority groups for targeted intervention. We proposed ramping up wearable sensor usage—adding GPS, accelerometry, and HRV measures—to better capture real-time patterns in physical activity and stress among both general and specialized urban populations.

3. Collaborative Brainstorming: Future Research Ideas

Project Vision:
We aim to develop a comprehensive research initiative examining how urban green space exposure interacts with dietary behaviors, physical activity, social environment, and mental health in diverse urban populations.

Key Hypotheses: 1. Regular engagement with green spaces independently and synergistically improves dietary quality and psychosocial wellbeing. 2. Social cohesion and perceptions of neighborhood safety mediate the relationship between green space usage and health behaviors. 3. Community-based interventions combining green space access, nutrition, and exercise foster meaningful improvements in both mental and metabolic health.

Research Questions:
- How does access to green space influence dietary patterns and mental wellbeing among city residents? - What specific activities, frequencies, or types of green space usage yield the most significant benefits for nutrition and psychosocial health? - How do social neighborhood characteristics and the quality of green spaces shape these relationships? - Can tailored, multi-component interventions deliver lasting changes in behavior and measurable improvements in health outcomes?

Methodological Approach:
Our proposed design is multi-level and mixed-methods: - Longitudinal quantitative tracking (potentially quasi-experimental), with repeated assessment of physical activity (wearables), dietary quality (FFQs/food diaries), mental health (tools like PHQ-9 and GHQ-12), and social constructs (e.g., social cohesion, perceived stress) - GIS and remote sensing to objectively quantify green space access - Qualitative focus groups and key informant interviews to capture neighborhood-specific barriers and cultural norms around healthy eating and recreation - Citizen science components, such as participatory well-being mapping and lived experience diaries

Pilot and Partnerships:
We agreed to initiate the pilot in Barcelona, focusing on the Jardins de Joan Brossa catchment area. Expansion to two more European cities is envisioned as the project develops. Early, close collaboration with municipal authorities, local health agencies, and established community networks is essential. Engaging policymakers from the start will be critical for uptake and impact.
