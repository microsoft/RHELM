## 1. Morning Routine: Balcony Yoga and Reflections

### Yoga Session

This morning began, as many do, with yoga on my balcony—six stories above Carrer d’Aragó. At 7:00 a.m., I rolled out my mat to the soft, early light and the hum of the city still just a whisper below. I structured the session around familiar sequences: five rounds of Sun Salutations over 15 minutes, followed by three sets of Warrior poses per side. I flowed through Mountain Pose, deep bends, and the strength of Warrior II, feeling my pulse rise most during that posture (Garmin recorded a peak heart rate of 128 bpm). The cool-down—seated forward folds, gentle twists, and finally Savasana—helped me ease into the day, both physically and mentally.

According to my Vivosmart 5, the 35-minute practice counted 1,360 steps and burned 127 calories, reflecting the dynamic transitions I’ve built into my routine. My average heart rate hovered around 99 bpm; the low–moderate intensity and a logged stress score of 23 align with what I felt: focused but calm, slightly sweaty from the post-dawn humidity. Living in Barcelona, I’m always mindful of the air quality, especially when exercising outdoors. Today, with an AQI of 45 and the temperature at a comfortable 24°C, the air felt clean and my breathwork was noticeably smoother compared with indoor sessions. The only real disruption was the subtle increase in traffic noise around 7:30, but by then, the meditative effects had taken hold.

Yoga on the balcony is my personal method for merging movement with mindfulness—core recommendations not only from WHO but from my own research into cardiovascular and mental health. For urban professionals juggling time constraints, these short, accessible sessions demonstrate how physical activity can be seamlessly integrated even in dense city environments. Tracking my environmental context each day helps reinforce the interplay between the urban setting, air quality, and exercise enjoyment—reminding me of what’s at stake, both personally and in my public health advocacy.

## 2. Lunch: Mediterranean Farro Salad and Sustainable Choices

### Meal Experience

By midday, I was ready for something fresh and hearty. Lunch today was a Mediterranean farro salad—a dish I’ve come to rely on for nourishment and satiety without any heaviness. Around 1:15 p.m., I combined cooked farro (sourced in the morning from the local Mercat de la Concepció) with juicy cherry tomatoes, crisp cucumber, tangy Kalamata olives, creamy feta, slivers of red onion, plenty of fresh parsley, and a dressing of robust extra virgin olive oil and bright lemon juice. I measure portions loosely but for the sake of my records, kept today’s meal to approximately 445 kcal and a healthy macronutrient balance: 13.4g protein, 60g carbs (with 8.4g fiber), and 15g fat. The crunchy vegetables and salty olives gave the salad real texture and depth, while the lemon and parsley brightened every bite.

As I assembled lunch, I thought about sustainability and food miles. Sourcing most ingredients locally—with farro, tomatoes, olive oil, and parsley picked up in bulk form with reusable bags—has become second nature. The olives and feta came in glass containers, easy to recycle, and the produce was from the ecological stall specializing in small-scale Catalan farms (most less than 45 kilometers from Barcelona). Whenever I prepare these kinds of meals, I’m reminded just how closely personal nutrition choices are tied to larger issues: the Mediterranean diet isn’t just scientifically proven to reduce risk factors for cardiovascular and metabolic disease [1], it also models a sustainable, plant-based eating pattern. Choosing locally grown produce and minimizing packaging are small daily actions, but they shape both my health and that of the wider environment.

## 3. Work Activities: Collaboration, Resilience, and Research Development

### Morning Meeting and Teamwork

Back at the University, the day’s main professional focus was our departmental meeting from 10:00 to 11:30 with colleagues from nutrition, epidemiology, and behavioral science. We’re building a cross-departmental grant proposal on “Urban Micro-environments & Dietary Choices”—work that sits at the intersection of my interests in urban health and dietary behavior. Reviewing our latest internal publication on Mediterranean diet adherence highlighted just how varied eating patterns can become, even within a single city like Barcelona.

I noticed a productive rhythm in our team today. We mapped out next steps: divvying up literature reviews, outlining a draft, and developing a strategy for engaging local neighborhoods (this time, focusing on the Raval district, where dietary exposures are highly variable). There was a genuine sense of collaboration—everyone eager to contribute and quick to adapt when obstacles arose.

### Navigating Digital Disruption

At 11:05, our Wi-Fi suddenly cut out, leaving us without digital connectivity for a full 25 minutes. Initially, I felt the familiar tailspin—so many of us depend on laptops and shared documents. But instead, we pivoted: whiteboards, colored markers, notebooks, and energetic discussion filled the void. One colleague took handwritten minutes for later upload. The forced screen-free session actually sparked more creativity and lively participation than usual. Reflecting afterward, I realized how resilient academic teams can be when they let go of technological crutches—sometimes, improvisation brings out the best in us.

### Research Hypotheses and Problem-Solving

After lunch, I led a focused whiteboard session exploring the question: How does living near major roads affect adolescents’ dietary behaviors in Barcelona? My own hypothesis is that proximity to busy streets limits access to fresh market foods while increasing exposure to highly processed options. I think neighborhood walkability might buffer these effects—teens in more pedestrian-friendly areas may visit local shops more often, countering any negative influence from traffic or pollution. We mapped out variables, mind-mapped relationships between shop density, pollution levels, and socioeconomic status, and started sketching the data collection plan for our upcoming fieldwork.

## 4. Social Interactions: Coffee with Carla and Community Engagement

This afternoon, I took a brief break from the academic pace to meet Carla Hernández, a dedicated graduate student in our public health MSc program. At 3:30, we grabbed coffee at Cafetería El Magnífico on Carrer de l’Argenteria—a favorite spot for its strong brews and understated Catalan design.

We both ordered V60 pour-overs of “La Fábrica,” a medium-dark roast combining Colombian and Ethiopian Arabica with Ugandan Robusta. The cups—handmade ceramics—were warm in my hands and their simple, unadorned style always reminds me to slow down. Over coffee, Carla shared updates on her project “#SanoEnElBarrio,” an outreach initiative using social media to promote nutrition awareness among adolescents. We discussed the challenges and opportunities of engaging youth online versus in person, and came up with a plan to pilot a series of eye-catching nutrition posters in the Gràcia and Poblenou neighborhoods, using local cafés and schools as informal community touchpoints.

These conversations reinforce my belief that health education has to move beyond clinics and classrooms—Barcelona’s urban cafés, with their intergenerational clientele and welcoming atmosphere, truly do have the potential to serve as grassroots health hubs. Supporting students like Carla through mentorship not only advances their projects but keeps me energized and connected to the community impact of our work.

## 5. Evening Walk: Urban Exploration and Advocacy

### Walk and Observations

At 7:05 p.m., with the worst of the day’s heat subsiding, I set out for an evening walk through Gràcia. My route wound from Plaça de la Virreina along Carrer de Verdi, circling back through Plaça de la Revolució. The air was still warm (27°C), but a mild breeze and a clean AQI of 42 made it comfortable. My Vivosmart logged 6,940 steps over 55 minutes; pace was leisurely, but I pushed harder up the gentle incline near Rambla del Prat, bringing my heart rate to 120 bpm at the peak.

As I wandered, I lingered to photograph vibrant new street murals celebrating local produce—art that will be perfect for my upcoming visual campaign on “Healthy Eating, Healthy Streets.” I passed a few community garden plots bursting with late-summer greenery and encountered a couple of pop-up health stalls distributing “Healthy Streets” pamphlets. There was a comfortable, communal energy along the streets: neighbors chatting about recycling, small children darting between market stalls, and signs everywhere of active, health-focused local networks. I jotted down notes in my notebook—plans to approach city council about further pedestrianizing the streets around key food markets, thoughts on mapping a “Healthy Route” for future behavior research, and contact points for the food co-ops discussed by residents.

This type of evening walk does more than close a move ring or tick off a step goal—it sharpens my perspective on urban nutrition environments, collects qualitative data for ongoing projects, and deepens my sense of connection to place. I always return home energized and with a handful of new ideas for research and advocacy.

## 7. Integrated Reflections: Linking Daily Practice to Public Health

Looking back over the day, I’m struck by how much my personal habits mirror the principles I try to instill in students and collaborators. Physical activity was woven naturally into my schedule—yoga in the morning, active commuting, purposeful walking at lunch and in the evening—well above national and WHO recommendations for daily movement. My lunchtime salad wasn’t just nutritious; it was also a tangible commitment to local sourcing and a lower-carbon footprint. I’m always conscious that these individual choices create ripple effects, especially when shared or observed by colleagues and students.

Work culture, too, is an area I’m passionate about improving. Our team’s ability to adapt during the Wi-Fi outage illustrated a resilience that academic environments desperately need. The day also reinforced for me the unique power of community—cafés, gardens, markets, and informal spaces to create momentum around health, food, and social connection.

I try to keep environmental awareness front and center: tracking air quality before exercising, advocating for walkable markets and pedestrian zones, and using city walks for both relaxation and research inspiration. The photographs and notes from today’s stroll will feed directly into future participatory projects, ensuring that my research remains rooted in lived urban experience.

Each element of this log stands both as a record of daily routines and as a practical, lived exemplar of public health principles in action. The choices I make for my own wellbeing reflect—and strengthen—the evidence we promote for healthier, more inclusive, and more sustainable urban lives.
