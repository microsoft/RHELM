## Introduction

This report presents an in-depth review and critical analysis of three peer-reviewed articles published between 2022 and 2024, each addressing food environment interventions within Europe. The primary focus is on the design and methodologies used, the effectiveness and measurable outcomes of these interventions, and their implications and potential for policy translation—especially in relation to ongoing nutrition policy efforts in Spain. These findings contribute directly to departmental priorities, inform future research directions, and support alignment with broader European agendas aimed at improving food environments and public health nutrition.

## Article 1: Healthy Food Availability and Purchasing in European Urban Environments

### Annotation and Critical Reflection

**Design and Methodology:**  
Garcia et al. conducted a robust, quasi-experimental study spanning five urban European cities. The intervention incorporated a range of supermarket-based strategies, including shelf labeling, strategic product repositioning, and well-designed point-of-sale prompts. Data collection relied on in-store purchase records and consumer surveys both before and after implementation, allowing for a comprehensive pre-post evaluation.

**Key Outcomes:**  
Following the intervention, sales of fresh fruits, vegetables, and wholegrain bread increased by 7–11% compared to the control sites. Notably, the effect was most pronounced in neighborhoods with lower socioeconomic status, suggesting the intervention also helped address health equity. Survey responses indicated modest improvements in customers’ knowledge about healthy eating and reported intentions to choose healthier options.

**Theoretical Framework:**  
The intervention was underpinned by socio-ecological and behavioral economics models, which emphasize nudging shoppers toward better choices at the point of sale while preserving individual autonomy.

**Strengths:**  
Several strengths stand out: the real-world, multi-country design enhances external validity; the analysis accounts for socioeconomic factors, highlighting possible reductions in nutritional disparities; and the use of objectively measured sales data reduces reporting bias.

**Limitations:**  
However, the study’s six-month intervention and follow-up window was relatively short, limiting assessment of long-term or sustained behavior change. The simultaneous presence of national nutrition campaigns could also have introduced confounding variables.

**Relevance and Adaptation Potential for Spain:**  
Given the similar retail environments and consumer profiles in Spanish urban areas, applying these interventions in Spain appears highly feasible. The focus on equity and improvement among more disadvantaged populations dovetails with national priorities addressing urban health disparities.

## Article 2: Implementation of Nutri-Score Labelling in School Food Settings

### Annotation and Critical Reflection

**Design and Methodology:**  
López et al. employed a natural experiment design involving 42 schools in Spain and France that voluntarily implemented the Nutri-Score front-of-pack labeling system in their cafeterias. The evaluation incorporated a combination of sales data, student surveys, and focus group discussions with students and cafeteria managers, facilitating a well-rounded understanding of both outcomes and process factors.

**Key Outcomes:**  
In the immediate aftermath of implementing Nutri-Score labels, sales of healthier items (those graded “A” or “B”) rose by 21%, while purchases of unhealthy (“E” graded) foods declined by 14%. Both nutrition knowledge and self-reported healthy eating intentions improved among students. Additionally, interviews suggested that cafeteria managers found it easier to promote healthy foods, citing positive feedback from both staff and students.

**Theoretical Framework:**  
The intervention design drew on dual-process theory, which posits that making nutrition information visually accessible and easy to interpret helps guide healthier food choices, especially in youth populations where food decisions can be impulsive.

**Strengths:**  
This study is directly relevant to current Spanish policy discussions about the national adoption of Nutri-Score. The multi-method approach yields both quantitative outcomes and qualitative insights, and the real-world school setting enhances ecological validity.

**Limitations:**  
At this stage, available data only covers early outcomes; further research is needed to assess long-term behavior and actual consumption rather than sales alone. Because participation was voluntary, schools that opted in could have been more health-conscious already, introducing possible selection bias.

**Relevance and Adaptation Potential for Spain:**  
With ongoing debates and pilot projects around Nutri-Score in Spain, these findings are timely and highly applicable. The positive feedback and measurable shifts in purchasing indicate strong potential for national scale-up in school settings, but longitudinal evaluation will be critical to gauge sustained impact and broader student health outcomes.

## Article 3: Fiscal Policies to Improve Food Purchases: Real-World Evaluation of the Soft Drinks Tax in Catalonia

### Annotation and Critical Reflection

**Design and Methodology:**  
This article by Sánchez et al. examined the impact of Catalonia’s sugary drinks tax through an interrupted time series analysis. Researchers analyzed both purchasing and consumption data before and after the tax’s introduction, using other Spanish regions without the tax as comparators.

**Key Outcomes:**  
In the tax’s first year, sales of taxed sugary beverages declined by 14%. There was no concurrent rise in purchases of untaxed substitutes such as diet drinks or alternative sweetened beverages, and energy intake among adolescents in taxed regions showed early signs of reduction.

**Theoretical Framework:**  
The tax leverages economic theory (specifically, price elasticity of demand) to influence consumer purchasing at the population level, aiming to shift behavior through pricing rather than direct education or restriction.

**Strengths:**  
The study stands out for its real-world policy relevance, rigorous use of regional data, and clear demonstration of measurable behavior change. Inclusion of adolescent consumption as an outcome is particularly valuable for understanding youth health impacts.

**Limitations:**  
Some important questions remain: there is not yet evidence on longer-term substitution effects or direct health indicators such as BMI or disease measures. As a single-region study, there may be regional factors that limit generalizability, though the findings are highly pertinent for Spain.

**Relevance and Adaptation Potential for Spain:**  
Given the clear reduction in sugary drink purchases and the absence of significant substitution, this evidence provides compelling support for considering similar fiscal policies in other Spanish regions or nationwide. The dataset and study design serve as strong models for evaluating such measures elsewhere.

## Synthesis of Cross-Article Themes and Methodological Lessons

Taken together, these three studies chart the growing sophistication and effectiveness of food environment interventions in the European context. By deploying economic, informational, and behavioral strategies, each intervention achieved measurable improvements in food purchasing behaviors within a relatively short timeframe. Importantly, the use of robust quasi-experimental or natural experiment designs, often bolstered by mixed quantitative and qualitative data, reinforces the policy relevance and practical value of these findings.

Common threads run through the interventions: success comes from multi-level approaches that combine environmental changes, clear information, and fiscal levers to produce more sustainable dietary shifts. The studies also underscore the critical importance of cross-sector partnerships, integrating efforts among public health authorities, schools, retailers, and policymakers.

Addressing equity remains essential—by prioritizing interventions in lower socioeconomic areas and monitoring differential impacts, these approaches help reduce health disparities. Ultimately, real-world evaluation, especially through linkage with administrative datasets, allows for more precise monitoring, scalability, and policymaker buy-in.

Nonetheless, the studies share certain limitations: most outcomes captured are short-term or intermediate. Future research efforts should prioritize long-term follow-up and direct measures of health status, such as BMI, chronic disease rates, and sustained changes in dietary intake.

## Next Steps and Research Priorities

Drawing on the insights from these articles and their relevance to the Spanish context, the following next steps are recommended for departmental activities and research collaborations:

- **Pilot supermarket interventions:** Initiate a targeted pilot of product placement and signage strategies in urban supermarkets, with specific focus on low-income neighborhoods in Catalonia. Establish rigorous pre-post evaluation mechanisms to track sales and consumer perceptions.

- **Expand and deepen evaluation of Nutri-Score in schools:** Develop and implement a longitudinal evaluation protocol for current and future Nutri-Score school canteen projects, emphasizing not just sales but also actual consumption, knowledge retention, and trajectories in student health outcomes over time.

- **Advance fiscal policy research:** Pursue multi-regional grant proposals—potentially in partnership with national and EU bodies—to evaluate the expansion of sugary drink taxes. Future studies should include prospective tracking of chronic disease incidence and careful monitoring of possible substitution effects.

- **Strengthen cross-sector collaborations:** Foster ongoing partnerships with education, retail, and public policy stakeholders to ensure successful implementation and scaling of food environment interventions, as well as shared learning about challenges and enablers.

- **Enhance knowledge translation:** Refresh departmental evidence mapping tools and develop new infographics and targeted communication materials to effectively disseminate these findings to both policy audiences and the broader public.

- **Internal knowledge integration:** Organize an internal seminar series to synthesize these new findings with existing departmental projects, aligning future funding applications (e.g., Horizon Europe, Spanish Institute for Health Research) and refining departmental research agendas accordingly.
