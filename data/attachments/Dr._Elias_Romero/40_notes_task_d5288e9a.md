# Dusk Walk Reflections – September 1, 2024

## Context and Setting

**Date and Time:** September 1, 2024, 19:45–20:30 CET  
**Location:** Barceloneta Beach, Barcelona, Spain

Tonight, I found myself walking alone along the shoreline of Barceloneta Beach, just after receiving news that my latest grant application—for the “Urban Nutrition and Community Health Cohort”—had been turned down. The sun was melting into the horizon, casting a warm, fading glow over the Mediterranean, while the sounds of the city softened into the background, leaving mainly the rhythmic hush of gentle waves. The disappointment was immediate—a heavy sensation settling in my chest—but the calm, open space of the beach gave me room to process and reflect on both the loss and what it revealed about my role as a researcher and leader in nutritional epidemiology.

## Reflections

### Introspective Thoughts and Emotional Responses

The initial shock of rejection sparked a brief surge of frustration and self-doubt. I’ve noticed this is a nearly universal experience among researchers—something I’ve felt before, but each time there’s a sting. Still, I’ve learned that allowing myself to acknowledge those feelings rather than rushing to ignore them actually makes it easier to move forward. As studies show, setbacks in academia can lead to deeper growth if approached with openness and structured reflection [1].

As I continued my walk, I started reframing the experience. Rather than seeing the rejection as a failure, I reminded myself that every unsuccessful application teaches me something new. In fact, previous setbacks have often forced me to clarify my objectives, refine my project design, and push me to seek out unexamined angles—all of which tend to make my subsequent proposals stronger [2]. The act of walking itself helped clear my head; there’s compelling evidence that physical activity and mindful solitude aid in emotional regulation and creative thinking, particularly after stressful events [3].

### Leadership Insights in Academic Research

Navigating setbacks is central to effective research leadership. In my own team, I believe in communicating candidly when things don’t go as planned. Admitting disappointment, while also modeling resilience and pragmatic optimism, helps foster both trust and cohesion. It’s essential that junior researchers see authentic responses—not just the polished surface—so they learn that failure is an expected part of scientific progress, not something to be concealed or stigmatized.

Today’s experience reminded me to return to some core principles in mentorship. Senior colleagues—and myself included—have a responsibility to guide younger members through these moments, not just by sharing coping strategies but by actively involving them in conversations about how to learn from obstacles. This collective approach strengthens our research culture and builds a shared understanding that resilience is learned and practiced.

I’m also reminded how crucial it is to adapt research methodologies to changing local realities. Barcelona’s urban landscape is constantly shifting, with new health challenges emerging almost monthly. Ensuring our nutrition research remains relevant means staying connected to communities, monitoring local trends, and being willing to reshape project goals as needed.

### Professional Insights in Nutritional Epidemiology

Grant volatility is an ongoing challenge. Funding gaps don’t just halt projects—they can destabilize team morale and interrupt vital collaborations with community partners. Reflecting on tonight’s setback made me think seriously about building more robust contingency plans within my research group and advocating for more flexible institutional policies to weather these inevitable interruptions.

Transparency in science is something I’ve tried to champion—sharing not only breakthroughs, but also struggles and failures. Publicly discussing rejections removes the isolation researchers often feel and helps destigmatize setbacks across the field. Open communication strengthens collaborations and opens doors for more robust interdisciplinary partnerships, both locally and internationally.

Moving forward, I see the importance of involving a wider circle of stakeholders—from municipal health authorities to immigrant advocacy groups and inter-university research networks—in future projects and funding proposals. Building these relationships not only deepens the quality of our research, but also broadens its impact within diverse urban communities.
