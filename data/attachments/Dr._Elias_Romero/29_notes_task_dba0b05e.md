## 3. Narrative Log and Methodological Notes

### Tamales

Handwritten notes from Lucía were on hand for tamales, but I noticed a discrepancy in the quantity of salt listed—her handwritten instruction differed from what I’d previously entered in the digital collection. To resolve this, I went back through older digital records, compared them directly to the handwritten page, and also messaged Lucía to clarify her current preference. I updated the digital record, noting the provenance event in the metadata so future users can see how and why that value changed. The record for tamales is now robust: the issue was resolved transparently and any reviewer can trace the update's rationale.

### Flan

Updating the flan recipe was more problematic because I had misplaced Lucía’s handwritten notes from our last call. The critical missing detail was the oven temperature—a key factor for both food safety and texture. Without the primary source, I had to rely on what I recalled from past conversations with Lucía, cross-referencing with digital photos of successful results to approximate the method as closely as possible. I was careful to mark in the recipe that the source was incomplete, and I’ve let the family know we need to confirm this step with Lucía the next time we talk. This incomplete documentation introduces some risk to accuracy and reproducibility, so I’ve clearly flagged the entry for further review.

### Empanadas

With empanadas, I did have some handwritten notes, but the section on seasoning was vague. We recently experienced an underseasoned batch, and I realized this change hadn’t been documented—no one wrote down when or why the spice ratios shifted. The lack of a written record here creates ambiguity about what’s authentic and most reliable for future uses. I annotated the entry as uncertain and plan to revisit this with Lucía and the rest of the family, but for now, the data integrity is only low to moderate due to these gaps.

### Arroz con Pollo

For arroz con pollo, all handwritten notes were available and clear. The only problem was a minor digital transcription error—I’d omitted the accent over “Arroz.” I fixed the oversight to restore linguistic accuracy, and after a careful review, everything else matched perfectly with Lucía’s instructions. This update is now complete and can be trusted for future reference or sharing.

## 4. Effects of Missing or Incomplete Notes on Recipe Updates

### Accuracy

Missing or incomplete notes have a direct, negative effect on how precisely recipes can be updated:

- In Flan’s case, lacking the oven temperature forced me to depend on my memory and supporting digital photos, which introduces risks—both in terms of food safety and the final product’s quality.
- Empanadas highlighted how quickly small changes can be lost to time if not properly documented. When the seasoning was off and the modification undocumented, it became nearly impossible to reconstruct the exact process, limiting the reliability of the recipe for anyone else trying to replicate it.

### Effectiveness

Without thorough documentation, the update process becomes much less efficient. Gaps in records require additional confirmation work, whether that's reaching out to Lucía again or flagging the recipe for later review. This undermines the quick, collaborative improvements our family aims for and can slow down our efforts to preserve and adapt our shared heritage.

### Reproducibility

Gaps in the original notes break the chain of data provenance and diminish the reliability of future recipe outcomes. As with Flan and Empanadas, when crucial modifications go unverified, the next person using the recipe runs a significant risk of inconsistent results—or worse, losing track of the dish’s authentic preparation altogether. This poses a challenge for both retaining our family’s culinary heritage and ensuring the practices remain safe and effective, especially if the recipes are shared more broadly.

## 5. Evidence-Based Analysis: Best Practices in Digital Archiving and Data Provenance

### Data Documentation Standards and FAIR Principles

Following standards like those set by the National Information Standards Organization ([NISO, 2017](https://groups.niso.org/apps/group_public/download.php/17446/Understanding%20Metadata.pdf)) and the FAIR Principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) has been essential in my recipe documentation process [1][2]. Every edit is carefully annotated with references to the specific source materials used, and when I’m missing key documentation, I make sure to flag that clearly and explicitly within the record.

### Transparency and Version Control

A robust changelog, paired with metadata tracking sources, ensures every revision is visible and understandable—even years later. This is especially crucial for entries like Flan or Empanadas, where changes were based on memory or incomplete data; I separate these from entries with clear, source-verified updates like Tamales or Arroz con Pollo to maintain transparency around data quality.

### Continuous Quality Improvement

Recipes that are flagged due to ambiguity or missing information are prioritized for follow-up review. In a collaborative and intergenerational family context, it’s all too easy for small pearls of knowledge to slip through the cracks if they’re not captured fully. I’m committed to pairing our family’s oral tradition with structured digital management so none of our culinary history is lost—and so trust in our digital archive remains high.

### Implications for Public Health Nutrition

Detailed, reliable recipe records aren’t just about preserving family tradition—they’re also critical for food safety and accurate nutrition analysis. Unreliable or incomplete records increase the risk of unsafe preparations or inaccurate dietary information. By maintaining high standards in digital stewardship, our family collection supports both our own health and the integrity of the recipes should they ever be shared beyond our home.
