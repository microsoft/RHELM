## Executive Summary

This batch cooking session centered on preparing three Mediterranean-inspired lunch dishes—Farro Salad, Chilled Zucchini-Mint Soup, and Fig & Almond Biscotti—with an emphasis on nutritional balance for ongoing public health research. ## Batch Cooking Documentation

Below, I detail the ingredients and steps for each dish, noting precise measurements, ingredient substitutions or modifications (with reasons), cooking methods, and cooling/storage procedures as per 2024 EU food hygiene regulations.

### 1. Farro Salad

| Ingredient           | Measurement         | Substitution/Modification             | Rationale                                          |
|----------------------|--------------------|---------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------|
| Farro                | 200 g (dry)        | Spelt if unavailable                  | Nutritional equivalence, whole grain               |
| Cherry tomatoes      | 150 g, halved      | Local seasonal tomatoes               | Best flavor, supports local growers                |
| Cucumber             | 100 g, diced       | -                                     | Refreshing component, authenticity                 |
| Red onion            | 50 g, thinly sliced| Shallot occasionally                  | Softer flavor, Catalan touch                       |
| Kalamata olives      | 60 g, sliced       | Manzanilla olives                     | Readily available in Barcelona                     |
| Feta cheese          | 75 g, crumbled     | Formatge fresc (local cheese)         | Supports local dairy, sustainability               |
| Parsley, fresh       | 1/4 cup, chopped   | Cilantro (family variant)             | Reminder of family roots, adds freshness           |
| Extra-virgin olive oil| 2 tbsp            | -                                     | Traditional and heart-healthy                      |
| Lemon juice          | 2 tbsp             | Red wine vinegar (per Lucía)          | Nuanced acidity, family tradition                  |
| Sumac                | 1/2 tsp            | -                                     | Family addition—brings Levantine notes             |
| Salt, pepper         | To taste           | -                                     | Standard seasoning                                 |

**Method:**  
I cooked the farro in lightly salted water for about 25 minutes, rinsed it with cold water, and drained it well. Meanwhile, I prepped the vegetables—halving the tomatoes, dicing the cucumber, slicing the onion, and pitting and slicing the olives. In a small bowl, I whisked together the olive oil, lemon juice (or vinegar as Lucía recommends), sumac, salt, and pepper. Once the farro cooled, I tossed everything together in a big glass bowl, adding parsley at the end.

**Cooling/Storage:**  
After mixing, I spread the salad in a shallow dish to accelerate cooling, then transferred it to sealed glass containers. Consistent with current [EFSA/EU food safety requirements](https://food.ec.europa.eu/safety/hygiene/eu-food-hygiene-rules_en), I made sure the temperature dropped to 20°C within two hours before refrigerating at or below 4°C. Each container was labeled "Farro Salad," date stamped, and set for consumption within three days for peak freshness.

### 2. Chilled Zucchini-Mint Soup

| Ingredient           | Measurement         | Substitution/Modification             | Rationale                                          |
|----------------------|--------------------|---------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------|
| Zucchini             | 500 g, sliced      | Organic courgette                     | Chosen for flavor, low pesticide load              |
| Potato               | 80 g, diced        | Sweet potato (occasionally)           | Sweeter, vitamin A boost                           |
| Onion                | 1 medium (80 g), diced| Spring onion                        | Lighter, milder summer taste                       |
| Garlic               | 2 cloves, minced   | Roasted garlic sometimes              | Smoother, gentle flavor                            |
| Fresh mint leaves    | 15 leaves (~10 g)  | Local spearmint                       | Grown nearby, tradition                            |
| Extra-virgin olive oil| 2 tsp             | -                                     | Mediterranean staple                               |
| Vegetable broth      | 500 ml             | Homemade whenever possible            | Control sodium, reduce waste                       |
| Salt, pepper         | To taste           | -                                     | Foundational seasoning                             |
| Greek yogurt         | 2 tbsp (garnish)   | Vegan yogurt (family variant)         | Option for lactose-intolerant family               |

**Method:**  
I heated olive oil in a medium pot and softened the diced onion and garlic for about 5 minutes on gentle heat. Then I added sliced zucchini and diced potato, stirring for another five minutes. I poured in homemade vegetable broth and let the mix simmer until the vegetables softened, about 15 minutes. When done, I added the fresh mint and blended the soup until smooth with a hand blender. The chilled soup (pale green, speckled with mint) went into clean glass jars. Once cooled, I topped each serving with a dollop of yogurt.

**Cooling/Storage:**  
For food safety, I cooled the soup to room temperature within two hours and refrigerated it in sterilized glass jars, labeling each with soup name and date. It stays fresh for up to three days.

### 3. Fig & Almond Biscotti

| Ingredient           | Measurement         | Substitution/Modification             | Rationale                                          |
|----------------------|--------------------|---------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------|
| All-purpose flour    | 200 g              | Half wholewheat as variation          | Higher fiber, improved nutrition                   |
| Baking powder        | 1.5 tsp            | -                                     | Needed for rise                                    |
| Salt                 | 1/4 tsp            | -                                     | Balances sweetness                                 |
| Sugar                | 100 g              | 70 g coconut sugar variant            | Lower glycemic index, subtle flavor                |
| Eggs                 | 2 large            | Flax eggs (vegan variant)             | Family preference for dietary needs                |
| Dried figs           | 80 g, diced        | Fresh local figs in season            | Seasonal, softer texture                           |
| Almonds, whole/toasted| 90 g              | -                                     | Anchors it in the Mediterranean                   |
| Orange zest          | 1 tbsp             | Lemon zest (family option)            | Adds vibrancy, variety                             |
| Vanilla extract      | 1 tsp              | Anise extract (family option)         | For tradition, distinctive aroma                   |

**Method:**  
I sifted together flour, baking powder, and salt, then whisked the eggs (or flax eggs on request) and sugar. After stirring in zest and vanilla or anise, I combined the wet and dry ingredients, folding in chopped figs and almonds last. The dough (just sticky enough) was shaped into two logs, set on parchment, and baked at 180°C for 25 minutes. After cooling, I sliced the logs and returned the slices to the oven at 150°C, baking each side for about 12 minutes until crisp and golden.

**Cooling/Storage:**  
Once fully cooled on wire racks, I stored the biscotti in airtight tins or glass jars—clearly labeled with the name and bake date. They tend to stay at their best for up to 10 days.

## Recipe Annotation & Analysis

### Farro Salad

**Sensory Experience**  
Every time I make this salad, I’m struck by the interplay of flavors and textures. The farro brings an earthy, nutty chew that underpins the juicy burst of tomato and the cool crunch of cucumber. Briny olives and creamy feta balance everything out, and a sprinkle of sumac gives a gentle pop of citrus tang. The parsley brightens every bite, making the salad as much a feast for the eyes—reds, greens, whites—as for the palate.

**Nutritional Highlights**  
This salad offers substantial fiber and plant-based protein, and the olive oil provides healthy fats associated with cardiovascular benefits. The mixed vegetables and whole grain fit right in with Mediterranean diet recommendations for everyday health [1]. I’m always mindful of sodium from the cheese and olives, but by portioning carefully, I keep the salt levels moderate.

**Cultural Connection**  
Farro traces its origins back to Roman times. This dish feels rooted in the local landscape as much as in family tradition: tomatoes, cucumbers, and parsley from the bustling nearby markets, and feta or local cheese from Catalan dairies. Adding sumac is something Lucía brought back from Levantine friends, and vinegar instead of lemon is my grandmother's reliable trick for when citrus is out of season.

**Ingredient Sourcing & Sustainability**  
Whenever possible, I choose ingredients from Barcelona’s neighborhood markets. Opting for organic vegetables and local cheeses keeps the environmental footprint low and supports producers close to home. Farro is easily found, but if not, spelt from a market co-op fills in without any loss.

**Family Reflections**  
This salad first came together at one of our noisy, sun-soaked summer lunches. Ingredients change depending on who’s in the kitchen: Lucía likes sumac, Mamá always wants vinegar. The recipe is a living thing, shaped by what we have on hand and by the memories we all bring to the table.

### Chilled Zucchini-Mint Soup

**Sensory Experience**  
On hot days in Barcelona, this soup is a cooling comfort. The first spoonful is fresh and grassy from the zucchini, with mint providing an aromatic lift and potatoes lending natural creaminess. Topped with yogurt, it feels light but decadent, and the color—pale green flecked with specks of mint—always looks beautiful in a clear glass bowl.

**Nutritional Highlights**  
Low in calories, high in potassium and vitamin C, and rich in fiber from both vegetables and potatoes, this soup is hydrating, restorative, and aligns with Mediterranean eating principles [2]. Stirring in Greek yogurt adds a little protein and probiotics for good measure.

**Cultural Connection**  
Chilled vegetable soups like gazpacho and soupe froide have a long history across the Mediterranean. Mint is our family signature, more than just an herb—it recalls my grandmother’s apartment, windows open, a garden bursting with aromatic plants that seemed to defy the city heat.

**Ingredient Sourcing & Sustainability**  
I choose organic local zucchini and mint whenever possible. Using homemade broth reduces waste and sodium, and the whole soup takes advantage of late-summer produce, making it sustainable as well as satisfying.

**Family Reflections**  
There’s nothing like coming back from the market in August, sweaty and tired, to a bowl of this soup. My great-grandmother first made it for us when the heat was too much for heavier fare. It’s part nostalgia, part self-care.

### Fig & Almond Biscotti

**Sensory Experience**  
These biscotti are a true family treat—crisp, nutty, with a sweetness that comes from caramelized figs and toasted almonds. The aroma of citrus zest fills the kitchen during baking, and sometimes I add anise for an extra layer of warmth. The bits of fig are chewy, a delicious contrast to the crisp exterior.

**Nutritional Highlights**  
With a mix of wholewheat and all-purpose flour, these cookies become a more wholesome option—higher in fiber, with healthy fats from almonds, and less sugar than typical versions [3]. Coconut sugar lends a caramel-like note and helps lower the glycemic load.

**Cultural Connection**  
Although biscotti are Italian by origin, Catalan markets brim with almonds and summer figs, so this recipe feels very much at home here. Swapping in orange or lemon zest and a dash of anise is a nod to my family’s migration and the festive flavors of holiday baking.

**Ingredient Sourcing & Sustainability**  
I use local almonds and figs whenever possible, especially during summer when both are freshest and cheapest. Choosing coconut sugar was an experiment for better blood sugar control, and so far, the flavor and texture haven’t disappointed.

**Family Reflections**  
This recipe has decades of history: I found my bisabuela’s handwritten notes tucked in an old cookbook. Whenever we bake a batch for christenings, Christmas, or just a quiet weekend, it’s a way of sharing those same moments—and those flavors—with the next generation.

## Digital Archiving Summary

Preserving these recipes goes well beyond simply writing them down. With every batch, I log metadata for each recipe—including the date, location (always my Eixample apartment), dish name, origins for key ingredients (documenting whether they’re local, organic, or seasonal), specific modifications, and allergen information. I follow [Dublin Core](https://www.dublincore.org/specifications/dublin-core/dces/) metadata standards for consistency and future searchability.

Every entry is date- and time-stamped. If I try something new—like coconut sugar in the biscotti or using vinegar in the salad—I mark it as a version update (e.g., v1.2). Family variants get logged separately; for instance, Lucía’s sumac or her grandmother’s vinegar substitutions are listed as “branch” versions for easy reference. This way, our evolving family adaptations form a living digital archive.

I include explicit weights and process steps so anyone (including myself, six months down the line) can reliably reproduce any dish. I also document the cooling and storage process, with links to relevant EU food safety rules. Whenever I can, I add photographs throughout cooking and of the finished product, so the archive is both practical and visually descriptive.

Preserving the social context is just as important to me as the recipes themselves. I always annotate sensory impressions, cultural touchpoints, and oral family stories, and I store scans of handwritten originals whenever available. Organization is standardized—PDF/A, CSV, and JSON for metadata—to maximize future compatibility, and I back everything up on both hard drives and the cloud (in line with [UNESCO digital heritage guidance](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000130071)), hoping some thread of tradition can survive whatever technology comes next.
