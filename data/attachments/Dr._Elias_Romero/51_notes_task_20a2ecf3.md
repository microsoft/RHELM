## Section 2: Cooking Process

I approach recipe development with a disciplined, scientific process that aligns with professional kitchen standards and food science documentation. Below, each step is carefully detailed to ensure another cook could replicate the results exactly, while allowing for easy tracking of changes for future research or scaling.

### Ingredient Preparation

- _Washing & Prep:_  
  Every vegetable and fruit was thoroughly washed in potable water and dried on sanitized towels. I made sure to use separate cutting boards to avoid allergen cross-contact. Squash was peeled, deseeded, and diced into 2 cm cubes. I minced the shallots, sliced the wild mushrooms after gently wiping them, and zested the orange. Persimmons were peeled and sliced thinly, ready for integration near the end.

### Batch Details

- _Portions:_ Six standard servings  
- _Equipment Used:_ 28 cm All-Clad sauté pan, 5L enamel Dutch oven, induction hob calibrated to within 2°C

### Step-by-Step Instructions

1. **Vegetable Broth**  
   I simmered 500 g of mixed organic vegetable trimmings (carrot, celery, onion, parsley) with 1.5 L cold water, held steady at 90°C for 45 minutes, and filtered it through cheesecloth. I reserved exactly 900 ml for use in this batch, noting the broth’s clear appearance and clean aroma.

2. **Squash Roasting**  
   The convection oven was preheated to 210°C. I tossed the diced butternut squash with 8 ml olive oil and half the salt, arranging cubes in a single layer on a parchment-lined tray. I roasted for 22 minutes, turning cubes halfway, aiming for deep caramelization. I checked that the largest cubes reached a core temperature of at least 95°C using a probe thermometer.

3. **Cooking Farro**  
   After rinsing, the farro was combined with 750 ml of the reserved broth in the Dutch oven. Once at a full boil, I lowered to a steady 92°C simmer, lid semi-on, stirring every 10 minutes. After 28 minutes, the grains were tender but maintained structure. I drained any excess liquid to prevent overcooking and spread out the grains to cool.

4. **Aromatics and Mushrooms**  
   I preheated the sauté pan to 165°C, verified by IR thermometer, then added 12 ml olive oil. Shallots were sweated until translucent, followed by mushrooms, which browned for about 2 minutes. Throughout, I avoided overcrowding and made sure the pan stayed above 155°C. Dry spices—sumac, piri-piri, and black pepper—were added and cooked for a minute to fully release their flavors.

5. **Assembly and Seasoning**  
   All cooked components—squash, mushrooms, and farro—were combined in the Dutch oven. I gently folded in sliced persimmons and orange zest, careful not to break up the fruits. I adjusted final seasoning with salt and extra sumac to taste.

6. **Sunflower Seed Garnish**  
   Just before plating, I toasted sunflower seeds in a dry skillet at 150°C for 3 minutes, ensuring a deep aroma and subtle browning. They were cooled immediately and sprinkled over the dish at service.

### Batch Outcomes

- **Yield:** Six portions, averaging around 360 g each (pre-garnish), for a total batch mass of 2180 g. Across recent repetitions, batch variance remained below 2%.

## Section 4: Recipe Update Log

### Modifications This Session

- Added orange zest, inspired by Lucía’s suggestion during a team review. Extensive sensory research supports the use of citrus as a brightening component in squash-based dishes [1]. The zest was folded in at the last moment to preserve its volatile aromatics.
- Increased mushroom quantity by 25 g to enhance umami balance.
- Adjusted roasting duration and temperature checks for squash to promote even, deep caramelization—resulting in richer flavor.
- Toasted sunflower seeds just before plating, preserving their full aroma and crunch.

### Peer Feedback and Takeaways

Feedback on these changes was positive across the board. The use of orange zest was supported by everyone at the tasting roundtable; one panelist proposed microplaning the zest for finer dispersal, which I’ll apply next time. All process and sensory data were logged for continued traceability and future reference.

## Section 5: Reflections and Digitization

### Culinary and Nutritional Reflection

The completed dish achieved a distinct autumnal character, uniting squash's caramelized sweetness, the wild mushrooms' earthy depth, and the freshness of ripe persimmon and orange zest. Farro contributed a backbone of nutty texture and gentle chew, while piri-piri and sumac added warmth and bright tang. Sunflower seeds delivered the final textural accent.  

Nutritionally, the recipe balanced slow-digesting carbohydrates, a modest but meaningful amount of plant-based protein and fiber, and nutrient density from mushrooms and persimmons. With olive oil providing healthy fats and careful attention to salt, each portion remained well within targets for public health compliance.

### Challenges and Next Steps

- Sourcing wild mushrooms was the most demanding aspect. Reliable, certified suppliers and inspection for freshness and safety are ongoing priorities.
- Achieving consistent farro texture required vigilant temperature control and immediate cooling after cooking—an adjustment that has now stabilized batch quality.
- I also experimented with the timing for adding orange zest, finding that incorporating it just before assembly best preserved its aroma.

### Progress in Digitization and Data Management

All ingredient sourcing records, procedural details, batch outcomes, and sensory panel scores are stored in accordance with FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) guidelines [2]. I have designed stepwise instructions suitable for scaling and operational coding, aiming to facilitate both large-batch production and future research. Sensory evaluations are now entered through a digital platform, tracking anonymized panelist input over time. Each ingredient and prep stage is logged with acquisition and batch information, ensuring full process traceability in line with both food safety and academic reproducibility standards.
