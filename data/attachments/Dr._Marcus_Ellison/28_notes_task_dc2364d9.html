From previous experience, Hotel Peter & Paul provides an ideal base for sociological fieldwork. The building’s generous proportions and thoughtful restoration offer quiet spaces for note transcription and interview recordings, and the large in-room desks with ergonomic chairs make it easy to process data after long days of field research. Wi-Fi is fast and reliable, while the blackout curtains and solid construction allow for needed privacy.

Amenities - The Rectory Lounge is typically calm before noon, making it one of my favorite spots for early-morning reading and reviewing field notes. - The courtyard provides a hidden escape to decompress or conduct informal debriefs away from the bustle. - Onsite food and drink options extend the working day, so I rarely need to leave the property when a deadline looms or an urgent call comes in. - Security has always felt robust: guests must key in after hours, and room safes are sufficiently sized for cameras and laptops.

Logistical Advantages - The location is within walking distance to the Marigny, Bywater, and St. Roch neighborhoods—central to my interviews and site visits. - Ride-shares and taxis are easily called to the entrance for longer trips. - The staff demonstrates a clear respect for visiting researchers, offering discretion around interviews and helping with printing/scanning on request. - Laundry services are a real help when packing light in the summer heat. For print and scan needs, I recommend calling the front desk ahead of heavy sessions—they are consistently accommodating.

5-Day Research & Fieldwork Plan

My approach is to balance structured meetings with opportunities for immersive observation, informal conversations, and evening engagement with New Orleans’ distinct cultural scene. Each day’s activities are anchored in locations relevant to historic and ongoing struggles around urban change, displacement, and local resistance.

Monday, July 15 — Arrival & Initial Exploration

Morning:
My flight from New York lands in the late morning. I catch a rideshare into the city, checking in early where possible or securely leaving bags with the front desk.

Lunch:
A quick meal at the hotel cafe or Bacchanal gets me grounded. Bacchanal in particular offers the chance to overhear snippets of local conversation and begin building a picture of the current community mood.

Afternoon:
I use these first hours to walk through the Marigny and adjacent Bywater. Armed with camera and notebook, I document visible shifts in the built environment, noting new developments, housing alterations, and street-level commerce. I pay attention to boundaries between older homes and new builds, listening for any overheard discussion of rent hikes or community events. This initial orientation shapes my research questions and sets the tone for deeper engagement over the week.

Evening:
Dinner at Café Sbisa offers the opportunity for informal networking amid locals and visiting scholars. I jot down early impressions, trying to note the interplay of old and new—both in the restaurant and in the neighborhood streets just outside.

Tuesday, July 16 — Housing Justice & Organizing

Morning:
I meet with Jamal Rivers at the Justice Housing Coalition NOLA office (2222 St. Bernard Ave). Jamal is an established figure in local housing activism, and our session focuses on understanding current grassroots organizing strategies and recent policy changes. We review the research consent forms, and he generously offers to connect me with tenant organizers and introduce me to some of his contacts in Treme.

Late Morning/Lunch:
Sharing lunch with Jamal and several staff members, I try to listen more than talk, learning about the daily realities facing renters and low-income homeowners in New Orleans.

Afternoon:
I make my way to Treme, spending the afternoon observing a tenant council meeting near Claiborne Avenue. The group discusses strategies for addressing evictions and unfair rent increases. I’m cautious with documentation, verbally explaining my research intent and obtaining permission before making notes or recordings.

Evening:
New Orleans breathes through its music, and in the evening I visit The Spotted Cat on Frenchmen Street—a venue well-loved by locals. Here, I reflect on how music, space, and nightly routines reveal another side of community resilience, as well as the pressures of tourism and gentrification.

Wednesday, July 17 — Academic Exchange & Neighborhood Interviews

Morning:
A peer debrief at the hotel lounge allows me to discuss the previous day’s observations with a colleague. These sessions are invaluable for refining research focus and comparing impressions, and often yield unexpected insights about local dynamics.

Lunch:
I head to Elizabeth’s in Bywater for lunch—a neighborhood spot known for its comfort food. The lively atmosphere helps me recalibrate before afternoon interviews.

Afternoon:
Interviews in Bywater and Mid-City are lined up with both activists and residents. I’m attentive to variations in narrative—some see the recent changes as positive, others are wary or openly resistant. Contrasting the gentrification dynamics in these neighborhoods with those in Treme, I focus on participants’ own framing of their housing struggles and aspirations.

Evening:
Dinner with a local academic at Marigny Brasserie provides an opportunity to share field insights and begin synthesizing emerging themes. I record notes for follow-up and collect recommendations for literature and further site visits.

Thursday, July 18 — Organizational Perspectives & Cultural Nightlife

Morning:
My day starts with a visit to St. Roch Community Housing at 1739 St. Roch Ave. I meet with staff, sit in on a project staff meeting, and arrange brief interviews with directors and a few residents who agree to participate. These discussions center on affordable housing strategies in one of the city’s most rapidly evolving neighborhoods.

Lunch:
St. Roch Market, with its variety of food stalls and diverse clientele, is not only convenient but also offers a cross-section of residents. I use the casual lunchtime setting to talk informally with fellow diners about daily life and neighborhood shifts.

Afternoon:
I join organizers at Project Homecoming for follow-up documentation. Here, narratives of displacement and recovery take center stage, and I pay particular attention to how stories of loss (post-Katrina dislocations, for example) are retold in communal and architectural spaces.

Evening:
The night is reserved for jazz along Frenchmen Street—d.b.a., Blue Nile, and other venues offer the distinct ambience of local resilience through music. I observe spatial patterns: how musicians, dancers, and audience members share or contest space, and I reflect on the role of music as social glue in these transforming communities.

Friday, July 19 — Synthesis & Departure

Morning:
I conduct the last set of interviews and spend quiet time consolidating my fieldnotes and digital files, making sure all photographic and audio data are securely backed up on my laptop and encrypted cloud storage. I double-check personal identifiers and consent forms to comply with all ethical requirements before departing.

Midday:
After a final check-out (requesting late check-out if needed), I transfer to the airport, using lounge time to review documentation and begin outlining key findings. My return flight is set for the early afternoon, bringing the week’s fieldwork to a close.

Field Photography & Data Security

Camera Equipment

With my Fujifilm X100V temporarily out of commission, I’ll use the Sony RX100 VII for its compact size, excellent image quality, and silent operation—ideal for candid street work and low-profile observation. The Ricoh GR IIIx provides another lightweight alternative for fast documentation. As a backup and for impromptu interviews or environmental audio, I’ll use my iPhone 15 Pro, making use of its ProRAW capabilities and an external microphone for high-quality audio capture.

Daily Data Workflow

  • Every evening, I transfer photos and recordings from the camera and phone to my encrypted laptop back at the hotel.
  • I upload a secondary backup to my private Box cloud folder, ensuring redundancy in case of loss or device failure.
  • SD cards are stored in a separate bag and kept in the room safe.
  • Digital files are organized by date, neighborhood, and pseudonym. All consent (verbal or written) is documented, either in Evernote or within image metadata as appropriate.
  • I am diligent about using silent shutter mode and refraining from flash to maintain a respectful approach.

Ethical Protocols

  • I always ask for explicit consent for any close-up or potentially identifiable photographs and explain the purpose and potential uses of all field documentation.
  • When possible, I share photographs or final research reports with participants who request them.
  • All data remains encrypted, and I keep a clear distinction between identifying information and anonymized research files, in line with best practices set out by the American Sociological Association’s Code of Ethics (source) and established qualitative data management guidelines (source).

Fieldwork Ethics and Research Integrity

Maintaining strong ethical standards is foundational to my approach in the field.

  • Engagement:
    I build rapport through authentic presence, recurring attendance at community meetings, and transparent communication about my research aims.
  • Consent:
    Written or verbal consent is secured for all interviews or identifiable audio/visual documentation. If needed, forms are explained and provided in accessible language.
  • Confidentiality:
    I maintain a digital log of all research encounters (stored separately from photos/audio). Pseudonyms are used consistently, and sensitive information is never shared without explicit permission.
  • Reciprocity:
    I believe in sharing findings, photographs (where consented), and progress updates with community partners, engaging in reciprocal knowledge exchange wherever possible.
  • Transparency & Respect:
    My research design, funding sources, and intended outcomes are always explained upfront. I avoid intruding into sensitive events or private spaces without direct approval from participants or community leaders.
