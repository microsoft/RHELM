## Mentee Photo Submissions and Group Discussion

### Janelle Carter: Visual Storytelling in Urban Storefronts

**Project Focus:**  
Janelle presented a series capturing storefronts in the west end district, emphasizing the interplay between architecture, signage, and everyday changes within a rapidly evolving urban neighborhood. Her work documents how store facades reflect economic flux, embodying both longstanding local businesses and emergent commercial ventures.

**Sociological Reflection:**  
The discussion anchored Janelle's project within Sharon Zukin’s insights on authenticity and urban transformation. Storefronts were recognized as visible markers of broader socioeconomic changes—signaling shifts in neighborhood identity, and illustrating how economic pressures manifest in the everyday landscape [1]. The group noted that Janelle’s photographs provide an effective lens on gentrification: some images poignantly capture both the resilience of long-term business owners and the subtle erasure of a community’s historical identity.

The group also explored how these visual narratives both highlight and obscure the complexity of urban change. While some photographs openly reveal economic struggle or adaptation, others hint at underlying tensions through more understated details, such as boarded windows or temporary signage.

**Technical and Conceptual Critique:**  
Janelle’s purposeful composition and thoughtful sequencing received recognition for establishing a strong narrative flow. The use of contrast between newly remodeled shops and weathered storefronts was particularly effective in conveying temporal transitions. Suggestions for further development included experimenting with varied lighting—such as shooting in the early evening or under overcast conditions—to reveal different textures and moods associated with city rhythms.

Additionally, incorporating more images where pedestrians interact with storefront environments was recommended to enrich the ethnographic aspect of her series. Visual cues like closed shutters or handwritten notices could be linked more explicitly to sociological themes such as economic instability or adaptation strategies, deepening the analysis of visible marginalization in urban space.

**Ethical Considerations:**  
The group discussed the importance of building trust with business owners and individuals who appear in public spaces. Janelle was encouraged to seek acknowledgment from owners or staff when photographing active businesses, ensuring all subjects understand the project’s purpose and granting permission as appropriate. Transparency and explicit consent, particularly when individuals might be identifiable, were emphasized as essential to ethical visual research [5].

### Tariq Mensah: Representation and Identity in Portraiture

**Project Focus:**  
Tariq shared a portrait series exploring the experiences of Ghanaian diaspora adolescents. His images aimed to capture layered expressions of identity, weaving together visual elements of intergenerational heritage and the realities of living between cultures.

**Sociological Reflection:**  
Drawing on Stuart Hall’s framework of cultural representation, the group considered how Tariq’s work both mirrors and interrogates prevailing narratives about diaspora communities [2]. Each portrait offered a lens through which viewers could reconsider how identity is constructed, portrayed, and perceived. The discussion also touched on Pierre Bourdieu’s concept of habitus, highlighting how body language, posture, and setting subtly communicate social history and belonging [3].

Attention was given to the collaborative dynamic between photographer and subject. The process of co-creating images was recognized as crucial for avoiding voyeurism and fostering agency among sitters. This approach aligns with broader ethical principles and supports more nuanced, authentic storytelling in visual sociology.

**Technical and Conceptual Critique:**  
Tariq’s photographs stood out for their sensitivity to mood and context, inviting nuanced interpretations of identity through the interplay of gesture and gaze. The feedback centered on refining technical choices to further highlight these dynamics: using shallower depth of field can draw attention to the sitter’s expressions, and more selective framing can intensify the relationship between subject and environment.

The group encouraged Tariq to add another dimension to the project by inviting portrait participants to share short audio or written reflections about their experience or sense of self. Integrating these personal narratives with the imagery would provide a richer, multi-voiced ethnography and further uphold ethical transparency [4].

**Ethical Considerations:**  
Ethics were a core part of the conversation, given the involvement of minors within a diaspora community. Tariq has begun gathering both verbal and written parental consent for all participants, thoroughly documenting the process to ensure accountability and respect. Beyond compliance, the group discussed how participatory methods—bringing subjects into decisions about image use and narrative framing—can counteract stereotype and objectification. The importance of respecting subjects’ comfort with how their image is used and contextualized was highlighted as an ongoing responsibility for the project.
