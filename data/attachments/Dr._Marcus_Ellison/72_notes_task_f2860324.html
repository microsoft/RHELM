Marcus & Maya – November 29, 2024

1. Spicy Lentil Soup Preparation Documentation

Chili Blend Reflections:
During prep, we debated just how much to lean into the chili’s heat. After some tasting, we realized that less aggressive spice—using more Aleppo pepper for gentle complexity over raw heat—fit the evening’s spirit. The chipotle powder was Maya’s idea; she wanted to evoke a sense of “smoky resilience” as a metaphor for making something strong from adversity (a theme we returned to in our later discussions). With the fresh chili, we went lighter and deseeded it, thinking of guests who are sensitive to heat, but still wanted to carry that green, vegetal note.

B. Step-by-Step Preparation Guide

  1. Sauté Aromatics:
    I started by heating olive oil in the large soup pot over medium. Once shimmering, I added the diced onions, carrots, and celery. We let these cook down, stirring occasionally, for about 5–6 minutes until everything took on a gentle golden hue and smelled impossibly inviting. Garlic and ginger went in next; after just another minute, the kitchen was filled with a bright, warm aroma.

  2. Bloom Spices:
    At this point, I slid in our cumin, coriander, smoked paprika, turmeric, and the carefully blended chili mix. This step always feels a bit magical—the spices sizzle and bloom, sending up a rush of fragrance that signals the soup’s real beginning. I stirred steadily for another full minute, not wanting anything to burn.

  3. Add Lentils:
    The rinsed lentils joined the vegetables next. I stirred everything together for a couple of minutes so the lentils could take on all that flavor before any liquid goes in.

  4. Simmer:
    In went the can of tomatoes and the broth. We tossed in the bay leaf and the salt, gave it all one last stir, then brought everything to a gentle boil. We lowered the heat, covered the pot, and let it simmer quietly for about half an hour. By now, conversation filled the kitchen, as we excitedly traced the aroma drifting into the other room.

  5. Finish and Season:
    When the lentils softened and the soup reached that perfect, hearty thickness, I removed the bay leaf and tasted for salt and pepper—adjusting just a little. Right before serving, Maya squeezed in fresh lemon juice, instantly sharpening the flavors and brightening the whole pot.

  6. Serve:
    We finished with generous handfuls of chopped cilantro. We ladled the soup into bowls and passed around warm flatbreads, plus lemon wedges for anyone craving more brightness. There was a quiet moment—everyone savoring the first spoonfuls—before the dinner conversation unfurled.

2. Dinner Reflections

A. Core Dialogue Points

As dinner was served, conversation naturally circled back to the project’s recent setbacks. We spoke candidly about the challenges we’ve faced: Exhibit production delays and, more painfully, the sudden loss of two team members. Some colleagues—especially younger ones—have seemed increasingly unsure or distant. Morale, at times, has felt fragile. I shared that I’d noticed a sense of frustration, and even Maya admitted the momentum sometimes feels impossible to regain after hitting so many roadblocks.

In the midst of all this, Marcus brought up Masten’s resilience theory (2001), reframing these stumbles as opportunities for growth. He reminded us that adaptation after adversity is not only possible—it’s often the foundation for stronger teams and deeper learning. Maya connected this directly to Antonovsky’s sense of coherence (1987), saying that acknowledging these difficulties openly and collectively helps young team members find meaning in setbacks and fosters a sense of manageability. Unlike glossing over troubles, creating a space where struggles are visible and discussed openly allows everyone—especially our youth collaborators—to build a real connection to the project and to each other.

B. Critical Analysis: Strategies for Youth Resilience in the Exhibit

Given these experiences, we turned our attention to the exhibit’s design, especially how we might better center youth experiences of resilience:

  • Narratives of Resilience
    We agreed on the importance of moving beyond mere tales of adversity. Instead, the exhibit needs to foreground stories of adaptation—where young people respond to setbacks not just defensively, but creatively and proactively. Ungar’s Social Ecological Model of Resilience [1] guided us here; the goal is to convey that resilience happens not just within individuals but through a network of supports, resources, and community actions.

  • Youth Agency in Storytelling
    We came back several times to the need for youth-driven content, rooted in Hart’s Ladder of Youth Participation [2]. Rather than collecting polished narratives, we want genuine voices—young people narrating their own experiences, including the ongoing messiness of not-quite-fixed problems. Giving this creative control upholds their legitimacy as authors of their own stories.

  • Participatory Design Adjustments
    In terms of exhibit interactivity, we brainstormed tweaks to our engagement platforms. One new idea was a visual “contribution map” that lets youth document not only obstacles but the concrete strategies and supports they draw upon over time, leveraging principles from participatory action research. This map would make visible how solutions emerge collectively, not just individually.

  • Exemplar Models
    To ground our plans, Maya brought up the Museum of Contemporary Youth’s virtual “digital confessional” project, which made space for everyday resilience stories through anonymous digital storytelling. I referenced the “Power of Story” exhibit at the Children’s Museum of Pittsburgh [3]: their iterative design sessions with youth lead to displays that felt immediate and authentic, not sanitized after the fact. Both models underscore the value of young people not as subjects of adult storytelling, but as equal collaborators and curators.

3. Brainstormed Concepts for Interactive Q&A Wall

To further support authentic engagement among youth visitors, we developed a list of potential interactive features for the Q&A wall. Each is grounded in current research about participatory engagement and resilience-building.

Concept Name Description Engagement Model / Theory Reference / Case Study
Resilience Questions Board Youth and visitors submit questions about overcoming challenges; others contribute responses, sparking dialogue and validation Peer-to-peer engagement; Hart’s Ladder Levels 6–8 [2], [4]
“Ask a Mentor” Digital Kiosk Young people pose their pressing questions to a rotating group of adult mentors, receiving personalized video or audio answers Mentor-based engagement; Positive Youth Development Theory [5]
Challenge Mapping Mural A collaborative whiteboard mural mapping real setbacks and creative solutions, built in real time and visible to all Participatory mapping; Social Ecological Model [1], [6]
Experience Sharing Wall Postcards or sticky notes for sharing brief first-person accounts of resilience, failure, support, and transformation Narrative inquiry; Reflective practice [3], [7]
Resilience Resource QR Codes Ark of QR-linked microsites providing mobile access to self-guided tools, podcasts, and crisis resources Multimodal/digital engagement; supports inclusive discovery [8]
“What Would You Do?” Prompts Prompt cards invite youth to respond to realistic (or fictionalized) dilemmas—encouraging thoughtful debate and perspective-taking Constructivist theory; Scaffolded participation [9]
Live Poet’s Corner Scheduled and impromptu spoken word or poetry sessions for processing and sharing adaptation through creative expression Expressive arts engagement; Transformational learning [10]

During our brainstorming, we recognized the need for a variety of engagement modes—some introverted, some public; some analog, some digital—all of which allow youth to choose how they share, listen, and learn.
