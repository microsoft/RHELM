## Session Overview

This session was developed specifically to support post-injury knee recovery through restorative yoga, integrating both holistic and trauma-informed principles. Drawing from established protocols in restorative yoga and best practices for injury rehabilitation, the session prioritized accessibility, psychological safety, and participant empowerment. Informed by trauma-sensitive approaches used in community wellness spaces like the Harlem Wellness Center, each pose was adapted to maximize comfort, restore functional movement, and foster self-trust throughout the healing journey.

The following notes provide detailed documentation of pose setups, prop choices (including the rationale for each), observed physiological and psychological effects, and reflective participant feedback. This comprehensive record is intended to guide ongoing recovery and serve as a practical resource for practitioners and educators adapting restorative yoga for others on similar paths.

## Session Reflections

### Physical Sensations & Embodied Experience

**Before Session**: Participants shared concerns about provoking pain or re-injury, with lingering swelling and feeling physically vulnerable. There was apprehension about certain movements that, in the past, triggered discomfort.

**During Session**: As props were customized and positions adjusted, participants’ bodies settled. Support beneath and around the knees led to immediate relief from sharp or nagging sensations. Over time, muscle protection responses—like guarding and holding breath—diminished, replaced by deeper exhalations and more even heart rates. Each successive pose reinforced a sense of being safely contained.

**After Session**: At the close, participants rated their knee discomfort as much lower than at the start (dropping from 6/10 to 3/10). Not only did their joints feel less reactive, but the whole body also felt “heavier” in a positive, grounded way, with a sense of gentle warmth around the knees. Emotionally, anxiety dropped markedly (7/10 to 2/10). Many noted a new sense of control over their recovery, the ability to self-adjust whichever position didn’t feel right, and increased trust in both their bodies and the healing process.

#### Anxiety & Mood Tracking

- **Pre-session**: 7/10—heightened vigilance, concern over pain
- **Post-session**: 2/10—feeling safe, relaxed, and at ease

For ongoing practice, recording these self-assessments on a simple 1–10 scale at the start and end of sessions promotes self-awareness and tracks progress. A daily wellbeing journal—brief notes about physical sensations, emotions, and energy—can complement this, allowing both practitioners and participants to identify trends, triggers, or improvements over time.

### Psychosocial and Holistic Wellbeing Markers

- **Empowerment & Agency**: Inviting participants to modify prop placements fostered genuine ownership of the recovery process. When individuals chose which cushions or supports work best, self-advocacy became part of their practice.
- **Relational Support and Community**: Simple sensory elements—like Dr. Grant’s handwoven napkin—offered grounding, but also symbolized care and ancestry. Even knowing these practices originated from the Harlem Wellness Center helped create a sense of collective recovery, of being supported by a wider circle, not just alone in injury.
- **Connection**: Participants described imagining themselves surrounded by a community of others on similar journeys. This unspoken connection relieved the isolation that often accompanies injury, making recovery feel like a shared endeavor.

## Recommendations for Future Sessions

1. **Prop Personalization and Choice**  
   Continue encouraging participants to adjust or experiment with supports—using household items like napkins, cushions, or blocks—to ensure optimal safety, comfort, and personal agency. Soft, customizable props offer both physical relief and emotional comfort.

2. **Building Empowerment and Autonomy**  
   Offer clear, invitational language that permits participants to modify or skip any pose as desired. Providing both verbal and written scripts (“If this doesn’t feel safe, please pause and rest or adapt with more support”) can foster confidence.

3. **Integrated Progress and Wellbeing Tracking**  
   - Include brief psychoeducational check-ins during or after sessions (e.g., “Name three words describing your state now”).
   - Introduce weekly checklists that monitor pain, emotional state, energy, fatigue, and sense of agency.
   - If appropriate, incorporate gentle reflection prompts: “How do I feel emotionally connected—or isolated—in my healing journey today?”

4. **Qualitative and Quantitative Metrics for Progress**  
   - Regular self-report pain and mood scales at each session.
   - Maintain an Embodied Wellbeing Journal: encourage reflections on sensory experience, shifts in mood, and perceived healing.
   - Use a “Restorative Agency” scale—simple, direct questions like “Did I feel in control of my recovery today?”
   - For those in group or virtual settings, use periodic guided debriefs or peer check-ins to enhance the sense of collective support.

5. **Enhancing Accessibility and Cultural Resonance**  
   - Create and share photographic or video prop setup guides for greater accessibility, especially for remote or home-based participants.
   - Craft and update adaptation notes using accessible, trauma-informed language, mindful to include people of all body types, histories, and abilities.
   - Reflect periodically on the cultural and emotional resonance of props and rituals, such as using objects with ancestral or familial significance to cultivate warmth, care, and continuity in healing traditions.
