## Executive Summary

This report presents the results of a detailed digitization, transcription, and analysis process, capturing the essence of fieldwork carried out in historically significant New Orleans neighborhoods—with a particular focus on communities such as Tremé. The project systematically documents and examines a range of firsthand accounts, statistical trends, and archival material, concentrating on the city’s enduring social inequalities and vibrant strategies of neighborhood resilience in the wake of post-Katrina upheavals and the continuing process of gentrification.

The digitization of the original field notes, which included both handwritten and audio records, involved a multi-phase process using OCR-enabled software, rigorous coding in NVivo, and quantitative analysis in Excel and R. Every record is assigned a unique identifier, time and location stamp, summary, and source type, in line with ethical standards—ensuring anonymity and confidentiality for all participants.

Situated within a broader sociological context, the research draws on and expands our understanding of spatial and racial inequalities in New Orleans, especially as these are manifest in neighborhoods like Tremé, the Lower Ninth Ward, and Central City. Hurricane Katrina remains a definitive event, with its aftermath reshaping local identities, economies, and landscapes. Today, these communities continue to confront new pressures: surging rents, deepening displacement, and the changing makeup of their streets and institutions.

The digitized material now serves as both a comprehensive record and a foundation for analysis, highlighting how residents confront and adapt to rapid urban change—negotiating new identities, sustaining traditions, developing mutual aid networks, and preserving local memory. These insights not only document transformation but are directly relevant to ongoing debates in urban sociology surrounding inequality, social networks, and community resilience.

## Introduction

The following summary brings together data, observations, and voices collected during ethnographic, interview, and archival research conducted across key New Orleans neighborhoods in May 2024. The research builds on a foundational body of scholarship documenting cycles of disinvestment, disaster, reconstruction, and the emergence of "new" urban inequalities, but shifts the focus to lived experience and neighborhood identity. Particular attention is paid to communities uniquely affected by these transformations—most notably Tremé, widely recognized as the nation’s oldest African-American neighborhood and an epicenter of local and national history.

Through direct engagement with residents, community organizers, local business owners, and archival records, the fieldwork offers a multidimensional account of both the enduring challenges and the adaptive strategies seen in contemporary New Orleans. By observing public events, conducting interviews, and consulting archival and oral history collections, the research records not just demographic and economic trends, but also stories of memory, solidarity, and change as understood by those most intimately involved.

## Methodology

### Data Collection

Fieldwork was structured to capture a representative cross-section of New Orleans’ most dynamic and historically significant neighborhoods:

- **Sites**: Tremé (noted for its unique history and ongoing transformation), Lower Ninth Ward (associated with both devastating flood impacts and ongoing activism), Central City (site of cultural endurance and economic challenge), and Bywater (a locus of recent gentrification).
- **Participants**: The research involved in-depth interviews and observations with longtime residents, community leaders, small business owners, and members of neighborhood associations. Particular care was taken to ensure a diversity of perspectives—including those experiencing direct effects of displacement or economic precarity.
- **Data Sources**:  
  - Semi-structured interviews—scheduled and impromptu, often conducted in participants’ homes, workplaces, or at community events  
  - Participant observation at neighborhood gatherings, block parties, meetings, and community forums  
  - Review of local archival materials, including oral histories, meeting minutes, and property records, to provide a deeper historical perspective

### Digitization Process

The digitization stage was designed for maximum accuracy and data integrity:

- **Technological Tools**:  
  - Handwritten journals and interview notes were transcribed using OCR scanning software, then checked against original recordings for accuracy.  
  - Qualitative notes were coded in NVivo, allowing for systematic thematic analysis.  
  - Quantitative data—such as demographic counts, rent levels, or event attendance—were entered into structured spreadsheets for aggregation and statistical review using Excel and R.

- **Security and Data Management**:  
  - Strict anonymization protocols were followed, including redaction of names, addresses, and other identifying details.  
  - All files were encrypted and backed up on secure institutional drives, with access restricted to authorized team members.  
  - Metadata for each entry included timestamp (ISO 8601), geolocation coordinates, source type, and concise summaries.

- **Cataloging**:  
  - Each field note received a unique identifier (e.g., FN20240522-01), ensuring traceability and easy cross-referencing between the digital catalog, analytical tables, and appendices.

## Synthesis of Core Insights and Themes

### Systemic Inequality

#### Rental and Housing Insecurity

Quantitative trends clearly show significant increases in median rent in Tremé and Bywater over the past six years, disproportionately impacting Black residents and families with multi-generational ties to the area. Qualitative data from interviews and community meetings consistently reveal deep unease over mass evictions, aggressive speculative buying, and persistent police presence in neighborhoods. Residents expressed concerns that affordable rental stock continues to dwindle, with some describing the search for stable housing as “an endless uphill climb.” Participants in advocacy meetings described the growing gap between stagnant local wages and the accelerating cost of housing, leading to endemic precarity.

#### Spatialized Racial Inequality

Across all observed neighborhoods, the evidence points to ongoing and deeply embedded patterns of marginalization. These have been amplified by market forces and uneven reinvestment since Katrina: public infrastructure and social services remain weakest in areas with the highest concentrations of long-term Black residents. Interviewees and archival sources alike recall a long legacy of public neglect, now compounded by speculative development that does little to serve existing populations. The material realities—overgrown lots, patchwork repairs, shuttered community centers—are matched by a keen local awareness of who benefits from citywide “recovery.”

### Community Resilience

#### Mutual Aid and Adaptation

Under challenging circumstances, local organizations and informal support systems have emerged as vital lifelines. Mutual aid networks—ranging from food banks to pop-up legal clinics—are active across the Lower Ninth Ward, Tremé, and Central City. Organizers and residents alike spoke repeatedly of the importance of “rebuilding hope,” not just through bricks and mortar but through cultural continuity. Traditional parades, second line dances, and social clubs continue, even when parade routes have had to be shortened due to road closures or neighborhood depopulation. These activities serve as powerful sources of affirmation and solidarity, and frequently function as informal organizing spaces as well.

#### Neighborhood Memory

Oral histories and daily conversations reveal a powerful attachment to place and a refusal to allow losses—of people, homes, or landmarks—to define community identity. Participants frequently returned to the stories of “what was,” ensuring that collective memory endures even when physical markers have disappeared. Some meetings included active efforts to record elders’ stories or to display old photographs at public events, making neighborhood memory a living and communal process.

### Neighborhood Identity

#### Traditions and Change

Despite the outmigration of longtime residents and loss of established businesses, block associations, festivals, and neighborhood rituals continue to hold communities together. These traditions have been adapted, rather than abandoned; parade leaders described how routes bend to fit new realities, but rituals and music remain central anchors of public life. The cultivation of these traditions is often a deliberate response to the threat of erasure, with organizers stating explicitly their commitment to “holding the line” on culture.

#### Narratives of Place

Across all neighborhoods, stories of resilience, renewal, and struggle form the core of local identity. Residents interpret change in ways that integrate both loss and possibility, asserting an enduring claim to the city even as landscapes shift around them. These localized narratives—expressed in conversation, celebration, and advocacy—help mediate the tensions between displacement and belonging, and serve as central themes in studies of urban resistance and identity formation.
