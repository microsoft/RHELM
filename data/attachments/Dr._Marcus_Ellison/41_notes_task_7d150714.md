# Comprehensive Reflexive Daily Log – October 11, 2024

---

## Overview

This log offers a detailed account and critical reflection on my academic, professional, and personal activities throughout October 11, 2024. Each entry captures not just what occurred, but my motivations, emotional responses, and the broader social contexts or theories that shaped my experience. By weaving together daily events with ongoing sociological themes and professional insights, I aim to document both growth in practice and the meaning found in routine.

---

## 6:30–7:30 AM: Morning Run in Central Park with Jamal Rivers

I started my day with a morning run in Central Park, meeting up with my colleague Jamal Rivers, who is an urban sociologist. We completed a 7.2 km loop over about 55 minutes, taking in the crisp autumn air and the ever-changing scenery of the park. Running this early, I always notice the diversity of people sharing the space—dog walkers, fellow runners, elderly couples, school groups—which sparks both a feeling of connection and curiosity.

During the run, Jamal and I found ourselves deep in conversation about urban public spaces. Our talk naturally gravitated towards Henri Lefebvre’s concept of the “Right to the City,” lingering on how parks like Central Park become battlegrounds for issues of access, inclusion, and gentrification. I was reminded again how seemingly ordinary activities like jogging can enact and reinforce collective urban identities. We also reflected on ongoing tensions over belonging and entitlement, especially as rising costs of living push out long-term residents. Jamal brought up Don Mitchell’s work on contested public space, emphasizing the need for urban policies to protect equitable access for all.

As we cooled down, I felt both the physical benefits and a sense of renewal from inhabiting and discussing public space so early in the day. These runs have become more than exercise—they offer a way to witness and participate in the ongoing story of the city, while exchanging ideas with someone who shares my passion for urban justice.

## 8:05–8:45 AM: Breakfast Preparation – Smoked Salmon & Avocado Toast

Back home, I prepared breakfast, focusing on ingredients I had intentionally sourced the previous weekend: multigrain sourdough from Pain d’Avignon, wild-caught smoked salmon certified by the Marine Stewardship Council, organic avocados from the NYC Greenmarket, and microgreens from a GrowNYC farmer. I sliced and toasted the bread, mashed avocado with lemon and dill, and layered it with silky smoked salmon before topping it off with microgreens and cracked black pepper. These simple steps let me start the day creatively but also with purpose—food choices, for me, are never just about nutrition.

As I sat down to eat, I thought about how the dish merged tradition and trend—Ashkenazi lox traditions reimagined through today’s cosmopolitan health-food lens. Eating in this way, I actively support regional economies and sustainable practices, reducing environmental impacts while connecting with food justice ideals. Of course, I’m also aware of the contradictions—how dishes like these, rooted in specific ethnic narratives, have become markers of urban gentrification and wellness trends. I felt a mix of satisfaction from sourcing and preparing the meal myself, and some ambivalence about how even something as basic as breakfast can become a site of cultural and economic negotiation.

## 10:00 AM–12:30 PM: Digitization of Symposium Notes

The rest of the morning was devoted to digitizing notes from last week’s symposium, “Cities in Crisis: Rethinking the Urban Commons.” I used Obsidian, a markdown-based app that I love for its efficiency in linking and tagging ideas. As I worked, major themes from the symposium came into sharper focus: the threat of increasing privatization of shared spaces, the creative resilience of mutual aid networks, and the ways digital technologies now facilitate collective urban advocacy.

Converting handwritten notes to digital form is never just a technical task for me. I constantly find myself making new connections as I tag excerpts (#urbancommons, #privatization, #mutualaid), quote particularly resonant lines, and cross-link ideas for future writing. This process also made me more aware of the privilege tied to such tools—having reliable access to digital platforms and the literacy to use them is not a given for everyone.

As I shared annotated notes with symposium colleagues, I realized how much this kind of networked scholarship accelerates collaborative thinking. The work reaffirmed theoretical conversations from the symposium about resisting both the enclosure of physical urban spaces and the privatization of knowledge itself.

---

## 2:00–2:45 PM: Afternoon Op-Ed Planning Call

After lunch, I joined a planning call with Dr. Carla Myles, policy analyst Roberto Reyes, and community organizer Maya Patel. Our goal was to map out a co-authored op-ed on the impact of urban heat islands, especially on communities that face disproportionate climate burdens.

We discussed how to structure the op-ed, exchanged thoughts on balancing technical findings with accessible, human-centered storytelling, and brainstormed appropriate venues for publication—City & State NY and The Guardian stood out. Maya volunteered to collect community vignettes, Roberto took on the first draft of translating technical insights, and Dr. Myles agreed to build a policy impact map.

Throughout the conversation, I was constantly reminded of Michael Burawoy’s idea of “public sociology”—the imperative to connect academic research with real civic engagement. The call was a practical exercise in flattening hierarchies: listening to on-the-ground organizers, co-authoring genuinely shared knowledge, and challenging the academic tendency toward extractive or top-down relationships. I finished the call feeling energized by the possibility of creating work that is both rigorous and accessible, shaped by and for the communities most affected.

## 4:30–5:25 PM: Jazz Piano Improvisation

In the late afternoon, I set aside time for solo jazz improvisation on the piano—a ritual I rely on when I need to reconnect with myself. I played around with ii–V–I progressions in multiple keys, exploring modal ideas influenced by Coltrane’s “Giant Steps” and harmonic voicings inspired by Thelonious Monk and the ECM label’s distinctive sound. I found myself alternating between structured drills and spontaneous melodic experimentation, layering in polyrhythms and lush extensions.

As I played, the worries and deadlines of the day faded into the background, replaced by the kind of “flow” that Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi describes. Improvisation always reminds me of the creative risks and discoveries at the heart of both art and research—knowing the theory, yes, but being willing to depart from it, to chase unexpected directions. In that moment, I felt both focused and free, my mind quieted by the process. The music became not just an escape, but a metaphor for the iterative, exploratory nature of academic life.

## 7:00–8:30 PM: Dinner – Fall Vegetable Stew (Communal, Local Sourcing)

Dinner this evening was a communal affair. I cooked a hearty fall stew using fresh ingredients from the Union Square Greenmarket: butternut squash, fingerling potatoes, carrots, turnips, kale, and thyme. The process felt grounding—dicing, caramelizing onions and garlic, deglazing with local white wine, simmering everything together, and topping with toasted pumpkin seeds. As the aroma filled the apartment, I invited a few neighbors along with two academic colleagues to join me. We pulled together extra chairs and lingered around the table, sharing stories about the changing city, our families, and even snippets from recent projects.

Sharing this meal carried a special satisfaction. The food itself embodied my values—support for local farms, minimizing environmental impact, and resisting the impersonal nature of industrial food chains. More importantly, the act of gathering to eat felt like a small act of community-building. The conversations were richer because of the diversity around the table, reminding me of Simmel’s theory about the meal as a site for social integration. In moments like these, I find a sense of conviviality and solidarity that’s increasingly rare in such a fast-paced, fragmented city life.
