# Hosting a Pescatarian Lunch Gathering in New York City – June 22, 2024

Bringing friends together around good food has always been one of my favorite ways to build community. Today, I hosted a pescatarian lunch in New York City—drawing inspiration from the city’s vibrant summer markets, local producers, and my own love for fresh, seasonal cooking. Here’s a detailed account of what I prepared, how I sourced each ingredient, and the ways I tried to create an inclusive, warm atmosphere for all my guests.

---

## Menu Highlights

### Mediterranean Salad  
I started with a big Mediterranean salad, making the most of heirloom tomatoes, which are just coming into their prime in late June. I chose crisp, leafy greens and added English cucumbers, juicy tomatoes, and plump Kalamata olives. I finished it off with tangy local feta from the Greenmarket—although I know some guests prefer goat cheese or vegan cheese, so I set those options out too. My dressing was a simple blend of peppery olive oil and fresh lemon juice, brightened up with handfuls of chopped parsley and oregano. The herbs really brought out the flavors of the produce, and I love being able to say most ingredients were picked just hours earlier.

#### Sourcing & Seasonality  
Union Square Greenmarket has rows of produce stalls overflowing with greens and tomatoes by late June, making it easy to buy everything fresh and local. I found some beautiful bunches of parsley and oregano at the market, and it felt good supporting regional farmers.

---

### Spicy Asparagus and Smoked Trout Tart  
One of the centerpieces of the lunch was a savory tart filled with slender, sweet asparagus spears from Gorzynski Ornery Farm and creamy local eggs. I layered in smoked trout from a small Brooklyn smokehouse—its depth paired so well with the asparagus. A pinch of cayenne provided just enough gentle heat, and spring herbs brought a light, fragrant note to the custard. The tart came out bubbling and bronzed on top, and I served it slightly warm. I’m always amazed how a simple tart can steal the show on the table.

#### Sourcing & Seasonality  
Asparagus is still at its best in mid-to-late June in New York. I picked up eggs and cream from local dairy vendors, and the smoked trout came from Acme Smoked Fish, a favorite for quality and sustainability.

---

### Herbed Couscous  
For the couscous, I wanted a side that tasted bright and green. I fluffed up the grains and folded in generous handfuls of mint, dill, parsley, and a touch of tarragon, all freshly picked at the market. I tossed in tender spring onions and sweet, just-shelled peas. Finished with lemon zest and a trickle of olive oil, this dish felt like the very essence of late spring. It's also perfect for a gathering—easy to make ahead, and it holds up beautifully at room temperature.

#### Sourcing & Seasonality  
The cousin of tabbouleh, this couscous came together with herbs sourced from several Greenmarket vendors specializing in organic produce. With so much greenery in season, I had fun mixing varieties to adjust the flavors.

---

### Grilled Eggplant  
Early eggplants started showing up at the markets last week, so I decided to highlight them simply. I sliced them thick, marinated with olive oil, slivered garlic, and more fresh herbs, then char-grilled each piece until the skins were smoky and the flesh was meltingly soft. A dusting of sea salt and black pepper was all the seasoning they needed. Arranging them on a big platter, I felt a little thrill seeing those deep purple stripes from the grill.

#### Sourcing & Seasonality  
June marks the start of local eggplant season in the city. Supporting farmers at Union Square Greenmarket not only ensures freshness but also helps reduce the carbon footprint of my meal.

---

### Iced Herbal Tea  
On a warm, muggy June afternoon, nothing beats a cold glass of homemade herbal tea. I brewed a refreshing mix of fresh mint, lemon balm, and chamomile—herbs that grow abundantly in local rooftop gardens and are easy to find at the market. After steeping, I chilled the tea in the fridge, then served it over ice with sprigs of herbs for garnish. I kept a jar of Andrew’s honey on the side for anyone who liked a touch of sweetness.

#### Sourcing & Seasonality  
All the herbs came straight from the Union Square Greenmarket, freshly bundled and beautifully aromatic. Supporting local beekeepers also means a more sustainable, environmentally-friendly sweetener.

---

### Smoked Trout Pâté  
To accompany the bread and crackers, I made a creamy smoked trout pâté. I blended the fish with crème fraîche, chopped herbs, lemon zest, and a pinch of black pepper. It’s silky, flavorful, and always a crowd-pleaser, especially when eaten with warm bread. It's also a sly way to spotlight regional aquaculture and artisanal techniques.

#### Sourcing & Seasonality  
I picked up the smoked trout from the Catskills via the market, trusting their quality and environmental stewardship. The crème fraîche came from Tonjes Dairy, also at the Greenmarket.

---

### Heirloom Tomatoes  
There’s something magical about a perfect summer tomato. I picked up several varieties—big, juicy red Brandywines, golden Striped Germans, and some tiny, sweet Cherry tomatoes. Sliced thickly and simply adorned with flaky sea salt and a drizzle of olive oil, they needed little else. Their vibrant colors brought the table to life.

#### Sourcing & Seasonality  
Local tomato season is just beginning to ramp up mid-June at the Greenmarket, and the farmers were eager to show off their new crops.

---

### Ramps  
While ramp season is usually winding down by late June, I managed to get a small jar of pickled ramps from a specialty forager stall. Their bold, garlicky flavor was perfect scattered over the tart and salad, giving a little spark to the meal. I set a dish of them on the table so guests could help themselves.

#### Sourcing & Seasonality  
Pickled ramps preserve a fleeting spring delicacy just a little longer—worth hunting down for a special meal.

## How I Moved Through My Day: Prep Timeline

Making big meals for people is one of my favorite rituals. Here’s how my day unfolded as I brought everything together.

**7:30 – 8:00 am**  
I started the morning with a quick jog in Central Park. Moving my body early gave me energy and focus—plus it’s a great way to clear my mind before a busy cooking day.

**8:30 – 9:30 am**  
After stretching and breakfast, I double-checked my guest list and sent a few last-minute texts to confirm dietary needs. I always want everyone to feel welcome, so that sort of communication is key.

**9:30 – 11:00 am**  
I grabbed my tote bags and headed to Union Square Greenmarket. Wandering through the market is such a joy—smelling fresh basil, chatting with farmers, and tasting a few sweet snap peas right from the pod. I picked up all the produce, eggs, trout, bread, and more; the conversations with vendors make these groceries feel even more special.

**11:00 – 11:30 am**  
Back home, I put on some easy-listening music, washed all my veggies, and laid out ingredients on the counter, feeling a surge of anticipation for the afternoon ahead.

**11:30 am – 12:00 pm**  
I set a pot to boil for herbal tea, steeped the mix of mint, lemon balm, and chamomile, and tucked the fresh-brewed tea into the fridge to chill.

**12:00 – 12:30 pm**  
Next, I shaped the pastry for my tart (sometimes I make it, sometimes I buy from Orwasher’s, depending on my mood and how much time I have). I salted the eggplant slices, letting them rest so they'd fry up tender.

**12:30 – 1:30 pm**  
I prepped the Mediterranean salad, rolling up and slicing the herbs, chopping tomatoes and cucumbers, and assembling the dressing. After that, I started prepping the custard for the tart—whisking eggs and cream, folding in herbs and asparagus, and layering in the smoked trout.

**1:30 – 2:30 pm**  
While the tart baked, I made couscous and stirred in the fresh green herbs, peas, and zippy spring onions, then set it aside to cool.

**2:30 – 3:00 pm**  
I turned my attention to the smoked trout pâté, blending everything together and popping it into the fridge. Then, I fired up my grill pan and started charring the eggplant, loving the smoky aroma that drifted through the apartment.

**3:00 – 3:30 pm**  
I sliced up the heirloom tomatoes, doused them with olive oil, and scattered some sea salt over top. I prepped the ramps too, arranging the pickled ones in a little dish for the table.

**3:30 – 4:00 pm**  
I set the table family-style, laying out big platters, jars of honey, and pitchers of herbal tea. I always try to make things look welcoming but unfussy—people seem to relax more that way.

**4:00 – 4:30 pm**  
A few finishing touches: soft music, candles for atmosphere, and making sure all serving utensils were close at hand.

**4:30 – 5:00 pm**  
With everything ready, I took a few quiet moments to reflect, breathe deeply, and prepare to shift from cook to host.

**5:00 – 5:15 pm: Guest Arrival**  
As guests arrived, I greeted everyone at the door with a glass of iced herbal tea. Since a couple were new to the city, I made introductions all around, then invited folks to help plate the salad—that always works as an easy icebreaker and gets everyone involved from the start.

**Throughout Lunch**  
I served everything on large sharing platters, encouraging people to pass dishes, go back for seconds, and help themselves as they pleased. I put extra bread, tea, and spreads within easy reach, so no one ever had to ask for more.

## Inclusivity, Community, and the Power of Sharing Food

Every element of the lunch was designed to foster inclusivity and a sense of belonging. I always want my table to feel open to everyone, so I focused on a pescatarian menu that could be tweaked for different preferences and dietary needs. I made sure to have gluten-free options and substitutes for dairy or fish, labeling each dish and happily talking with anyone who had concerns.

Hosting family-style meant nobody had to worry about being left out or singled out—platters can be adjusted and passed; people can build their own plates. During the meal, I swapped stories with my guests about where the trout was smoked or which farm grew the tomatoes, weaving a connection between our little gathering and the broader local food community.

I also noticed how inviting people to help assemble the salad broke down social barriers quickly. Even guests who didn’t know each other soon started chatting over kitchen tasks or making recommendations for what to try next. There’s something inherently warm about sharing tasks and stories as you prepare and serve food together.

Research shows—and my own experience confirms—that eating together enriches friendships and can even foster new ones. When the food on the table reflects the season, the place, and the people around it, I sense a real, tangible feeling of togetherness. Meals like this build community in a city that often feels rushed or anonymous.

I know from urban living that these moments of connection are deeply valuable, whether you’re welcoming newcomers or simply strengthening old ties. Cooking with local, seasonal ingredients and opening my home on a Saturday afternoon helps turn strangers into friends and friends into a kind of family.
