Discussion Summary

1. Project Overview and Context

Dr. Ellison began the session by reiterating the project's central aim: to comprehensively examine evolving patterns in urban housing inequality, with a special emphasis on eviction dynamics in Harlem. He emphasized the importance of rigorously integrating quantitative eviction trends with the qualitative experiences of affected residents. Dr. Grant agreed, stressing that the lived realities captured in community narratives are essential for framing actionable recommendations and for ensuring research findings are directly relevant to policy and advocacy work.

2. Quantitative Data: Current Eviction Trends and Disparities

The team conducted a detailed review of eviction data spanning 2020 to 2024, focusing on Harlem and comparable neighborhoods. Key findings included:

  • Chronological Trends:
    During 2020 and 2021, formal eviction rates declined precipitously as a direct result of COVID-19 moratoria at both state and federal levels. In Harlem, these measures led to an almost complete cessation of court-processed evictions. However, following the expiration of these protections in late 2022, the area experienced a sharp resurgence in eviction filings, surpassing pre-pandemic levels by the second quarter of 2023. Notably, there was an immediate spike in filings coinciding with the end of legislative protections.

  • Demographic Disparities:
    The data highlighted persistent inequities, with Black and Hispanic households facing eviction at rates considerably above the city average. Single-parent families—particularly those led by women—and multigenerational households were shown to be especially vulnerable. Additionally, recent data indicated that undocumented tenants and newly arrived immigrants have experienced elevated rates of both formal eviction and unreported, informal displacement, reflecting the heightened risks faced by those with precarious legal or economic status.

  • Geographical and Process Anomalies:
    In several blocks within Harlem’s rapidly gentrifying areas, formal eviction rates appeared lower than expected. However, qualitative interviews revealed these areas are marked by significant “soft evictions,” such as unregulated rent hikes or landlord harassment, resulting in tenants leaving without going through formal court processes. The team also noted an unexpected reduction in formal filings in some public housing developments in late 2023, which appears linked to targeted legal aid programs implemented in those locations.

3. Qualitative Insights and Sociological Interpretation

Building on the quantitative analysis, Dr. Grant led a discussion of the interview and focus group data collected from affected Harlem residents:

  • Community Experiences:
    Participants described the profound psychological and social impacts of ongoing housing instability. For youth and elderly individuals, these effects often manifested as heightened stress, anxiety, and disruptions to educational or social networks. Many tenants expressed a deep sense of powerlessness, driven in part by confusing legal proceedings and a lack of accessible information regarding their rights. Reports of intimidation, threats, and sudden rent increases were common, compounding the precarity many already faced.

  • Structural and Intersectional Factors:
    The team applied frameworks of structural inequality and intersectionality to interpret these patterns. Both Dr. Ellison and Dr. Grant emphasized that systemic disinvestment in minority neighborhoods, the legacy of racially exclusionary housing policies, and ongoing economic marginalization collectively form the backdrop against which these eviction patterns unfold. The intersection of race, gender, and immigration status emerged as particularly significant, underscoring the need for interventions that address overlapping vulnerabilities rather than isolated risk factors.

4. Ethical Considerations and Advocacy

Several ethical imperatives guided the group’s discussion of next steps. There was unanimous agreement on the importance of protecting participant confidentiality and rigorously anonymizing qualitative data in any reporting. The team also recognized an obligation to actively use their findings to inform policy change and support community-based advocacy. Expanding the availability of legal resources and platforms for tenant voices in policymaking emerged as immediate advocacy priorities.

Next Steps

  1. Synthesize Data for Initial Manuscript:
    Integrate validated datasets and qualitative analyses to draft an initial results manuscript targeting journals focused on urban studies and housing inequality.

  2. Strengthen Community Partnerships:
    Initiate collaboration with Harlem-based legal aid offices and tenant associations, both to validate interpretation of findings and to jointly craft policy recommendations.

  3. Engage External Stakeholders:
    Distribute a brief summary of key findings to city policymakers, relevant community organizations, and public defenders to solicit input and identify further research and advocacy needs.

  4. Grant Application Planning:
    Identify and prepare to apply for funding opportunities with the National Science Foundation (NSF), the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD), and philanthropic funders committed to urban equity. Assemble supporting evidence and narrative materials for these proposals.

  5. Maintain High Ethical Standards:
    Reconfirm full compliance with IRB protocols and ethical guidelines throughout all aspects of research, with particular attention to protecting participant confidentiality and informed consent.

  6. Set Forthcoming Agenda:
    Next meeting will include the review of updated datasets, refined thematic memos, and draft abstracts, along with discussion of feedback from community and policy stakeholders.
