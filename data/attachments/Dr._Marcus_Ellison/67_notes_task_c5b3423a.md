## 2. Location and Environment

Today’s session took place in the private yoga studio within my home, a space I value for its sense of tranquility and seclusion. The room itself is generously sized, with uncluttered, smooth wooden flooring that lends itself perfectly to grounded practices. As the sun rose, natural morning light filtered softly through large sheer curtains, gentle enough to soothe the senses rather than overstimulate. For additional comfort, warm LED ambient lights gently illuminated corners, ensuring a cozy atmosphere without harsh glare.

The silence in the room was near total, shielded from external noise—a deliberate choice to foster immersion. Low-volume instrumental music played quietly in the background, subtle enough to promote a sense of sanctuary and soften mental chatter without drawing attention. To maintain physical comfort, I set the temperature to a consistent 22°C (72°F) using a nearly silent electric heater. Along one wall, I arranged several potted plants—pothos, snake plants, and a peace lily—which added a gentle touch of nature and improved the freshness of the air. Complementing this, a diffuser released a faint aroma of lavender, rounding out the sensory environment with a note of calm.

Cultivating this environment has become integral to my restorative practice. The thoughtful combination of natural light, gentle sound, modulated temperature, greenery, and subtle aromatherapy creates a haven for deep relaxation, mirroring guidelines for restorative yoga settings [1]. Every sensory element is meant to withdraw distractions and bring attention inward, allowing for authentic rest and mindful awareness.

## 4. Routine Breakdown: Step-by-Step Sequence with Adaptations

The following sequence reflects both the traditional restorative yoga flow and Dr. Patel’s personalized modifications for my SI joint sensitivity and variable hip mobility. Each pose incorporated deliberate prop placement and awareness cues, supporting both structural safety and psychological ease.

| Step | Pose/Sequence                 | Time        | Description                                                                             | Adaptation                                        | Rationale                                                |
|------|------------------------------|-------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------|
| 1    | Seated Breath Awareness       | 5 min       | Cross-legged, eyes closed, hands on knees, focusing on slow abdominal breaths            | Folded blanket under pelvis for elevation         | Supports spinal neutrality and eases lower back tension  |
| 2    | Supported Child’s Pose        | 8 min       | Torso draped over bolster, arms forward, knees wide                                     | Blanket under ankles and knees, block for forehead | Relieves joint stress, fosters gentle spinal extension   |
| 3    | Reclined Bound Angle          | 10 min      | Lying on back, soles together, knees open and supported by blocks/bolsters              | Strap to maintain position, blocks under thighs    | Supports hips/knees, prevents overstretch, improves circulation |
| 4    | Supported Supine Twist        | 6 min/side  | Lying on back, knees over bolster to one side, arms in a T                              | Blanket between knees, block under lower back      | Protects lumbar spine, enables gentle rotation           |
| 5    | Legs-Up-The-Wall              | 10 min      | Legs vertically supported against wall, buttocks near wall, arms relaxed                | Blanket under sacrum, eye pillow for darkness      | Minimizes back compression, encourages venous return     |
| 6    | Supported Cat/Cow Flow        | 5 min       | Table-top on mat, hands on blocks, slow arching/rounding with breath                    | Blocks to elevate hands, reduce wrist strain       | Modulates spinal movement, reduces wrist discomfort      |
| 7    | Restorative Bridge            | 7 min       | Lying on back, sacrum resting on block/bolster, shoulders on mat                        | Extra blanket under head and shoulders             | Safeguards cervical spine, facilitates chest/hip opening |
| 8    | Savasana (Final Relaxation)   | 10 min      | Supine, limbs comfortably relaxed, eye pillow/napkin over eyes, blanket covering body   | Bolster under knees, arms wide                     | Maximizes full-body relaxation, supports parasympathetic shift |

During each pose, adjustments honored my current physical condition. For example, I paid particular attention to SI joint alignment, and used props to prevent even mild discomfort, enabling a sense of safety and true surrender. Adaptations also supported mindful engagement—a principle central to restorative yoga and echoed in recent empirical work on trauma-informed and accessible practice [2,3].

## 6. Personal Reflections

As the session unfolded, I was struck by the deepening of the mind-body connection. Sinking into stillness with the support of props, I could feel layers of muscular holding—patterns I hadn’t consciously noticed during the week—gradually unravel. The physical support of the blankets and bolsters felt like a physiological invitation to trust, to let go of habitual defense without any sense of risk. It was as if my body recognized the gentleness of the practice and responded in kind.

Sustaining present-moment awareness through long holds, particularly in poses like Supta Baddha Konasana, was sometimes challenging. Intrusive thoughts about upcoming lectures or deadlines would surface, tugging at my focus. Each time, I made a point to gently return my attention to the sound and rhythm of my breath, or the gentle pressure of the bolster beneath me. It became an exercise in resilience against mental agitation—an achievement that carries over to moments of stress away from the mat.

The discipline of restorative yoga provides more than physical release. I see it as a pedagogical model—teaching the skills of self-regulation, somatic literacy, and adaptability that are vital both in the classroom and in leadership roles [4]. Amidst the demands and urgency of academic and professional life, these practices are a deliberate act of resistance: making space for sustainable resilience and holistic well-being.

In reviewing contemporary research, I’m continually reminded that such approaches are evidence-based. Restorative yoga has been shown to enhance vagal tone, reduce perceived stress, and facilitate emotional regulation [3,4]. Dr. Patel’s individualized prop use is aligned with trauma-informed guidelines and best practices in making yoga deeply accessible. The positive shifts I experienced in both physiology and mental clarity underscore the reciprocal relationship between body-based restoration and psychological balance.

Today’s restorative yoga practice was a comprehensive experience of both structure and surrender. From curating an optimal sensory environment to integrating evidence-based adaptations, every element was chosen to support my specific needs. The resulting sense of deep rest, renewed clarity, and emotional steadiness highlighted the essential role that restorative yoga plays not just in injury prevention, but in fostering true resilience—within myself, my academic context, and the broader culture in which I work.
