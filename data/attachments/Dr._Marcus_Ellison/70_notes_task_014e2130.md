## 2. Basil-Lemon Spread: Experimental Attempt and Reflections

I wanted a new condiment for today’s lunch—a basil-lemon spread that would add brightness to both the salad and whole-grain bread. I approached it as a small recipe experiment, both for flavor and to document the science of the emulsification process.

### Ingredients (Yield: ~150ml)
- 1 packed cup fresh basil leaves
- 1/4 cup extra-virgin olive oil
- 2 tbsp fresh lemon juice
- 1 tsp lemon zest
- 1 small garlic clove
- 1/2 tsp salt
- 1/2 tsp Dijon mustard (emulsifier)
- Optional: 2 tbsp soaked cashews OR 1 tbsp Greek yogurt for a thicker, creamier texture

### Preparation Process

1. **Prepping Ingredients:**  
   I rinsed the basil thoroughly and patted the leaves dry to minimize extra water. I zested and juiced a fresh lemon, and peeled the garlic.

2. **Blending the Base:**  
   Into the food processor, I combined the basil, garlic, lemon juice and zest, and salt. After pulsing, the mixture looked vibrant and aromatic, if a bit coarse.

3. **Starting the Emulsion:**  
   With the processor running, I began to drizzle in the olive oil. About halfway through, the mix started to separate—the oil refused to blend in smoothly.

4. **Emulsifier Addition & Adjustment:**  
   I quickly added the Dijon mustard, hoping it would pull things back together. The emulsion was still weak, so I followed by blending in the soaked cashews, which did thicken the consistency. When it still seemed a little loose, I added a spoonful of Greek yogurt. That finally stabilized the texture, although it slightly dulled the basil’s sharpness.

5. **Final Taste & Storage:**  
   After adjusting a bit more lemon and salt, I transferred the spread to a glass jar. The flavor was fresh and tangy with subtle creaminess, but the texture—while spreadable—still showed some slight oil separation over time.

### Scientific Notes: Emulsification Observations

This batch highlighted the challenge of achieving a stable emulsion when working with ingredients like basil (which has significant water content) and direct acids from lemon. Adding the mustard after the oil had already started to separate made it hard to recover a perfectly smooth result. Cashews and yogurt both helped, but changed the overall richness and mellowed the flavor.

- **Emulsification works best if the emulsifier (mustard or yogurt) is added at the very beginning** with the water and acid before introducing the oil. This helps tiny droplets to suspend evenly before phase separation starts.
- In this attempt, the combination of aggressive mechanical blending and delayed addition of the emulsifier seemed to break the initial emulsion, making recovery difficult.
- Next time, I’ll try pulsing everything except the olive oil and, crucially, **adding the emulsifier earlier**, then slowly drizzling in oil to monitor emulsion stability.

### In-Process Adjustments Recorded:

1. **Paused oil:** When separation started, I stopped pouring oil and switched my focus to adding the mustard.
2. **Added soaked cashews:** These helped bulk up and stabilize the spread, but softened the herbal taste.
3. **Included yogurt:** Texture improved, though the yogurt’s tang rounded things off.  
4. **Comparison baseline:** In hindsight, the initial water content in basil may be too high; partially drying the leaves might help next time.

### Reflection and Next Steps

This process underscored how delicately balanced emulsified sauces can be. For the next trial, I will:
- Start with the emulsifier blended in alongside the basil, lemon, and garlic before introducing any oil.
- Add oil in a finer, slower drizzle, monitoring closely for signs of separation.
- Consider briefly air-drying basil leaves to reduce excess water content—and see how that affects overall flavor intensity.
- Photograph each stage and time-stamp them in Notion to improve troubleshooting and pattern recognition.
