# Comprehensive Travel Itinerary & Daily Routine Log: New Orleans Fieldwork (Beginning July 10, 2024)

## Overview

This report outlines my fieldwork itinerary in New Orleans, starting July 10, 2024. As an Associate Professor of Sociology focused on urban change, music cultures, and community resilience, I planned a daily routine that not only advances my research goals but also integrates wellness, reflexivity, and responsibility in the city’s changing social landscape. My intention is to immerse myself in local routines, build genuine relationships, and maintain ethical rigor throughout each step—whether in interviews, casual conversations over lunch, or evenings spent in storied music clubs.

The following sections detail my accommodations, transportation strategies, day-by-day schedule for the first five days, research milestones, meal planning with sociological context, engagement with evening cultural venues, ethics in fieldwork, and opportunities for community connection.

---

## 1. Accommodations

### Chosen Airbnb: Faubourg Marigny

After reviewing several options, I selected a private one-bedroom cottage in Faubourg Marigny, “Historic Creole Cottage, Walk to Frenchmen St. & River.” The cottage’s location—just a few blocks from vibrant Frenchmen Street and close to key field sites—makes it ideal for participant observation, spontaneous walking interviews, and note-taking in real time.

The comfort and amenities were critical for practical and health reasons. I prioritized high-speed WiFi for transcription and virtual meetings, a functional kitchen to safely handle my food allergies, and allergy-friendly linens. The workspace on the patio quickly became my favorite spot for morning writing and reflection.

The Marigny’s unique blend of historic architecture, lively street life, and colorful resident mix allows me to observe shifting neighborhood dynamics—particularly ongoing discussions about authenticity, gentrification, and cultural preservation. Living within walking or biking distance of community centers and music venues means that I remain embedded in the community rhythms I’m studying, reducing reliance on cars or ride-shares.

---

## 2. Transportation Approach

### Daily Mobility and Research Practice

Getting around on foot is not only practical—most of my interviews, markets, and venues are 10 to 30 minutes away—but it also enhances my fieldwork. Walking allows me to absorb nuances: the morning rush of Crescent Park joggers, idle sidewalk conversations, changing patterns in housing, and the audible transition from day to night as buskers filter onto Frenchmen Street.

I arranged for a bike rental (Blue Bikes NOLA), which gives me flexibility to reach outlying neighborhoods like Bywater, Treme, or St. Roch. It’s a sustainable choice that also opens up brief, informal chats at street corners or stoops that I might easily miss in a car.

When interviews run late or storms roll in, I rely on Uber or Lyft to ensure safe, timely travel home. Public transit—especially the RTA streetcars—offers a window into daily life and gives me a reason to strike up conversations with fellow passengers, always being mindful of privacy and ethical boundaries in my note-taking.

Throughout my travels, I remain acutely aware of the ethical implications of data collection in public spaces and prioritize consent and transparency, especially in informal or overheard interactions.

## 3. Daily Routine: Field Notes from the First Five Days

Below, I detail the first five days of my fieldwork, blending personal insights, methodological choices, and sociological motivations. Each day is structured to balance research goals, self-care, and genuine engagement with the complexities of everyday life in New Orleans.

---

### Day 1: Wednesday, July 10, 2024

**6:30–8:00 am:**  
I began my day with gentle yoga on the cottage patio, then set out for a walk along Crescent Park Trail via the Piety Street entrance. The city was just waking up: joggers, ferry commuters, and dog-walkers crossed my path. I took several pauses to jot down my first impressions, sketching a neighborhood map and noting subtle demographic shifts in housing style.

**8:30 am:**  
Over coffee, I mapped out the neighborhood, making it a point to capture how spaces felt in motion rather than from afar. It struck me how daily routines intersect and layer over historic infrastructure—how the cobblestones and shotgun houses both anchor and unsettle the present.

**10:00 am:**  
My first interview was with Dr. Dayna Harris, an Urban Affairs scholar at Tulane. We met at Who Dat Coffee Café (2401 Burgundy St.), and our lively discussion centered on community resilience and the city’s ongoing recovery from post-Katrina gentrification. I kept the recorder out of sight after receiving her consent, but it felt like a genuine, candid exchange.

**11:30 am:**  
Taking a break over my notes, I reflected on my presence as a researcher—what assumptions was I bringing about “authenticity”? I wrote freely about my anxieties of being an outsider and my responsibility to portray community voices with care.

**12:30 pm:**  
Lunch at Coop’s Place was my first true taste of the city. Sitting at a communal table, I ordered blackened redfish with okra and tomato salad, taking care to ask about potential allergens. The mix of regulars and tourists at the bar led to an impromptu conversation about the city’s best music venues, and I found myself invited to a brass show later that week.

**2:30 pm:**  
I spent the early afternoon in transcription and annotation, syncing audio and written notes while my first impressions were still fresh.

**4:00 pm:**  
A late-afternoon walk through Chartres & Esplanade led me to pockets of street art and spontaneous musical performances. Each corner revealed a new layer to the city’s informal economy—painters on one block, a poet across the street, brass musicians attracting both tourists and neighbors.

**7:00 pm:**  
Dinner at Buffa’s Lounge was simple—shrimp and grits—but rich in sociological observation. I noted how the clientele at Buffa’s—mostly working-class locals—created a distinctly different energy from my lunch experience.

**8:30–11:00 pm:**  
An evening at Spotted Cat Music Club transported me into the heart of the city’s live jazz culture. I found myself shifting between observer and participant—dancing when invited, scribbling notes in the restroom, and occasionally chatting with the saxophonist between sets about their journey to New Orleans.

**11:15 pm:**  
Before bed, I made sure to follow my allergy regimen—saline nasal spray and medication—then reviewed the day’s notes before drifting off, already anticipating tomorrow’s sunrise walk.

**Reflexive Insight:**  
Capturing both casual and structured moments felt essential; the act of note-taking, at times, seemed to dance on the edge of performance art itself. I reminded myself repeatedly to prioritize transparency and always ask for direct consent when recording or quoting anyone.

---

### Day 2: Thursday, July 11, 2024

**7:00 am:**  
Rose early for a sunrise walk from the French Market to Crescent Park. Vendors were setting up their stalls, and port workers unloaded the day’s shipments. The atmosphere was both industrious and relaxed. I wore a mask as the pollen count felt high, but I didn’t let it slow me down.

**8:00 am:**  
Attended a morning yoga class at Wild Lotus Yoga, led by a local instructor. Practicing alongside regulars gave me a window into the community’s wellness rituals—a reminder that self-care here is often collective.

**9:30 am:**  
Beignets and café au lait at Café du Monde is a tourist staple, but by 9:30, I counted nearly as many locals as out-of-towners. I paid attention to table negotiations between groups and staff, jotting down notes on how “insider” and “outsider” roles blurred over powdered sugar.

**10:30 am:**  
Interviewed Ms. Joelle Baptiste, a community organizer from the Faubourg Marigny Improvement Association, at The Elysian Bar. We discussed housing politics and urban renewal, and her stories of resistance—some hopeful, some jaded—echoed in my mind long after.

**12:00 pm:**  
I sat in a quiet corner to synthesize the morning: merging my vendor observations with themes from Joelle’s interview, drawing a tangled concept map that, to me, symbolized the city’s ever-evolving identity.

**1:00 pm:**  
Lunch at Central Grocery & Deli brought me into New Orleans’ Italian heritage. I sampled a classic muffuletta and spoke briefly with servers about their family roots and the sandwich’s history. The blend of flavors felt like a metaphor for the city’s cultural layering.

**2:00 pm:**  
Picked up my bike and cycled through St. Roch and Bywater, mapping shifts in graffiti, corner stores, and visible street activism.

**4:30 pm:**  
Downloaded and annotated my field photos—a practice that helps me slow down and catalog the emotional temperature of places, not just their visual details.

**7:00 pm:**  
Dinner at Dooky Chase’s Restaurant—jambalaya—immersed me in the Black-owned culinary tradition that played a deep role in civil rights. The energy in the dining room was palpable: stories, laughter, and the sense of continuity through food.

**8:30–10:00 pm:**  
The night concluded with live music at d.b.a. on Frenchmen Street. I observed the dance floor’s fluid social “rules” and how quickly locals embraced—and gently corrected—newcomers, including myself.

**10:30 pm:**  
Closed the day with a walk back, reflecting on how much my role oscillates between participant and observer, and the mental tightrope of both.

---

### Day 3: Friday, July 12, 2024

**6:45 am:**  
My day started with yoga and a quiet breakfast at my Airbnb. Intentions set for a packed schedule, I double-checked my allergy regimen.

**8:00 am:**  
Strolled down Esplanade Avenue to Louis Armstrong Park. The early hour meant I mostly encountered dog walkers and retired couples. I could feel the park’s historic weight—these were spaces shaped as much by protest and policing as by leisure.

**9:00 am:**  
Interviewed Mr. Jean-Luc Martin, director of the New Orleans Musicians Clinic, at StudioBE. Our conversation wound through the health challenges facing local artists and the social capital embedded in music scenes.

**10:30 am:**  
Stepped outside and recorded immediate reflections—how the affective energy of that interview lingered, how embodiment and voice shaped the narratives I was collecting.

**11:30 am:**  
Visited the bustling St. Roch Market, chatting with vendors from different backgrounds and observing how small commerce activities bind the neighborhood together.

**1:00 pm:**  
Lunch at Parkway Bakery & Tavern: a po’ boy sandwich, served up in one of the city’s most storied settings. As I ate, I listened to stories of union organizing and labor roots that still animate the community.

**2:30 pm:**  
Spent an hour organizing my digital notes and photo backups. If I’m honest, this is tedious but necessary—losing a day’s interviews or images would be an irretrievable loss.

**5:00 pm:**  
Made time for a bit of reading—recent articles on urban change in Southern cities—and let new ideas mingle with my fresh observations.

**7:30 pm:**  
Dinner at Coop’s Place (gumbo this time) allowed me to compare afternoon energy with the previous lunch crowd. The room’s tone was more relaxed, with families settling in for the night.

**9:00–11:00 pm:**  
Ended the evening at Preservation Hall. The performance felt tightly curated, and I found myself analyzing audience behavior as much as the music—what does “authenticity” mean when it’s policed so carefully?

**11:15 pm:**  
I wound down with my nightly allergy regimen, jotting late-night reflections on the day’s ethical quandaries and emotional highs.

### Day 4: Saturday, July 13, 2024

**7:00 am:**  
Started the weekend with gentle yoga and a mindful breakfast on the patio, attentive to how the street slowly filled with life—a daily rhythm I’ve come to cherish.

**8:30 am:**  
Took a walk along Elysian Fields to the Crescent Park Dog Run, documenting the morning routines not just of people, but also their pets, watching how informal dog-owner communities negotiate space.

**10:00 am:**  
My scheduled interview with Ms. Celeste Desiré, local artist and community researcher, took place in her vibrant studio on Frenchmen Street. We delved into themes of racial memory and creative resistance, her artwork serving as both subject and prompt.

**11:45 am:**  
During the conversation, I gifted Celeste a portrait I’d painted as a gesture of reciprocity. Sharing creative work in the field feels personal and transformative; it established trust and set the tone for future collaboration.

**12:30 pm:**  
Reflected on the ethics of gifting—what does it mean to foreground agency, and how might these moments shape the story I tell?

**1:00 pm:**  
Lunch at Lil Dizzy’s Café—red beans and rice—with families and regulars, all lively after a week of work. I watched young children care for their grandparents, and made note of intergenerational social ties sustained through shared meals.

**3:00 pm:**  
Spent the afternoon archiving, sorting field artifacts, and processing new consent agreements. The process allowed me to internalize the day’s themes, preparing me for the next round of field visits.

**5:00 pm:**  
Ventured out for a festival near the Marigny, loosely structured to allow for spontaneous stoop conversations and unexpected invitations.

**7:00 pm:**  
Dinner was an oyster po’ boy at Domilise’s—a well-loved fixture—where I observed family work patterns and the shifting presence of gender and LGBTQ labor in the kitchen.

**8:30–10:30 pm:**  
At Blue Nile, I enjoyed live brass (with a surprise bounce set), mingling with a crowd that included tourists, musicians, longtime locals, and first-timers. Every conversation seemed to reveal a new aspect of neighborhood life.

**11:00 pm:**  
A short, contemplative walk home—followed by allergy meds—closed out my Saturday.

---

### Day 5: Sunday, July 14, 2024

**8:00 am:**  
Yoga and meditation helped center my morning before diving into a new round of fieldwork.

**9:30 am:**  
Walked along St. Claude Avenue, attending open hours at St. Augustine Catholic Church. The echoes of gospel music and the presence of immigrant families told a story of faith and migration that paralleled the city’s ever-changing demographics.

**11:00 am:**  
Met Dr. Vanessa Gomez, Community Health Initiative lead from Xavier University, in a small office below the church. Our conversation dove deep into health inequality, with Dr. Gomez emphasizing the delicate network between churches, schools, and clinics in historically gentrifying neighborhoods.

**12:15 pm:**  
Captured a voice memo on the intersection of faith, health, and space, mapping the networks I’d observed during the morning’s service.

**1:00 pm:**  
Brunch at Lil Dizzy’s Café (chicken and andouille gumbo this time) reminded me how vital family meals are on Sundays—the air was alive with laughter, and etiquette between tables felt almost ritualized.

**3:00 pm:**  
Spent the early afternoon consolidating data, updating my “found object” log, and sorting photos from the week. These photos, paired with field notes, help me reflect on moments I might otherwise forget.

**5:00 pm:**  
Took a quiet hour to write in my research diary about challenges faced—moments of fatigue, struggles to access certain communities, my evolving identity as an “outsider-insider.”

**7:00 pm:**  
Dinner at Willie Mae’s Scotch House—smothered greens and fried chicken—felt like both a celebration and a sociological observation. The sense of camaraderie in the waiting line was as vivid as the meal itself; people shared post-storm rebuilding stories while hoping to snag a coveted seat.

**9:00 pm:**  
Headed over to BJ’s Lounge in Bywater for Cajun waltz night. The energy was authentic and unfiltered, with open-mic performances drawing everyone in, creating a space for communal storytelling.

**10:30 pm:**  
Ended the night with my allergy routine and a short session reading over my notes; another week of fieldwork lay ahead.

## 4. Significant Research Milestones

### Gift Exchange: Portrait to Celeste (July 13)

The act of gifting a portrait to Ms. Celeste Desiré, a local artist and collaborator, held personal and methodological weight. Not only did it help dissolve the transactional barrier between researcher and participant, but it also enabled us to co-create knowledge—her subsequent reflections on the gift gave me deeper insight into her motivations and sense of artistry.

### Data Integrity and Ethics

Maintaining daily digital backups became a non-negotiable routine; at the close of each evening, I sorted and de-identified data in anticipation of possible device loss. I spent every night reflecting on my emotional state and potential blind spots, scrutinizing how my own biases might creep into the analytic process. Every meal, music venue, and faith institution doubled as a site of careful, deliberate ethical decision-making, with informed consent forming the baseline for all recorded exchanges.

---

## 5. Meals as Windows into Social Life

Each meal played a dual role: nourishment and sociological investigation. At Coop’s Place, the mingling of local traditions with a bustling tourist presence gave me a front-row seat to the ongoing negotiation of authenticity. Meals at Lil Dizzy’s and Dooky Chase’s placed me in the center of Black-owned, intergenerational spaces where political conversation and community advocacy play out as naturally as Sunday family dinners.

I selected Domilise’s, Willie Mae’s, and Buffa’s not just for their culinary reputation, but for their distinctive flavors of working-class, multiracial, and gendered labor stories. Even quick snacks at Café du Monde or Central Grocery inspired brief conversations about heritage and place. These experiences continually reminded me that what’s on the table is always tied to much larger stories.

---

## 6. Evenings: Music Venues and Reflexive Practice

My time at Spotted Cat, Preservation Hall, Blue Nile, BJ’s Lounge, and d.b.a. confirmed what I suspected—the city’s music scene is as much about economic and racial negotiation as it is about entertainment. Each venue offered a slightly different social ecology: locals-only jazz, tightly curated nostalgia, welcoming family performances, or raucous working-class gatherings.

I took every opportunity for honest participant observation and reflective practice—whether ducking aside for quick notes, using my phone discreetly, or making time at the end of each night for a full memo. I wrestled with the realities of my own observer effect—wondering whether my note-taking itself became a kind of performance, and constantly revisiting IRB-approved protocols when documenting anything beyond formal interviews.

---

## 7. Fostering Community Engagement

The heart of my fieldwork is built on spontaneous, everyday exchanges—with hosts, baristas, fellow music lovers, ride-share drivers, and even other late-night walkers. I strike up conversations wherever possible, always identifying myself honestly and weaving those fresh perspectives into my anonymized field notes. These chance encounters often reveal as much about the city’s pulse as any formal interview.

---

## 8. Ethical Commitment

I treat informed consent not just as a box to tick, but as a continuous process—checking in often with participants and giving them opportunities to share their own feedback on how their stories are used. Reciprocity is a guiding principle, whether through knowledge sharing, public events, or the simple act of gifting. My allergy management routine is as much about self-care as it is about maintaining reliability and trust in the community. Daily digital backups and careful data security practices are non-negotiable.
