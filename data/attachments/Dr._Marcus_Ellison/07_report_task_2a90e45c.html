Abstract

This systematic review examines collaborative research led by Marcus Ellison and colleagues, as presented at the American Sociological Association (ASA) conference, focusing on the dynamics and contemporary realities of social inequality. The research integrates foundational theories of intersectionality, cultural capital, and structural barriers, employing both quantitative survey analysis and qualitative ethnographic methods. By combining stratified random sampling with targeted in-depth interviews, the team explored modern forms of inequality across dimensions of race, class, and geography. Analysis drew on multivariate statistics and rigorous thematic content analysis, with additional field insights gathered through observational notes made by Ellison during an Amtrak journey—shedding light on lived experiences of mobility and visibility. The findings reveal persistent disparities in access to resources, mobility, and narrative agency, highlighting the complex interplay between individual experiences and broader structural constraints. Looking ahead, the research aims to deepen the operationalization of intersectional effects and enhance participatory approaches to advance policy and advocacy efforts. These results provide new, evidence-based directions for sociological understanding and social change.

Background

Over the past several decades, social inequality has remained a central focus within sociological research, evolving in form and scope as society changes. This study is built on key theories and frameworks, including:

  • Intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989): This framework recognizes that individuals inhabit multiple, overlapping identities—such as race, gender, and class—which together shape distinct experiences of privilege and oppression.
  • Bourdieu’s Theory of Capital: Bourdieu’s work underscores the lasting impact of economic, cultural, and social capital, explaining how advantages and disadvantages are reproduced across generations.
  • Spatial and Structural Inequality Theories: These approaches highlight the importance of place, patterns of mobility, and institutional barriers in shaping life trajectories.

Recent scholarship emphasizes how processes such as urbanization, racial stratification, shifting labor markets, and policy reforms continue to reshape the landscape of inequality ([1], [2]). Yet, while much attention has focused on outcomes and broad structural patterns, there is comparatively less understanding of how inequality is experienced in the transitions—such as during commutes, travel, or everyday movement between communities. These lived realities are crucial for understanding how inequality is both reproduced and challenged in contemporary society.

Methods

Study Design

We employed a convergent mixed-methods design to gain a comprehensive understanding of social inequality from both structural and experiential perspectives.

Quantitative Methodology

  • Sampling: Stratified random sampling was used to recruit participants from diverse backgrounds, ensuring that key demographic variables—race, class, and geography—were well represented in the sample.
  • Data Collection: Standardized surveys were administered to 1,250 respondents, measuring access to resources, perceptions of social mobility, and levels of trust in institutions.
  • Analysis: Multivariate regression and interaction models were applied to assess predictors of differences in key social outcomes.

Qualitative Methodology

  • Sampling: Purposeful sampling identified 35 individuals for in-depth interviews and ethnographic shadowing, with particular attention paid to contexts such as transit, public spaces, and community institutions.
  • Data Collection: Semi-structured interviews and participant observations were supplemented by detailed field notes. My own observations and reflections, especially those from an Amtrak train ride, provided unique perspectives on day-to-day experiences, micro-interactions, spatial positioning, and informal conversations.
  • Analysis: Thematic content analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006) and systematic memo-coding procedures were used to identify emergent themes and patterns in the data.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB), ensuring that ethical standards were upheld throughout. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and strict protocols were followed to protect anonymity and confidentiality.

Findings

Analysis to date highlights several interconnected themes, informed by both the statistical data and the qualitative insights gained from interviews and ethnographic observation—including specific reflections from my Amtrak journey.

Core Themes

  1. Spatial and Social Mobility:
    Both physical mobility—such as the ability to traverse different neighborhoods or cities—and perceptions of social mobility, were highly stratified by both race and class. While riding the Amtrak, I observed distinct differences in how people used public space. Patterns emerged around who felt comfortable moving about the train, who was subject to ID checks or surveillance, and who formed informal groups. These experiences were often shaped by visible social markers, such as race or economic status, demonstrating how daily routines are structured by broader forces of inequality.

  2. Narrative Agency and Voice:
    Across interviews, respondents from marginalized backgrounds described feeling excluded from decision-making processes and reported limited influence within their communities. In transit, I overheard—and sometimes participated in—conversations about frustration with institutional systems and barriers to being heard. These exchanges reinforced how structural marginalization is experienced as a sense of voicelessness or invisibility, particularly in formal or bureaucratic settings.

  3. Resource Disparities:
    The quantitative survey data revealed persistent and significant gaps in access to fundamental resources, including healthcare, affordable housing, and quality education. These disparities were not abstract statistics: in our ethnographic work, participants described concrete struggles to navigate services, manage costs, and advocate for their needs. Stories of adaptation and resilience coexisted with accounts of chronic insecurity and stress.

  4. Intersectionality in Everyday Life:
    Many participants described facing multiple, intersecting sources of disadvantage—including race, gender, immigrant status, and disability. On the train and in subsequent interviews, I witnessed how group dynamics and everyday interactions often reflected these overlapping layers, leading to particularly acute experiences of discrimination or exclusion.

Observational Insights: Amtrak Field Notes

Drawing from my own experiences during the Amtrak ride, I found it impossible not to notice how patterns of “mobility anxiety,” “public visibility,” and “transit-based stigma” shaped the behaviors and comfort levels of passengers. For some, travel was merely a means to an end, but for others, it brought palpable tension or self-consciousness. Watching who was stopped by train staff, who chose isolated seats, and how conversations unfolded offered a real-time lens into how social divisions play out far from formal institutions.

Discussion

Our research demonstrates that social inequality is enacted—and felt—in both visible structural domains and subtle day-to-day encounters. While policy discussions often center on resource distribution and institutional reform, the lived realities revealed in this study underscore the importance of everyday mobility, agency, and voice. Intersectionality is not just a theoretical construct but a tangible part of how people experience the world, negotiate public spaces, and navigate opportunities or barriers.

Notably, insights gained from mobile ethnography—such as my Amtrak observations—proved critical for understanding how social divisions map onto movement through public space and transit environments. These micro-dynamics of surveillance, self-presentation, and social interaction offer valuable direction for future inquiry into how mobility shapes, and is shaped by, inequality.

Next Steps

To build on these insights, our ongoing and planned work will:

  • Enhance Analytical Depth: We will expand our quantitative models to rigorously test how specific intersectional identities shape experiences of inequality. The addition of participant diaries and longitudinal interviews will allow us to capture how these dynamics change over time.
  • Deepen Collaborative Ethnography: By bringing community partners directly into the research as co-investigators, we aim to better represent lived experiences and foster reciprocal relationships.
  • Promote Policy and Advocacy Engagement: Drawing from our findings, we will develop actionable policy briefs in partnership with local organizations and hold public symposiums for urban planners and transit officials, encouraging direct dialogue grounded in evidence.
  • Broaden Dissemination: Beyond academic publications, we are committed to sharing our work with advocacy groups and through public-facing summaries to ensure accessibility and real-world impact.
  • Foster Inclusive and Ethical Practices: We will continue to reflect critically on power dynamics in the research process and actively seek feedback from underrepresented groups, ensuring their perspectives inform the direction of our ongoing work.
