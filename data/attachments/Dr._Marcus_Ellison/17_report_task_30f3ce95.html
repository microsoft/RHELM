2. Key Discussion Points

2.1 Updates on Luis Martinez’s College Application Journey

Luis began the session by sharing recent developments in his college application process. He spoke candidly about his excitement regarding several acceptance letters, balanced by anxiety around making the right decision for his future. The conversation naturally touched on the broader context of pursuing higher education as a first-generation student. Dr. Ellison provided relevant sociological background, contextualizing Luis’s experiences within ongoing challenges faced by underrepresented students in college admissions. The group discussed how social capital—including access to mentors and supportive networks—can profoundly shape the experience of navigating educational pathways (referencing [1] and [2]).

Institutional resources and the varied impact of socioeconomic status on college access were addressed, and the attendees highlighted the importance of proactive support systems. The discussion emphasized that responding to these challenges requires not just emotional encouragement but targeted, culturally aware mentorship that recognizes and builds upon the unique strengths mentees bring to the process.

2.2 Proposal for Harlem Photo Walk Workshop

Luis then introduced an idea he has been developing: a photography workshop in Harlem aimed at engaging local youth in exploring and documenting their neighborhood. He described his passion for photography and noted how the project could foster both technical skills and community engagement. Attendees discussed how such place-based initiatives can promote collective identity and stimulate intergenerational dialogue, while also challenging one-sided, negative narratives often associated with Harlem ([2]). The potential of this workshop to blend sociological inquiry with creative expression adds depth and visibility to youth perspectives within the community, and aligns with best practices in participatory action for empowering young people.

To advance the proposal, the group agreed on collaboratively formalizing the event’s framework, incorporating participant input throughout the planning process, and ensuring that the workshop actively promotes inclusion and positive representation of Harlem residents.

2.3 Formalizing the Workshop and Community Outreach

Building on the momentum, Dr. Ellison committed to drafting a formal proposal for the Harlem Photo Walk Workshop. He will also initiate outreach with a local Harlem community center to identify potential partners and venues. The conversation highlighted the critical importance of ethical practice: securing informed consent from all youth participants, protecting privacy, and making event participation entirely voluntary and accessible.

The group reviewed ethical guidelines for youth-involved community programming ([2], [3]), with a focus on transparency, joint accountability, and mutual respect in partnerships. These measures are intended to model integrity and inclusiveness for all event participants.

The anticipated impact of the workshop extends beyond creative output. By offering youth tangible opportunities to shape their narratives and contribute to public perceptions of Harlem, the initiative aims to deepen civic engagement, foster resilience, and build a stronger sense of community agency. This approach reflects current research supporting youth participation in local projects as a driver for both individual and collective empowerment ([2], [3]).

4. Closing Remarks

Dr. Ellison closed the session by reiterating the group’s commitment to evidence-based and culturally responsive mentoring, referencing best practice frameworks such as the MENTOR National Mentoring Partnership’s “Elements of Effective Practice for Mentoring” ([1]). He underscored several key principles:

  • All action items and contributions are to be clearly documented and tracked, promoting transparency and accountability throughout the mentorship process.
  • Inclusivity remains central, with all mentees encouraged to contribute to the planning and development of projects.
  • Active listening, goal co-creation, and ongoing feedback form the foundation of a strong mentoring relationship, ensuring mentees’ voices are heard and valued.
  • Throughout all programming, the group will adhere strictly to privacy and informed consent standards consistent with both university policies and American Sociological Association guidelines ([2], [3]).

The team agreed to maintain regular check-ins and use a collaborative decision-making model for ongoing planning. At the conclusion of each session, participants are encouraged to reflect on group discussions and provide feedback for continuous improvement.

Next steps include reviewing the workshop proposal draft, circulating updated college resources, and advancing outreach with the Harlem community center. The date and agenda for the upcoming mentorship session will be finalized shortly and shared with all participants.

5. Alignment with Standards and Best Practices

This mentorship initiative is firmly grounded in established best practices. The structure and philosophy align with MENTOR National Mentoring Partnership Standards, which prioritize goal-oriented mentoring, autonomy-supportive engagement, and inclusivity ([1]). All planning and activities are documented in accordance with the American Sociological Association’s guidelines for ethical community engagement and youth research in public spaces ([2]). Throughout, the program remains committed to upholding privacy, informed consent, and equitable access according to rigorous academic and community outreach standards ([3]).
