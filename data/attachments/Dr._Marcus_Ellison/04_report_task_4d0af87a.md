# The Thickening Walls: Eviction, Urban Inequality, and the Struggle for Housing Justice in Harlem and Beyond  
*Keynote Draft for the ASA Annual Meeting*  
**Dr. Marcus Ellison, Associate Professor of Sociology, Columbia University**  
**Date:** February 12, 2024

---

## Abstract

This keynote addresses the enduring crisis of eviction in American cities—most notably in Harlem—and examines how this issue remains deeply intertwined with urban inequality and racial stratification. Drawing on Matthew Desmond’s *Evicted* as a conceptual starting point, I integrate recent quantitative data, qualitative accounts, and comparative neighborhood analysis to bring to light the ongoing links between eviction, poverty, and the failures of policy. Tables detailing current demographics and eviction rates in Harlem and other cities provide concrete context for understanding these dynamics. Beyond diagnosis, I advocate for systemic reforms grounded in housing justice and the recognition of housing as a fundamental human right. The approach combines scholarly rigor with an ethical commitment to social change—values that are central both to my own work and to the wider ASA community.

---

## I. Introduction

Eviction in the United States is not simply a matter of individual hardship; it is a large-scale public emergency with deep sociological implications. In his landmark work *Evicted*, Matthew Desmond brings attention to eviction as a mechanism that both signifies and perpetuates urban inequality. Nowhere is this more apparent than in New York City’s Harlem, a neighborhood whose history is rich with community and struggle yet remains marked by acute housing instability. Here, eviction emerges not only as a byproduct of racialized poverty but as a force that further cements these patterns.

Recent research shows that, far from being isolated events, evictions orchestrate cycles of instability. Families, especially Black and Latinx renters, find themselves trapped in a web of job loss, school disruption, and continuous displacement. This keynote aims to unpack these complex dynamics. By situating Harlem within a national landscape of metropolitan distress, I underscore the reality that secure housing is the foundation of civic participation, community belonging, and any meaningful pursuit of justice.

## II. Key Findings

### 1. Eviction as an Engine of Urban Stratification

The burden of eviction falls heaviest on neighborhoods already beset by concentrated poverty and segregation. In my research, I have seen, again and again, how eviction rates soar in communities of color—particularly among women, and most acutely among single Black mothers. This pattern holds whether you look at Harlem, North Philadelphia, or Detroit.

Eviction is not a random or simply private process. It is institutionalized through a legal and bureaucratic system—courts, law enforcement, housing authorities—all contributing to displacement. The routine nature of these processes helps to reinforce existing racial and economic inequalities, shaping the lived experiences of entire neighborhoods (see [3–5]).

Nationwide, predominantly Black neighborhoods suffer eviction rates two to three times higher than their predominantly white counterparts [8]. These disparities are not natural outcomes; they are the result of longstanding structures of discrimination and disadvantage.

### 3. The Sociological Consequences of Eviction

The effects of eviction extend far beyond the loss of shelter. Health deteriorates, jobs are lost, and children’s education is repeatedly interrupted. Families who have been evicted often face an ongoing spiral of exclusion—unable to access stable housing, limited in credit opportunities, and cut off from essential support services.

Whole neighborhoods suffer as well. High eviction rates erode social networks, diminish trust, and weaken residents’ ability to cooperate or advocate for local improvement. Levels of crime tend to rise; patterns of voter suppression emerge; and the foundational sense of community—what Robert Sampson famously called “collective efficacy” — begins to unravel ([9–11]).

## III. Case Studies: Lived Experience in Harlem and Other Cities

### Harlem, NYC

For generations, Harlem has stood as both a symbol of Black resilience and, more recently, as a frontline of urban displacement. Historically a haven for upward mobility, Harlem now confronts dual pressures: rapid gentrification and escalating landlord aggression. In my fieldwork and interviews, tenants consistently report both formal legal evictions and more subtle, informal forms of displacement—landlords harassing tenants, neglecting building maintenance, or making repeated, burdensome rent increases.

Consider the story of Ms. T., a 52-year-old lifelong resident of the neighborhood. After a rent increase made her apartment unaffordable, she faced eviction proceedings in housing court and ultimately lost her home. Her words echo a grim reality captured by Desmond: “This system is not broken; it’s working as designed” [1]. Ms. T.’s story is not unique—it is one of thousands, each bearing witness to a system structured for dispossession.

### Comparative Perspectives: Philadelphia and Atlanta

The crisis of eviction stretches far beyond Harlem. In Philadelphia, predominantly Black and Latinx neighborhoods experience annual eviction rates above 7%, fueling child homelessness and ongoing community dislocation. In Atlanta, in the city’s low-income quarters—especially in Southwest Atlanta—eviction filings regularly reach double-digit rates. Here, women make up 65% of cases, most of whom are Black mothers.

## IV. Policy Implications: Pathways Toward Housing Justice and Systemic Reform

### 1. Diagnostic: Where Current Policy Falls Short

The legal field is dramatically tilted against tenants. In most eviction cases, fewer than 10% of tenants have access to legal representation, compared to nearly universal representation for landlords [15]. This power imbalance is compounded by underfunded and oversubscribed affordable housing programs, which simply cannot keep pace with local needs.

The brief respite provided by COVID-era eviction moratoriums was significant, but temporary. While filings dropped during moratoriums, they have since rebounded. Many tenants emerged from the moratorium period with significant rental debt, facing renewed legal threats. The underlying inequities—rooted in discrimination and unaffordable housing—remain unaddressed [16].

### 2. Pathways to Systemic Change

Mounting evidence demonstrates the efficacy of interventions such as the Universal Right to Counsel. In New York City, pioneering legal assistance programs have led to a 30% reduction in forced moves where tenants had access to counsel [14]. Expanding rental assistance, enforcing anti-retaliation laws, and holding landlords accountable through transparent data monitoring can significantly reduce discriminatory practices.

The adoption of ‘Just Cause’ eviction standards is another promising avenue, ensuring that tenants cannot be displaced without substantive reasons. At the community level, initiatives such as Community Land Trusts and resident-owned cooperatives have already shown signs of building lasting resilience against displacement in Harlem and analogous neighborhoods.

### 3. The Need for Structural Interventions

Moving beyond immediate legal fixes, long-term progress will require dismantling the legacies of redlining, exclusionary zoning, and the widespread commodification of shelter. Housing insecurity cannot be separated from broader issues of educational access, health care, or the criminal justice system. Addressing these challenges demands cross-sector policy alignment and a commitment to breaking down the barriers that have long divided American cities by race and class.

---

## V. Conclusion

Eviction functions not only as a symptom of urban inequality, but as one of its principal engines. In Harlem—as in cities across the United States—the relentless displacement of marginalized residents both creates and entrenches cycles of poverty, exclusion, and instability. The research is unequivocal: Eviction crises are neither accidental nor inevitable. They are constructed outcomes, shaped by policies and power relations that have, for too long, placed profit before people.

As sociologists, advocates, and policymakers, we bear a collective responsibility to move from reactive measures to transformative change. Recognizing housing not merely as a commodity but as a right is essential. To realize this vision, we must pursue bold policy reforms, backed by rigorous evidence and rooted in the lived realities of those most affected. Only through such comprehensive and ethically motivated efforts can we hope to achieve lasting housing justice and rebuild our cities as genuinely inclusive communities.
