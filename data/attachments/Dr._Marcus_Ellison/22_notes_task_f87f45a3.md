# Weekly Meal Prep Plan for Dr. Marcus Ellison (June 2–8)

## Introduction

As the first week of June unfolds, I find myself acutely attuned to the seasonal rhythms threading through both city life and the upstate farmlands that supply my kitchen. Food, for me, has always been more than mere sustenance; it’s a conduit for connection—to place, to people, and to ideas. This week’s meal plan is an intentionally crafted intersection of my scholarly interests as an Associate Professor of Sociology at Columbia and my everyday rituals. I’ve chosen to anchor my menu in the varied bounty of our regional markets—fresh ramps, heirloom tomatoes, and Hudson Valley smoked trout—while treating the process of planning, preparing, and sharing meals as an ongoing, improvisational reflection on community, sustainability, and belonging.

A few dishes stand out among the week’s offerings. The spicy asparagus and smoked trout pâté tart, for example, was inspired by a recent conversation with my collaborator, Maya, and sets the tone for a week of exploring how food rituals can bridge flavors, histories, and friendships. Throughout these days, I’ll be mindful not only of sourcing (with regular stops at the Union Square Greenmarket and Riverside Park Farmers’ Market), but of how each meal, prepared and shared, serves as both a personal and collective anchor.

## Inspirations and Methodologies

### Maya’s Input

The genesis for Sunday’s asparagus and smoked trout pâté tart came from a late-night exchange with Maya, my frequent collaborator and friend. She has a knack for marrying robust Hudson Valley ingredients with a sense of playful experimentation. Her vision—melding smoky fish with the clean, grassy notes of spring asparagus, layered with the acid of heirloom tomato—reminded me that some of the best kitchen moments come from trusting intuition and crossing culinary boundaries.

### My Approach: Sourcing, Improvisation, and Community

- **Sourcing and Provenance:** This week, I return again and again to the Union Square and Riverside Park farmers’ markets, not only for their freshness but also because every purchase is a chance to reinforce the local food economy and cultivate relationships with the growers. Whenever possible, I choose Hudson Valley trout, just-picked ramps, and farm eggs that taste distinctly of place.
  
- **Improvisation:** Rather than adhere rigidly to a script, I adapt daily: if einkorn isn’t available, I’ll reach for farro or quinoa from my pantry. Leftover smoked trout pâté finds new purpose as a tartine. Each adjustment reflects my belief in the value of resourcefulness, a pattern shaped by both my sociological outlook and the unpredictability of city living.

- **Sociocultural Context:** Food at my table rarely arrives without a story. From the Sunday tart shared with friends to weekday lunches eaten at my desk, these meals thread together family traditions, global influences, and the distinct cadence of the northeastern growing season.

- **Identity and Community:** I often find myself gravitating toward flavors and dishes that echo my own background—ramps and trout from my upstate childhood, plant-based grain bowls grounded in metropolitan diversity. Whether through communal meals or quiet moments at my kitchen counter, I seek to use food as a medium for forging and strengthening relationships.

## Sociological Reflections

### Rituals and the Social Week

I deliberately structure my week around meals that anchor community—Sunday’s shared tart and Saturday’s collaborative potluck create bookends of ritual and belonging. Cooking and eating together become extensions of academic life, providing relaxed spaces for dialogue and the kind of camaraderie that’s harder to find in formal settings.

### Localism and Sustainable Practice

Every market trip is an act of intention: by selecting ramps, asparagus, and heirloom tomatoes, I’m participating in a hyper-local food system that resists the anonymity and scale of globalized supply chains. These choices support farmers, reduce food miles, and imbue each meal with a sense of place. I’m constantly reminded that food systems are sociological systems—what ends up on my plate is the result of a thousand human and ecological relationships.

### Improvisation and Reflexivity

Academia has trained me to be reflexive, to adjust my approach in response to shifting contexts. Cooking is no different. Leftovers aren’t just reheated—they’re reimagined: yesterday’s pâté is tomorrow’s tartine, and a surplus of greens inspires new ways to blend and serve them. My kitchen is an experimental space, never far from the discursive rhythms of my research.

### Food, Identity, and Inclusion

One of my greatest joys is in layering food traditions—borrowing freely from Mediterranean, Japanese, and Ashkenazi kitchens, but always filtering them through local, seasonal ingredients. I see these hybrid meals as microcosms of broader social processes: the migration of people and tastes, and the ongoing creation of inclusive rituals that make room for difference at the table.

### The Table as Public Sphere

The boundary between “academic” and “personal” often dissolves at my table. Meals act as semi-public spaces where friends, colleagues, and visitors exchange ideas just as readily as they share bread and cheese. Here, community isn’t abstract—it’s immediate, sensory, and sustained by the simple act of eating together.

## Closing Thoughts

This week’s meal plan is more than a logistical exercise; it is a living reflection of my belief that food can sustain, connect, and inform us in ways both material and symbolic. By approaching each meal as an opportunity for improvisation, intentional sourcing, and shared ritual, I find nourishment for body and mind—and, I hope, create spaces where community can quietly thrive.
