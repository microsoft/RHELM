# Margins Within the Metropolis: Urban Inequality and Lived Experience in Harlem and New Orleans  
## Photography Exhibit Proposal  
### Photographer: Dr. Marcus Ellison  
### Date: September 15, 2024

---

## Executive Summary

"Margins Within the Metropolis" is a photography exhibit I have developed to investigate the everyday realities of structural inequality in Harlem (New York City) and New Orleans—urban landscapes shaped by migration, resilience, and enduring racialized marginality. As both a sociologist and documentary photographer, I seek to make visible the complex interplay of social forces that define these cities, while honoring the creativity, agency, and dignity of their residents.

Through the lens of critical sociological theory—including Bourdieu’s forms of capital, Wacquant’s concept of advanced marginality, and ethnographic approaches exemplified by Mario Luis Small—I aim to capture how inequality is lived, negotiated, and, at times, contested. My guiding questions are straightforward but profound: How do the structures of the city appear in the rhythms of daily life? In what ways do neighborhoods and individuals respond to and reshape the inequalities that surround them, through both subtle adaptation and open resistance?

Combining rigorous documentary work with deep ethnographic engagement, the exhibit invites scholars, artists, policy-makers, and the broader public to engage with questions about urban transformation, stratification, and the persistence of community against the odds. Through this exhibition, I hope to contribute to a broader, more empathetic dialogue about cities in transition and the people whose lives are so often rendered invisible in public narratives of change.

## Exhibit Concept and Objectives

### Conceptual Framework

The exhibit is grounded in ongoing scholarly debates about urban inequality and the ethics of representation. Harlem and New Orleans are, for me, living archives: both have been shaped by waves of Black migration, systemic disinvestment, and creative reimagining. My approach is visual ethnography, focusing tightly on lived experience as both a site of constraint and a wellspring of creativity.

I draw inspiration from Pierre Bourdieu’s theorization of economic, social, and cultural capital [1] to probe how privilege and deprivation are mapped onto the city’s physical and social terrain. Loïc Wacquant’s research on advanced marginality [2] informs the curatorial vision, emphasizing how certain neighborhoods become physically and symbolically stigmatized, yet also harbor forms of resilience and resistance. I also follow contemporary calls within visual sociology for ethically-grounded, community-engaged photographic work [3][4], striving to balance documentation with accountability.

### Objectives

- **Visualize Structural Inequality:** Create images that illuminate how institutional forces—housing policy, labor markets, public investment, policing—shape daily life and spatial experience in Harlem and New Orleans.
- **Reveal Multi-layered Realities:** Pair wider processes such as gentrification or segregation with intimate portrayals of social ties, routines, and small acts of adaptation and resistance.
- **Bridge Disciplines and Audiences:** Foster dialogue across sociology, art, public policy, and local communities, making research on inequality accessible and actionable.
- **Model Ethical Practice:** Center collaborative image-making, transparency, and mutual respect, following best practices in visual sociology.
- **Spark Critical Conversations:** Invite viewers to question not just the causes and consequences of inequality, but also the power of representation in shaping public understanding.

## Image Selection Criteria

### Guiding Principles

Three interlocking principles shape my approach to image selection:

1. **Sociological Relevance:**  
   Each photograph must visibly engage with how large-scale forces—like unequal schooling, concentrated poverty, or urban renewal—are inscribed onto both the built environment and the routines of community life.

2. **Ethnographic Depth:**  
   I am committed to grounding my images in long-term, respectful involvement within neighborhoods. Instead of seeking out the spectacular, my focus is on rituals and relationships: moments that express adaptation, solidarity, or constraint [3]. I avoid reducing lived struggles to mere spectacle, instead documenting the subtle systems that structure everyday existence.

3. **Documentary Integrity:**  
   Every image carries the weight of compositional care and narrative honesty. To this end, photographs are accompanied by field notes and supported by deep contextual knowledge, adhering to the highest standards of visual sociology practice [4].

### Inclusive and Exclusive Criteria

- **Included Images:**  
  - Portraits of individuals set meaningfully within their home streets, workplaces, or gathering spots.
  - Scenes of communal action—public rituals, labor, protest, or cultural expression—which reveal resilience or collective agency.
  - Visual traces of segregation, neglect, or recovery (e.g., empty lots, community gardens, hand-painted protest signs, rebuilding after disaster).

- **Excluded Images:**  
  - Any photograph lacking context, fieldwork documentation, or sufficient background.
  - Work that is staged, sensationalized, or neglects to honor the subject’s dignity.
  - Photographs made without informed consent or substantial community collaboration.

Each image included in this exhibition stands as more than a visual artifact; it is the product of deliberation, dialogue, and trust-building within the communities I photograph.

## Technical Requirements for Display, Digitization, and Preservation

### Display

To ensure clarity and accessibility:

- All images are printed as archival pigment prints, 20x24 inches (unless indicated), mounted behind non-reflective glass.
- Lighting is carefully set to 300 lux and filtered for UV to protect works without compromising visibility.
- Each print is accompanied by a wall label featuring a detailed sociological interpretation, plus QR codes connecting visitors to supplementary oral histories and fieldnotes.

### Digitization and Preservation

**Digitization**  
- Master scans are created at 4800 DPI, 16-bit TIFF format, after calibrated color correction.
- This process preserves subtle details such as film grain, lighting variability, and tonal fidelity.

**Archival Preservation**  
- Originals are protected in acid-free sleeves and stored in temperature- and humidity-controlled conditions.
- Digital files are stored with three layers of redundancy: local external drives, encrypted cloud storage, and the photographic archive of my university’s research library.

**Equipment & Workflow**  
- My principal camera throughout the project is a Canon AE-1, manufactured in 1976 and professionally serviced in 2018; using it is a deliberate commitment to craftsmanlike, intentional shooting. Negatives are digitized on an Epson V850 scanner, with painstaking dust and scratch removal and careful color balancing, to ensure that each image remains faithful to its original context and quality for years to come.

## Reflective Artist Statement

As both a sociologist and photographer, my work emerges from a deep-rooted commitment to uncovering injustice while lifting up the resilience of community life. Harlem and New Orleans are inseparable from my own biography: as a Black, Southern-born scholar, I am acutely aware of the textures of race, history, and struggle that run through their streets.

The act of restoring my Canon AE-1 was a conscious recommitment to the ethics of slow photography—to move deliberately, immerse myself fully, and build trust with those I photograph. Each image you see is the product of careful, sustained engagement; I always strive to approach my subjects with transparency and to invite genuine collaboration. My goal is never to exploit, but to bear witness—amplifying stories too often overlooked in the dominant urban imaginary.

This project is driven by a sense of obligation to contest the ongoing erasures of urban marginality [2][3]. By assembling this visual archive, I hope to dignify everyday experience, provoke critical empathy from viewers, and contribute to a growing movement for more just and equitable cities.

Methodologically, my approach is rooted in best practice: contextual transparency, subject collaboration, iterative feedback from community partners, and scrupulous care in representing each scene [4][5]. This is not simply a question of seeing, but of listening—and of leveraging the power of documentary work to foster understanding and, ultimately, collective action.

## Situating the Exhibit in Academic Discourse

This exhibition integrates and extends contemporary academic work on the visual representation of inequality. By drawing together Bourdieu’s insights into forms of capital and symbolic power [1], Wacquant’s theories of marginality and territorial stigma [2], and the immersive ethnographic sensibility championed by Mario Luis Small [3], I connect rigorous social science with messy, real-world experience. Within visual studies, my curatorial practice is attentive to Susan Sontag’s concerns about photographic ethics and the lure of voyeurism [4], as well as Patricia Hayes's reflections on the political and historical implications of photographic archiving [5].

"Margins Within the Metropolis" aims to break with narratives that flatten people’s lives into stereotypes of victimhood. Instead, by fusing careful photographic practice with critical sociological reflexivity, the exhibit enables viewers to discern the structures and human strategies underlying urban transformation. In line with prevailing academic and museum standards [5][6], all phases of exhibition planning and curation are conducted with strict adherence to ethical guidelines, prioritizing informed consent, contextual clarity, and respect for all participants.
