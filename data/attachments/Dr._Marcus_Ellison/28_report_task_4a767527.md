# Comprehensive Analysis and Documentation of July 2024 Sabbatical Research Activities: Housing Justice and Social Inequality

## Executive Summary

This report chronicles my July 2024 sabbatical research as an Associate Professor of Sociology at Columbia University, focusing on the intersections of social inequality and housing justice. Guided by five interrelated objectives, I have sought to integrate theoretical development, rigorous empirical inquiry, daily scholarly writing, immersive ethnographic fieldwork in New Orleans, and critical methodological reflection. My approach is grounded in established qualitative methods, blending archival research, participant observation, interviews, and visual analysis. Through daily sessions at the Schomburg Center for Research in Black Culture, field-based engagement with New Orleans housing justice activists, and the integration of photographic documentation, I aim to contribute nuanced, actionable insights to academic debates and social policy discussions around housing inequality. This report presents a transparent summary of my activities and preliminary findings, intended for both academic colleagues and institutional stakeholders committed to advancing housing justice.

---

## Research Objectives

Throughout July, my research was guided by five primary objectives:

1. **Advance theoretical and empirical understanding of contemporary housing justice within broader debates on social inequality**, specifically examining the ways in which race, class, and urban space intersect in determining access to stable housing.

2. **Systematically document and reflect on daily scholarly writing practices at the Schomburg Center**, with attention to both measurable progress and qualitative development of manuscript arguments.

3. **Conduct ethnographic fieldwork in New Orleans**, including participation in grassroots organizing, in-depth interviews with movement leaders and residents, and focused analysis of thematic trends in local activism.

4. **Employ photographic methods as a tool for documentation and analysis**, using visual material to enhance ethnographic description and deepen understanding of spatial and symbolic facets of housing struggles.

5. **Critically reflect on the research process**, grappling with ethical challenges, methodological concerns, and emergent questions for policy and future scholarship.

All activities were designed to converge in the production of a rigorous and comprehensive fieldwork summary, to support academic publication and inform social action.

## Fieldwork in New Orleans

Ethnographic fieldwork in New Orleans was the core of my efforts to capture lived experiences of housing activism. I intentionally chose New Orleans for its complex history of displacement, post-Katrina redevelopment, and dynamic grassroots housing movements.

### Grassroots Housing Justice Meetings

During July, I participated in two major community organizing meetings—one focused on anti-eviction campaigns, the other on tenants’ rights strategies. At these gatherings, I observed how participants shared personal stories, debated organizing tactics, and built consensus on immediate priorities. Women, BIPOC organizers, and residents who have experienced homelessness played visible leadership roles. The meetings had a palpable energy—equal parts frustration, hope, and resolve. I found that storytelling was a crucial mechanism for cultivating solidarity, with personal narratives serving as rallying points for collective action.

### Key Interviews

In addition to group meetings, I conducted semi-structured interviews with a diverse group of stakeholders:

- **Directors of grassroots housing organizations**, who described coalition-building strategies, challenges in sustaining volunteer networks, and ongoing battles for legal reforms.

- **Volunteer legal advocates**, who shed light on the limits of current tenant protection measures and shared accounts of navigating complex bureaucracies.

- **Long-term activists and tenants with lived experience of displacement**, who underscored the persistence of structural barriers and described the psychological toll of housing insecurity.

- **Emerging youth organizers**, who spoke to the importance of intergenerational dialogue and creative approaches to community outreach.

The COVID-19 pandemic emerged as a recurring theme; interviewees emphasized how the crisis both deepened vulnerabilities and opened new organizing possibilities.

#### Thematic Synthesis

Across interviews and observations, several patterns crystallized:

- **Community autonomy and mutual aid** stood out as vital counterweights to systemic neglect. Activists described how collective kitchens, legal hotlines, and informal support networks have filled gaps left by state and philanthropic interventions.

- The language of **"housing as a human right"** not only signaled a political demand but defined the ethos of local action, setting grassroots work apart from institutional policy models focused primarily on market incentives.

- Organizers made frequent reference to **historical memory and intergenerational trauma**, framing current campaigns as both a continuation of earlier justice movements and a response to the fresh wounds opened by disasters like Katrina and COVID-19.

I made a concerted effort to gain verbal consent whenever photographing individuals, following ethical guidelines to preserve privacy and dignity, especially in scenes of vulnerability or protest. Photographs often revealed details I could not capture fully in field notes—the way a mural repurposed a derelict wall into a symbol of resistance, or how protest banners transformed a city block into an assertive statement. These images will become part of a longer-term archive to historicize visual strategies of the housing justice movement.

## Reflections and Next Steps

### Critical Reflections

Integrating daily scholarly writing, fieldwork, and photographic methods enabled a more comprehensive approach to understanding housing justice. Navigating power imbalances in research relationships—especially as an outside academic engaging deeply invested local activists—required ongoing reflexivity and openness. Questions about representation, reciprocity, and the politics of visual documentation remained alive throughout the fieldwork.

Participating in community meetings, rather than simply observing, challenged me to reconsider my role. On several occasions, I contributed to discussions about research ethics, shared academic resources, and offered logistical support. These moments underscored the importance of collaborative knowledge-building and accountability in community-driven research.

### Methodological Considerations

Triangulating interviews, field notes, and photographs added analytical depth and allowed me to cross-check preliminary findings. I also kept a regular field journal to track my own assumptions, shifting perspectives, and methodological challenges—particularly around gaining informed consent for sensitive photography and balancing transparency with confidentiality.

Session tracking helped link the progression of site-based insights to theoretical synthesis—making it possible to continuously update my manuscript in light of new empirical material.

### Policy and Research Implications

The initial trajectory of my research points to the need for more transformative approaches in housing policy—approaches that move beyond piecemeal reform, addressing the structural roots of exclusion and instability. Collaboration with resident-led organizations appears crucial, both as a means to produce grounded knowledge and to inform advocacy strategies.

I recommend that institutional stakeholders prioritize participatory research models and explicitly incorporate grassroots perspectives in urban planning programs. Such engagement not only democratizes the research process but strengthens the legitimacy and efficacy of policy interventions.

### Directions for Future Research

Building on this foundation, I intend to extend my ethnographic engagement with New Orleans communities beyond July, tracking the outcomes of current organizing campaigns and policy debates over time. I am also planning a comparative component, examining similarities and divergences between housing activism in New Orleans and other U.S. cities facing acute affordability crises.

A longer-term goal is to curate and analyze a photographic archive tracing the evolution of housing justice strategies, offering both an analytic and public-facing resource for researchers and community partners alike.
