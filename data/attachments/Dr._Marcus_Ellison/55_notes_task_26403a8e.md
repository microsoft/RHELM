# Celebration Dinner at Minton’s Playhouse – October 28, 2024  
## Reflections from a Practitioner-Scholar

---

## I. Dinner Experience: Culinary Reflections at the Table

Last night’s dinner at Minton’s Playhouse was more than a meal—it was a sensory immersion into Harlem’s living history. The menu, deeply rooted in Southern and Black American culinary tradition, served as the evening’s prologue, each dish a narrative in itself.

| **Dish**                  | **Description**                                                                                                                     | **Sensory & Cultural Observations**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
|---------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Blackened Redfish**     | Fresh redfish fillet seasoned with bold Cajun spices, seared to a dark crust, served atop creamy grits and sprinkled with microgreens. | When the plate arrived, the redfish caught my eye first—its dark, glossy crust with a halo of reddish-pink around the edges. The spicy, smoky aroma pulled me in, with the subtle sweetness of fresh fish and a faint grassy scent from the microgreens. The first bite delivered the deep umami and peppery zing characteristic of Creole cooking, followed by an herbal finish that stayed with me. That interplay between crispy exterior and tender fish inside—so satisfying. This dish, steeped in the legacy of New Orleans and Pan-Southern Black cuisine, felt like a tribute to the Great Migration—a culinary bridge from the Deep South to Harlem, full of stories about movement and transformation.               |
| **Smoked Collard Greens** | Traditional collards slowly simmered with smoked turkey and a hit of vinegar, served with its own pot liquor.                            | The collards arrived glistening, a deep, soulful green dappled with pieces of smoky turkey. The aroma was both earthy and tangy, with a humidity that reminded me of steamy kitchens from my childhood. Flavor-wise, the mix of bitterness and sweetness was perfectly balanced—the smoke hung around in the background, the tang of vinegar brightened everything. The greens were tender but still had some bite, far from mushy. Eating them felt like connecting to tradition, tasting the deep roots of resilience and community that have long sustained Black families during times of upheaval and transition. I felt almost grounded, reminded of the comfort food brings, especially in communal, celebratory moments like this.            |
| **Fried Oyster Po’boy Bites** | Mini brioche buns filled with freshly fried, cornmeal-battered oysters, topped with a spicy remoulade and pickled okra.                 | These po’boy bites were a playful gesture—bite-sized, golden, and inviting with bright, fresh garnishes. The warm scent of fried cornmeal mingled with the zesty punch of the remoulade. The texture was a revelation: a crisp shell yielding to the creamy oyster, all balanced against the soft brioche. The pickled okra gave a pop of acidity that kept each bite lively. Here, the culinary influences felt wonderfully layered—Creole, French, traces of West African technique. For me, this dish captured Harlem’s spirit: ever-adaptive, honoring roots while embracing newness. It spoke of migration, innovation, and the way food lets us claim spaces and stories for ourselves—especially in a neighborhood always in flux.                    |
| **Buttermilk Cornbread**  | Thick, rustic wedge brushed with rosemary-honey butter, served warm.                                            | The cornbread came out hearty and sun-yellow, edges darkened to perfection. Its aroma—sweet, buttery, and faintly herbal—reminded me instantly of home. The taste was softly sweet but balanced, the tang from buttermilk lingering after each bite, the rosemary saturating the finish. I loved how it managed to be both moist and crumbly. Its Appalachian and Southern roots evoke a kind of ritual togetherness—the familiar presence that always brings people to the table. Especially here, it felt less like a side dish and more like a statement: in the midst of celebration or challenge, simple, honest food still sustains and unites.         |

**Food as Community Ritual**  
What struck me most was how each dish summoned memories—not only my own, but those of entire communities. At Minton’s, Southern foodways meet Harlem’s changing streets. Sharing these plates, with their deep roots and bold flavors, I felt a palpable sense of belonging. This meal was an act of remembering, a rehearsal of resilience that felt all the more urgent amidst gentrification and ongoing pressures facing so many who built this neighborhood. Through food, we marked continuity even as the city shifts around us.

## II. Live Jazz Trio: A Night of Music, Memory, and Urban Renewal

As we finished our meal, the lights dimmed and the house jazz trio took the stage—piano, upright bass, and drums. The first notes of Thelonious Monk’s “In Walked Bud” rang out, and I was immediately pulled into their conversation; their music was less a performance than a living dialogue, full of subtle glances and bold improvisations.

The pianist danced around angular melodies, supported by the elastic, driving energy of the bass. The drummer’s brushwork shimmered, sometimes fading to a whisper, then crashing back into crisp, rhythmic accents. I found myself swept up in the musicians' ability to read each other in real time, the shared risks they took in shifting a groove or stretching a chord farther than seemed possible.

The room responded in kind—soft gasps punctuated with applause, collective laughter at playful musical asides, an entire audience held in delicate suspense and spontaneous joy. In that space, jazz became more than entertainment: it was a language of survival. It struck me how the music mirrored Harlem itself—negotiating change through improvisation, resilience, and deep communal listening.

**Music as Urban Resilience**  
What unfolded on stage echoed deeply with my research on urban life. At Minton’s, a space already soaked in the memory of jazz revolutions, each musical risk stood for collective adaptability. The interplay between musicians wasn’t just technical, but social—a model for how communities absorb change without losing their essential groove. The crowd’s participation made the performance feel co-created, an act of putting community back in harmony after the discord of the outside world. Especially in a neighborhood where demographic turnover and economic challenges often mean loss, music offered a brief but powerful reminder: we can still find moments of shared purpose, even if only for a night.

## III. Research Collaboration with Maya Chen

### Supporting Each Other, On and Off the Field

This fall, my collaboration with Maya Chen has been a real lifeline. Recovering from knee surgery was hard—I worried I’d fall behind or be unable to keep up with research demands. Maya stepped up in every way imaginable, making sure I had reliable transportation to campus and helping navigate the logistical obstacles of returning to work. When my mobility was limited, she didn’t hesitate to advocate for accessible lab meetings, setting up shared online whiteboards that let us brainstorm just as fluidly from home as in person. I felt genuinely cared for, not just as a colleague but as a friend—her support, both emotional and practical, made it possible for me to stay fully engaged in our work.

### Interdisciplinary Sparks in Urban Resilience Research

Since late summer, our weekly sessions have been intensive idea generators. We’ve mapped the intersections of urban sociology, public health, racial and ethnic studies, architecture, and environmental science. Together, we are refining our “layered resilience” research framework, tracing how urban infrastructures, informal networks, and cultural memory interlock with ecological adaptation in New York City neighborhoods.

Maya brought forward innovative participatory action research (PAR) strategies, arguing persuasively for embedding resident storytelling and on-the-ground focus groups into our core methodology. This approach opened up new possibilities for understanding adaptation, especially among communities facing rapid environmental and economic change. Our ongoing brainstorms have shaped a more grounded, community-centered way of examining how rituals—shared meals, block parties, live music—help anchor neighborhoods under stress. These aren't just academic ideas; they connect directly to what I felt last night at Minton’s.

### Building Our Case Study: Harlem’s Brownstone Blocks

We're set to explore Harlem’s brownstone block associations as micro-nodes of resilience, testing our hypotheses during both ordinary routines and in response to crisis moments like COVID-19 or power outages. Our toolkit mixes ethnographic shadowing, participatory mapping, and network analysis, aimed at uncovering how deeply rooted organizations can buffer stressors wrought by rapid urban redevelopment.

Some of our current research questions:
- How do longstanding community organizations mediate the pressures caused by displacement and gentrification?
- In what ways do shared practices around food and music strengthen collective response to adversity?
- How do informal networks fill the gaps left as formal supports are eroded?

These inquiries are already shaping the contours of our project, and last night’s experiences at Minton’s offered fresh, tangible examples of the resilience and creative adaptation we hope to document.

## IV. Gratitude and Renewed Commitment

Reflecting today, I feel a wave of gratitude—first and foremost for Maya’s steadfast partnership. Her vision, her care, and her steady encouragement have not only kept our research on track but have helped me heal, physically and professionally.

I’m also thankful for spaces like Minton’s Playhouse, where community isn’t theory but practice—where food, music, and memory come together to reaffirm what matters most even in uncertain times. Finally, I’m reminded of the many mentors, peers, and residents who have shaped my commitment to justice-oriented, participatory scholarship.

Sharing in last night’s dinner, listening to jazz pulse through a historic room, and reconnecting with Maya’s collaborative energy—all of this has reinvigorated me. I’m more determined than ever to produce research that not only observes urban resilience and community strength, but helps amplify and sustain it. In our city’s contested spaces, hope and solidarity are never abstract; they are lived, shared, and made anew, one meal and one song at a time.
