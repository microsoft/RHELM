Executive Summary

This report presents the principal findings from the Urban Housing Inequality Project, examining housing instability and displacement in Harlem and New York City, with a particular focus on Harlem and the Bronx. By combining quantitative data on eviction filings, rental market shifts, and demographic trends with rich qualitative perspectives from residents and community organizations, the analysis reveals a sharp increase in eviction rates in these areas—especially in the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic. These trends are intertwined with deepening structural inequities, disproportionately affecting Black and Latinx communities. Drawing on robust data and community collaboration, the report outlines methodological rigor, prioritizes ethical and equity considerations, and delivers policy recommendations that reflect both statistical analysis and lived experience. The findings aim to inform scholars, policymakers, and advocates committed to advancing housing justice in New York City.

Introduction

Housing inequality is a pressing urban challenge in 2024, especially in historically marginalized neighborhoods such as Harlem and the Bronx. Economic recovery following the pandemic has not been equally distributed, and the aftermath is most evident in rising housing instability, sudden rent escalations, changing neighborhood demographics, and firsthand accounts of displacement. The purpose of this project is to illuminate these interconnected trends by collecting and analyzing both numerical data and residents’ experiences. Ultimately, this research seeks to support fair and effective interventions that address housing insecurity at its roots, while keeping equity and social justice at the forefront.

Methodology

Quantitative Data Collection and Sampling

  • Eviction Filings: Eviction data was drawn from NYC Housing Court records and city open-data platforms, covering Manhattan and Bronx zip codes from 2019 to 2024. This comprehensive approach allows for year-by-year comparisons and the identification of new or worsening hotspots.
  • Rental Price Trends: The analysis covers American Community Survey (ACS) data, supplemented by private sector indices such as Zillow and StreetEasy, with a spotlight on Harlem and the Bronx. This captures both long-term trends and more immediate shifts in rental affordability.
  • Demographic Data: Datasets from the U.S. Census, NYC Department of City Planning, and Public Use Microdata Samples track shifts in age, race, ethnicity, income, and rates of homeownership—crucial for understanding who is most affected and how neighborhoods are changing.
  • Sampling Approach: Stratified random sampling ensured a demographically and economically representative cross-section of Harlem and the Bronx, including distinctions by race/ethnicity, income brackets, and tenure (renters, owners, subsidy recipients).
  • Ensuring Data Validity: All datasets were cross-checked for consistency and completeness, with duplicates removed and attention given to mitigating selection or reporting biases. Data was weighted as needed to counteract potential overrepresentation of certain groups.

Qualitative Data Collection and Sampling

  • Resident Interviews: Forty-eight semi-structured interviews were conducted with residents at risk of or experiencing eviction and displacement. Participants were purposefully selected to reflect varied tenure—including rent-stabilized apartments, public housing, and subsidized units—and to ensure voices from diverse backgrounds.
  • Community Meeting Transcripts: Researchers attended and analyzed transcripts from nine community coalition and advocacy group meetings in Harlem and the Bronx, focusing on themes related to tenant organizing and anti-displacement efforts.
  • Data Analysis: Interviews and meeting discussions were systematically coded using NVivo, which enabled the identification and synthesis of recurring themes such as precarity, community resilience, landlord-tenant dynamics, and perspectives on city support and policy.

Data Validity and Ethical Protocols

  • Validation Strategies: Triangulation of quantitative and qualitative data, member-checking with residents and advisors, and participatory review processes were all critical to minimizing analytic bias and ensuring findings reflected lived realities.
  • Ethical Safeguards: Every step of data collection complied with Institutional Review Board (IRB) requirements; informed consent was obtained, confidentiality was protected, and sensitive information was securely stored. Community review provided oversight and helped ensure the research respected participant dignity and autonomy.

Key Findings

Quantitative Trends

  • Eviction Filings:
  • Both Harlem and the Bronx have seen dramatic increases in eviction filings from 2022 to 2024. The Bronx now reports the highest per capita eviction rate of any NYC borough, with hotspots in Harlem’s 10027 and the Bronx’s 10453 zip codes.
  • Across New York City, monthly eviction filings are up 22% over pre-pandemic levels. This trend is even more pronounced in Harlem and the Bronx, with increases of 30% and 38% respectively.
  • Rental Price Trends:
  • In Harlem, average rents have risen 9% from 2022 to 2024, outpacing both inflation and the citywide average. The typical Harlem household now allocates 60% of its income to rent, twice the traditional affordability threshold of 30%.
  • While rents in the Bronx remain lower in absolute terms, the rate of increase is higher than in adjacent boroughs, placing additional strain on low-income residents.
  • Demographic Shifts:
  • Since 2019, homeownership among Black residents in Harlem has dropped by 7%. This coincides with rising displacement pressures and signals a significant threat to long-term community stability.
  • Patterns of gentrification are clear: there has been a 12% rise in higher-income, white residents arriving, while the Black and Latinx population has declined noticeably in both neighborhoods.

Qualitative Narratives

  • Displacement and Precarity:
  • Interviewed residents voiced a pervasive “constant fear of losing [their] home.” Many described the near impossibility of finding affordable alternatives in the neighborhood, attributed to escalating rents and reports of landlord harassment.
  • Seniors shared stories of how displacement has undermined long-standing, intergenerational social networks and cultural ties, leading to greater isolation and vulnerability.
  • Many tenants described facing punitive lease terms and legal intimidation tactics by landlords, further compounding their housing insecurity.
  • Stress and Mental Health:
  • Anxiety, depression, and chronic stress were commonly cited and directly linked to housing instability and perceived failures by institutions meant to protect tenants’ rights.
  • There was considerable frustration with the accessibility and efficacy of city legal support and social programs; many felt these resources were out of reach or insufficient.
  • Community Solidarity and Resilience:
  • Despite adversity, informal mutual aid networks and organized community efforts have become lifelines for residents facing eviction. Many are actively helping neighbors navigate legal processes, resist displacement, and access emergency resources.
  • Calls for “community control of housing” and greater investment in non-profit models repeatedly surfaced during community meetings, reflecting a growing movement for collective solutions.

Focus: Emergent Eviction Rate Patterns in Harlem & the Bronx

  • Recovery following the pandemic has been highly uneven. Eviction rates in Harlem and the Bronx now outpace increases seen in other parts of the city.
  • The escalation of filings began in early 2023, aligning with the expiration of eviction moratoria and shrinking rent relief programs.
  • Certain zip codes emerged as eviction “hotspots,” marked not only by high volume but also by repeated filings against the same tenants—an indication of serial legal harassment.
  • Informal or “silent” evictions, in which tenants vacate before formal legal proceedings, are widely believed to be under-counted. Thus, the true extent of displacement is likely even greater than the court records indicate.

Sociological Analysis: Contextualizing the Findings

The concentrated crises of eviction and housing insecurity documented in Harlem and the Bronx are consistent with long-standing sociological research. The frameworks developed by scholars such as Massey & Denton shed light on how legacies of redlining, discriminatory lending, public housing disinvestment, and profit-driven urban redevelopment have created and reinforced spaces of heightened vulnerability for Black and Latinx populations. These inequalities are not an accident or simple market outcome; they are the cumulative result of systemic policies and private sector actions over decades.

For residents, these structural factors translate directly into chronic stress, psychological hardship, and an experience of exclusion from the city’s economic recovery. The limitations of market-reliant solutions become starkly apparent in both the quantitative data and personal testimonies gathered here. While community organizing and mutual aid initiatives remain powerful sources of resilience, these grassroots efforts are often left to buttress their communities with little meaningful support from public policy.

Policy Implications and Recommendations

Findings from this project point toward concrete and urgently needed policy responses:

  • Expand Right-to-Counsel: Guaranteeing legal representation for all tenants facing eviction should be a priority. NYC’s current program must be broadened in scope and more effectively targeted to reach tenants most at risk in Harlem and the Bronx.
  • Increase Funding for Anti-Eviction and Rent Stabilization Efforts: Support programs that prevent eviction, bolster rent regulation, and preserve existing affordable housing stock in neighborhoods experiencing rapid change and displacement.
  • Support Community-Led and Non-Profit Housing Models: Investment in Limited-Equity Cooperatives, Community Land Trusts, and nonprofit ownership models can help protect affordability and embed housing decisions within the community.
  • Improve Data Collection on Informal Evictions: Participatory research and better collection of data on non-court-mediated displacements are essential for understanding and addressing the full scale of the crisis.
  • Center Equity and Lived Experience in Policy and Research: True solutions require power-sharing and collaboration between policymakers, researchers, and the residents most affected by these challenges. Community members must be partners—not merely subjects—in developing interventions and evaluating their impact.

Next Steps for Research and Community Engagement

Building on this project, recommended next steps include:

  • Developing participatory action research frameworks that employ residents as co-researchers and decision-makers throughout the research cycle.
  • Launching a longitudinal study to follow the long-term housing and wellbeing outcomes for households impacted by eviction in Harlem and the Bronx.
  • Collaborating with local organizations to create real-time “eviction and displacement dashboards” available to the public and advocacy groups, enhancing transparency and mobilization efforts.
  • Convening regular forums where city officials, researchers, and community members can co-create policy solutions and monitor progress over time.

Conclusion

The realities captured in this report underscore that housing inequality in Harlem and the Bronx is not simply a matter of abstract statistics, but an urgent and personal crisis for thousands of residents. The resurgence of eviction hotspots since the pandemic threatens the hard-won stability of communities and deepens existing inequities. Effectively tackling these challenges will require more than piecemeal policy fixes; it demands a transformative and multi-sectoral response—one that centers those most affected and grounds solutions in equity, justice, and community partnership.
