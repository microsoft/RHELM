## Session Summaries & Key Findings

### 1. Housing Insecurity and Embodied Health Disparities  
**Presenters:** Dr. Roberta Ling (UC Berkeley), Dr. Harun Dorsey (Temple University)  

Dr. Ling and Dr. Dorsey opened the session with a comprehensive analysis of housing insecurity and its direct impact on physical health. Drawing from NHIS data (2015–2022), covering 9,341 metropolitan households, they found that renters who pay over 30% of their income towards housing are at significantly higher risk for poor self-rated health (AOR 1.8; 95% CI: 1.5–2.1). Their multilevel modeling demonstrated that even after accounting for individual socioeconomic differences, neighborhood poverty consistently predicted higher rates of hypertension.

Qualitative interviews with 40 renters in Oakland highlighted the daily realities behind these statistics. Participants reported chronic anxiety, disrupted sleep, and increased emergency room visits for conditions such as asthma, all linked to housing instability. Many described repeated administrative roadblocks when attempting to access aid like Housing Choice Vouchers, underscoring the compounding nature of exclusion and bureaucracy.

Framing their findings within embodiment and ecosocial theoretical frameworks, Dr. Ling and Dr. Dorsey emphasized how lived social and economic stresses are translated into biological consequences—a phenomenon reflected in previous work linking eviction to adverse birth outcomes [1,2,3]. They concluded with policy recommendations focused on expanding housing voucher coverage and integrating health screening into eviction proceedings, arguing these reforms are essential to addressing the root causes of health disparities.

### 2. Redlining Revisited: Land Use Policy and Racial Exposure  
**Presenter:** Dr. Samuel Lee (University of Michigan)  

Dr. Lee continued with a historical and spatial analysis of Detroit, overlaying 1930s HOLC redlining maps with current census tract data. His mapping revealed persistent spatial continuity of racial segregation, making clear that the legacies of redlining endure. Using the SEER-Medicare database (*n*=7,100), Dr. Lee showed that residents in formerly redlined neighborhoods experience more than double the rates of uncontrolled hypertension and diabetes compared to those living in historically "green" (favored) zones.

Through structural equation modeling, Dr. Lee identified a web of mediating factors—ongoing segregation, heightened exposure to PM2.5 pollution, and reduced access to nutritious food—that continue to disadvantage these communities [4,5]. Importantly, he noted that urban greening and infrastructure investments have largely benefited neighborhoods in the throes of gentrification, often resulting in further displacement of long-time Black residents. Focus group discussions with Black elders reaffirmed the collective sense of loss tied to these transformations.

Dr. Lee advocated for robust reparative policies—such as targeted health investments in marginalized areas and strict anti-displacement protections—to begin redressing these cumulative harms.

### 3. Eviction, Policing, and Neighborhood Trauma  
**Presenters:** Dr. Anita Nuñez (NYU), Dr. Kayla Mayfield (UIC)  

This presentation examined the intersection of eviction, policing, and trauma in Chicago. Linking police incident reports with county eviction filings (2010–2020), Nuñez and Mayfield demonstrated that census tracts with the highest eviction rates also recorded a 27% bump in police incidents per capita, independent of baseline poverty rates.

A longitudinal survey of 800 families revealed that those at risk of eviction faced higher levels of PTSD symptoms, while frequent police stops—an experience more common among Black families—were closely tied to both race and underlying housing instability [6,7]. In interviews, young Black men described the compounding stresses of frequent moves and intensified police scrutiny, painting a vivid picture of how poverty itself becomes criminalized.

Theoretical perspectives from relational poverty (Purser) and carceral spillover (Stuart) framed these patterns as interconnected and systemic rather than isolated occurrences. The presenters called for integrated interventions: coupling eviction prevention services with mental health support and legal advocacy.

### 4. Urban Renewal as Structural Violence: Chicago Case Studies  
**Presenter:** Dr. Marcus Ellison (Columbia University)  

Dr. Ellison presented a mixed-methods analysis focused on the consequences of urban renewal on Chicago’s South Side. By pairing demolition and redevelopment data from the Chicago Housing Authority with IDPH mortality statistics (2010–2022), he illustrated that areas affected by the Plan for Transformation showed a 35% increase in asthma hospitalizations relative to North Side controls—even after accounting for age and baseline health.

Life history interviews with former Cabrini-Green residents revealed the deep personal and social costs of displacement, including increased chronic stress and the erosion of longstanding support networks. Drawing on the concepts of “structural violence” [8] and urban marginality (Wacquant) [9], Dr. Ellison argued these harms are systemic and predictable outcomes of policy decisions. Spatial entrapment theory (Massey) [10] offered further insight into how displacement and marginalization reinforce poor health.

He concluded by emphasizing the critical need for participatory planning and right-to-return policies, warning that without such measures, redevelopment continues to perpetuate cycles of harm.

### 5. Roundtable: Pathways Forward—Policy, Engagement, Equity  
**Moderated by Dr. Ellison; All Panelists**  

The roundtable synthesized insights from across the session, highlighting the value—and necessity—of interdisciplinary collaboration. Panelists emphasized integrating urban sociology, public health, and community-based advocacy. There was a shared recognition of the ethical responsibility researchers have to address power imbalances, particularly regarding positionality and ensuring marginalized voices are included and respected in both research design and dissemination.

Calls for truly community-engaged, anti-racist policy design were unanimous. The panel noted persistent gaps between research and practice, particularly in executing equity-driven urban health interventions, and urged ongoing, open dialogue between academic, policy, and community spheres.

## Reflections

### Ethical Considerations

Adequate participant protections are paramount, particularly when working with displaced families or individuals experiencing traumatic events like eviction. Building reciprocal, non-extractive partnerships means involving community stakeholders—not only as study participants but as real collaborators throughout research design, analysis, and dissemination. Critical self-reflection on researcher positionality, especially around race, class, and institutional affiliation, is essential to avoid perpetuating existing power imbalances.

### Theoretical Implications

The body of work presented reinforces the relevance of “structural violence” and “embodiment” as guiding frameworks for unpacking how inequalities in the urban environment become engraved upon the body. There is a clear opportunity to operationalize concepts like territorial stigma and the loss of social capital within quantitative research, and to engage further with abolitionist perspectives that are beginning to shift debates on urban health systems policy.

### Community-Engaged Research Opportunities

Participatory planning emerged as a vital practice, including direct involvement of resident councils, tenant unions, and frontline agencies in the design—not just the implementation—of research and intervention. Panelists discussed ways to support authentic co-production of knowledge, ensuring research outcomes meaningfully inform reparative local policy. Effective collaboration with public health and housing agencies offers significant potential for developing trauma-responsive, community-benefiting interventions.

### Next Steps

1. Launch a scoping review of available longitudinal datasets linking housing status and health outcomes in the Chicago metro area.
2. Develop a pilot proposal for a resident-led research collective to further study trauma and resilience following redevelopment.
3. Begin drafting a comprehensive policy brief to share session findings directly with public health and urban policy decision-makers.
4. Organize a follow-up ASA workshop focused on ethical, anti-racist approaches in urban inequality research for 2025.
