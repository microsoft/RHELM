Fall Dinner Menu Planning Session: Collaborative Field Note

Prepared by Dr. Marcus Ellison & Maya Chen – October 13, 2024

Introduction: Setting the Fall Table Together

As the leaves began turning in New York City and the air took on a crisp edge, the idea for our fall dinner came easily. Both of us—Marcus and Maya—are drawn to the markets this time of year, when the city's Greenmarkets show their full autumn splendor. We wanted to create a menu that truly honored the local, seasonal bounty, and to do so together, letting each of our perspectives guide the process.

Our joint approach began with a Saturday morning at the Union Square Greenmarket. Walking those stalls, sampling apples, chatting with growers, and making decisions together set the tone for the entire menu: thoughtful, rooted in place, and designed for hospitality that brings people together. Throughout, we aimed to blend the practicalities of household cooking with the intentionality of academic inquiry—making this both a living dinner and a teaching model for collaborative food planning.

Menu Overview

The resulting menu features three dishes, each chosen after plenty of debate, laughter, and taste-testing at the market and back at home. We prioritized flexible recipes that would adapt to what the market offered on the day, while also keeping in mind the preferences of our guests—especially around allergies, dietary restrictions, and ease of shared cooking.

Menu Selections: - Fall Vegetable Stew
I wanted something that celebrated root vegetables and hearty greens, so we built a stew around what we found: fat carrots, tender parsnips, sweet squash, crisp kale, and fragrant leeks. We agreed to keep it rustic and vibrant, inspired by both our culinary traditions and what was most beautiful at the market stalls.

  • Smoked Trout Pâté
    Marcus has a long-standing fondness for smoked fish, especially when it's sourced from the likes of Acme at Greenmarket pop-ups. After a few sample bites (with the vendor’s encouragement), we settled on smoked trout as the centerpiece for a pâté—creamy, tangy, and perfect with fresh sourdough.

  • Apple Tart
    Dessert was non-negotiable. We tasted countless apple varieties before settling on tart, slightly floral heirlooms. I insisted on a classic tart: thinly sliced apples, a buttery crust, a glossy finish, and room for improvisation depending on what looked best that morning.

Our Collaborative Method: Throughout the planning, we let the market guide our choices, never forcing something that didn’t feel seasonally right. We talked through adjustments for dietary needs (keeping the stew gluten and nearly dairy-free, offering a lighter alternative for the pâté’s base), and divided prep roles based on our kitchen strengths: Marcus with the knife work and savory prep, myself (Maya) with pastry and plating. This intent—to collaborate openly and play to our mutual strengths—became as fundamental to the meal as the ingredients themselves.

Recipe Instructions

Each dish was developed with deliberate collaboration, balancing efficiency and creativity. We chose moments to cook side by side, and others to give each other space in the kitchen. Here’s how our process unfolded:

Fall Vegetable Stew

  1. Prepping Ingredients Together:
    Marcus dove into peeling and chopping the root vegetables, moving quickly but with a certain reverence for each vibrant carrot and sturdy parsnip. I tackled the greens—washing kale in cold water, admiring the curls and shades—and prepped the fresh herbs. We debated briefly about whether to cut the vegetables chunky or diced, landing on chunky for a rustic, autumnal feel.

  2. Sautéing the Base:
    We heated olive oil in a Dutch oven, adding the leeks and garlic. The aroma spread through the apartment, sweet and subtle. To deepen the flavors, we decided to sauté on low heat, savoring this step before adding the heavier vegetables.

  3. Building the Stew:
    Once the base was soft, we tossed in the carrots, parsnips, and squash, seasoned with salt and pepper, and stirred to coat. We watched for a little caramelization before pouring in the broth and bringing everything to a simmer. We added thyme and rosemary, then covered the pot, letting it cook gently for about 25 minutes. Seven minutes before finishing, I stirred in the kale, giving it just enough time to soften but keep its bright green color. Together, we tasted and adjusted the seasoning, a small moment of genuine teamwork.

  4. Serving:
    We ladled the stew into wide bowls, taking care to distribute vegetables evenly. On my suggestion, we topped each serving with a sprinkle of fresh herbs and a swirl of good olive oil—a small flourish that made the meal feel complete.

Smoked Trout Pâté

  1. Preparation:
    Marcus took charge of flaking the trout, meticulously checking for bones and discarding the skin. I measured out the cream cheese and crème fraîche, letting both come to room temperature for easy blending.

  2. Mixing:
    All ingredients—trout, cheese, crème fraîche, lemon juice—went into a bowl. We used a fork for mixing, deliberately keeping the texture rustic, not too smooth. We both prefer the pâté to have some character and heft.

  3. Finishing Touches:
    Chives, dill, and black pepper were folded in last. We tasted the mixture together, adjusting the seasoning and lemon until balanced. The pâté was then covered and set to chill for at least an hour; we found that an overnight rest only improved the flavor.

  4. Serving:
    Just before dinner, we spooned the pâté into a wide ramekin and set it out with thick, chewy slices of rye bread, artfully arranged. We both appreciate when appetizers feel casual but still a bit elegant.

Apple Tart

  1. Working the Pastry:
    With the oven preheating, I rolled the puff pastry on a floured board, aiming for a thin, crisp base (Marcus is, admittedly, less interested in pastry precision). We debated par-baking the crust; in the end, I insisted, and we agreed it did help the finished tart stay crisp.

  2. Preparing the Apples:
    We cored and sliced the apples as a team, grouping tart and sweet varieties together for complexity. Arranging them in overlapping layers became a collaborative moment, each of us adjusting slices for an appealing pattern.

  3. Assembly and Baking:
    We dotted the apples with butter, sprinkled over sugar and cinnamon, and slid the tart into a hot oven. The smell as it baked—apples and spice—was the essence of fall.

  4. Finishing:
    As soon as the tart came out, I warmed apricot jam to make a glaze, brushing it over the apples for shine and a gentle tang. Marcus handled the final plating, arranging warm slices on a rustic wooden board.

  5. Serving:
    We served the tart either warm or at room temperature—always the subject of friendly debate.

Reflections: Collaboration at the Table

Negotiating Taste and Agency:
What stood out most about this planning session was how much we both brought to the table, quite literally. From ingredient choices to how the stew was cut or the tart was glazed, every detail was a small negotiation between our tastes, skills, and values. It was never about one person’s vision—it was always about listening and adjusting, creating something unique in the overlap.

Engagement with Growers and Markets:
Our morning at the Greenmarket reminded us of why we love shopping locally. There’s a particular pleasure in talking to growers, asking for their advice on which apple bakes best or hearing about that morning’s freshest greens. These exchanges made us grateful for the relationships and knowledge nested in the city’s food systems—something far richer than a grocery store experience.

Workflow as Care:
Dividing tasks felt less about efficiency and more about care. Taking turns, giving each other space, and returning at key moments to taste or finish a dish underscored what it means to cook (and host) together. These moments of collaboration reflected not only practical hospitality, but also the kind of reciprocal relationship-building explored in food studies ([4]).

Teaching and Learning:
Documenting our process—what worked, what changed, and why—strengthened not only our own practice but also provided a real-life template for collaborative menu planning. In academic and community food systems settings, this kind of process makes the ideals of food studies tangible—negotiation, flexibility, and critical engagement in action.

Intentional Hospitality:
Ultimately, the act of planning, cooking, and serving this dinner reinforced what intentional hospitality means to us. By foregrounding local sourcing, open communication, and shared authorship, we created a table that was as much about connection as about what was on the plate.
