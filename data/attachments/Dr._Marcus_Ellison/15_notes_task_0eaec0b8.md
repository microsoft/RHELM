## Clinical Overview and Personal Reflections

### Allergist's Assessment & Guidance

My appointment confirmed what I had suspected for weeks: I’m dealing with classic seasonal allergic rhinitis, strongly tied to this time of year’s tree pollen—especially birch and oak, which dominate Manhattan’s spring allergen profile.  

The allergist recommended a combined treatment plan: regular, daily use of Flonase (a nasal corticosteroid) for ongoing inflammation, with Azelastine (a topical antihistamine nasal spray) as an add-on whenever symptoms become particularly bothersome. I received a quick refresher on correct nasal spray administration—slight forward head tilt, steady pressure, and alternating nostrils—plus the advice to resist sniffing hard right afterwards to increase effectiveness.

Non-medicinal measures were also discussed. I was reminded to keep windows closed when pollen counts spike, to use a HEPA air filter in the apartment (if possible), and to shower and change clothes when coming home from work or time outdoors. The allergist was thorough about possible side effects: Flonase can lead to mild nasal irritation or headaches, while Azelastine sometimes causes a bitter taste or slight nasal burning. If my symptoms don’t improve within 2–4 weeks, or if I develop problems like asthma or severe sinus pain, I’ll need to follow up for possible allergy testing or to explore immunotherapy.

## Manhattan’s Springtime Pollen Surge: Environment & Context

April in Manhattan is an endurance test for anyone sensitive to airborne allergens. Tree pollen—particularly from birch, oak, and maple—is rampant. Daily NYC Department of Health updates consistently mark pollen levels as “high” through April and May.[1] The urban landscape itself compounds the problem: tall buildings create wind tunnels that trap and recirculate pollen, causing it to linger at street level long after it settles. Even with closed windows, I find that pollen is almost impossible to escape, especially when it clings to jackets and hair during crowded subway commutes or in busy university hallways.

Working in academic and medical settings, I’m acutely aware that not everyone has equal tools for dealing with these exposures. Many university offices and classrooms lack modern HEPA filtration, and public transit practically guarantees I’ll track allergens home at the end of each day. From a sociological standpoint, it’s sobering to consider how differences in air quality, access to filters, and available healthcare deepen health disparities—making urban allergies not just a nuisance, but a genuine public health concern, particularly in high-density cities like New York.[2]

Because these environmental triggers and equity issues overlap with my research interests, I’m now inspired to keep a detailed daily health log—connecting my personal experiences with broader patterns in urban environmental health. This ongoing project could offer new perspectives both for my own care and for future academic reflection.

The prescribed medications—Flonase and Azelastine—are both recommended by leading U.S. and international guidelines for first- and second-line management of allergic rhinitis. The dosing instructions I received line up exactly with FDA labeling and best clinical practice.[4][5]
