### 3.2 Developing Grant Proposals for Digitisation Projects

I began by highlighting how digitisation standards continue to evolve, especially within the digital humanities. We noted the importance of consistency and rigorous methodology, referencing the TEI (Text Encoding Initiative) and best practices from leading scholars such as Schreibman, Siemens, and Unsworth (_A New Companion to Digital Humanities_)—tried and tested approaches that remain central to our work [1].

Professor Sinclair brought valuable insights into current funding avenues. She highlighted the European Research Council (ERC) grant criteria and the Leverhulme Trust’s emphasis on interdisciplinary research, especially where palaeography, digital imaging, and medieval studies intersect. Our conversation focused on integrating these perspectives to strengthen our proposal.

Throughout, we agreed that the proposal must emphasize the use of TEI-compliant metadata and strictly apply FAIR data principles—ensuring materials are findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable, as recommended by recent scholarship [2]. This is crucial for future-proofing our digitised collections for scholars and archivists.

**Next Steps:**  
- I will draft a section of the proposal focused on our digitisation methodology. This draft will incorporate TEI standards and FAIR principles as central pillars, demonstrating how our approach guarantees sustainability and accessibility.

### 3.3 Advances in Watermark Identification in Medieval Manuscripts

Professor Sinclair moved the conversation toward watermark identification—a topic increasingly relevant to both provenance studies and the broader field of manuscript research.

She provided a concise review of recent breakthroughs in non-invasive, automated watermark detection. We specifically discussed the Bernstein Consortium’s advances, as well as the enduring importance of the Piccard and Briquet watermark catalogues [4]. These tools, which combine digitised databases and sophisticated image analysis, are proving transformative in the identification and cataloguing of watermarks in historical paper.

We both underscored the value of watermarks: they don't just reveal the age and geographic origins of a manuscript, but also paint a detailed picture of scribal networks across medieval Europe. Integrating watermark analysis with digitised manuscripts strengthens our ability to trace manuscript histories, enrich archival descriptions, and, ultimately, to foster new research avenues.

To this end, we discussed the benefits of combining spectral imaging techniques with catalogue-based cross-referencing. The protocol we are developing will leverage existing scholarship and recent algorithmic approaches [5], ensuring our analysis is both comprehensive and innovative.

**Next Steps:**  
- Watermark identification will feature prominently in the grant proposal. Professor Sinclair will prepare a literature review and concise methodology overview by 20 June 2024, summarizing recent technological developments and outlining our research approach.
