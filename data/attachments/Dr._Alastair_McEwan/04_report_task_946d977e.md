## Abstract

This keynote lecture examines Scottish tower houses, iconic expressions of the late medieval and early modern landscape, with a particular focus on the Dumfries and Borders regions. Drawing upon archival research, architectural analysis, recent fieldwork—including insights from the Criffel hillwalk—and oral histories, this study explores the changes in tower house design, their roles within local communities, and their continuing cultural resonance. Tables and images illustrate key architectural features and document permissions for academic use. The lecture concludes by laying out future research opportunities and highlighting the importance of community engagement in preserving these remarkable structures.

## 1. Introduction

Scottish tower houses are enduring witnesses to centuries of change—social, political, and architectural—across the Dumfries and Borders regions. From their imposing silhouettes on ridges and river valleys to their vital roles within local heritage, these buildings encapsulate complex stories. In this lecture, I bring together findings from extensive archival research, my own published analyses, and field surveys, alongside living memories and anecdotes, aiming to offer an integrated perspective on the significance of tower houses past and present. This approach bridges history, architectural study, landscape interpretation, and personal recollection, providing a fuller understanding of how these towers have shaped, and continue to shape, their communities.

---

## 2. Historical Context

### 2.1 Tower Houses in Late Medieval and Early Modern Scotland

Tower houses flourished in Scotland from the fourteenth through the seventeenth centuries, a response to local insecurity stemming from frequent conflicts, shifting allegiances, and the turbulence of border society. Initially designed as strongholds for nobility and lairds seeking domestic safety, they quickly became trophies of social status and symbols of local power. The “license to crenellate” issued under James IV encouraged the proliferation of towers by formalizing landowners' rights to fortify their residences. Over time, these structures evolved from purely military refuges into substantial homes, combining security with comfort.

### 2.2 Dumfries and the Borders: Regional Dynamics

Dumfries and the Borders have historically been zones of fluid identity and frequent confrontation, shaped by border reivers, cross-border raids, and changing patterns of landholding. Tower houses in these areas reflect both defensive necessity and local tradition. Archival records from families such as the Douglases, Maxwells, and Scotts show adaptations to local topography, resource availability, and the pressures of succession and inheritance. Drumlanrig, Smailholm, and Neidpath represent more than architectural innovations; they are microcosms of broader regional transitions, from fragmented feudal domains to increasingly centralized Scottish governance.

## 3. Architectural Analysis

### 3.1 Evolution of Scottish Tower House Forms

A defining feature of Scottish tower houses is their tall, narrow structure and compact plan, which prioritizes defensibility. Early forms were typically rectangular keeps with thick stone walls and minimal openings. Over the course of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the addition of wings and stair towers led to more complex L- and Z-plan layouts. Such changes were driven both by advances in construction technology (notably vaulting and secure stone staircases) and by evolving expectations of domestic comfort. In my own field surveys, I have found many towers retain essential defensive features, even as later modifications introduced features like larger windows and ornamental detail.

### 3.2 Key Features and Innovations

Scottish tower houses are distinctive for several architectural elements:

- Barrel-vaulted chambers, which provided fire-resistant storage and living spaces beneath main floors
- Corbelled parapets and turrets, especially prominent in later examples, serving both structural and decorative purposes
- Clear division between public halls and private family spaces, often separated vertically by heavy stone vaults and accessed via turnpike (spiral) stairs
- Locally sourced stone—sandstone and whinstone dominate in Dumfries and Borders, though rubble masonry is widely seen in less affluent estates
- Turnpike stair towers positioned for security and privacy

These features persist across the region, with towers in the Borders tending to exploit natural rock outcrops for extra protection, while those in Dumfries sometimes form the nucleus of sprawling manor complexes.

## 4. Case Studies

### 4.1 Drumlanrig Castle

Drumlanrig Castle stands as a testament to architectural ambition in seventeenth-century Scotland. Originally a robust keep, it was gradually transformed into a grand baronial mansion, marked by chamfered angles and dramatic stair towers. Estate record surveys reveal the castle’s orientation was designed to command views over the valley, with security gradually giving way to comfort and display. The later addition of baroque wings signaled a definitive shift from martial architecture to domestic grandeur, making Drumlanrig a transitional milestone in Scottish building history.

### 4.2 Smailholm Tower

Smailholm Tower is instantly recognizable atop its rocky perch, a sentinel over the Tweed valley. Its compact design and thick walls bear witness to an era when safety was paramount. Accounts passed down through my family—my father, a gamekeeper in the Borders, often recounted the traditions maintained around Smailholm—demonstrate how human stories are intertwined with the stone and landscape. Daily rhythms of wildlife management, seasonal gatherings, and memory-making anchor the tower in community life.

### 4.3 Neidpath Castle

Set on a commanding bluff above the Tweed, Neidpath Castle is an exemplary Z-plan tower, with projecting turrets optimized for sightlines and defense. Its stonework has weathered centuries with remarkable resilience, and the internal vaulting offers invaluable material for architectural study. Estate documentation, alongside modern conservation reports, chart the evolution of Neidpath’s uses—from fortified home to residence, and later to site of restoration efforts—pathways that echo the changing fortunes of the region.

## 5. Landscape Context

### 5.1 Tower Houses and their Defensive Setting

Landscape selection was never arbitrary; Scottish tower houses frequently occupy positions of maximum strategic advantage. Prominent hills, crags, and river bluffs provided natural barriers, while open vistas enabled oversight of lands and approaches. Defensive utility often dictated location, but equally, towers became integral markers within local networks of power and resource control.

### 5.2 Social Significance of Landscape Integration

Control of the surrounding land was integral to tower house identity. Estates were often sited to dominate arable fields, access to water, and established routes of travel and trade. As social expectations shifted, features like gardens, parks, and planned vistas began to appear, reflecting the changing role of the land—from battle ground and production site to symbol of cultured refinement. These changes are documented in estate records and archaeological surveys, revealing how families not only lived off the land, but inscribed meaning on it.

### 5.3 Insights from the Criffel Hillwalk

Walking Criffel recently, I was struck again by the interconnectedness of topography and settlement. The steep ascent and panoramic outlook explain, in a visceral way, why elevated sites were so prized by tower builders. From the summit, the visibility across the Solway Firth and back toward Dumfries reveals how towers like Drumlanrig and others could control enormous stretches of territory. My field notes detail the sightlines between towers and subsidiary farms, affording not just physical security but an enduring sense of dominion that still shapes the local imagination.

## 6. Oral History and Living Memory

### 6.1 Borders Gamekeeper Anecdote

Oral tradition remains a vital source for understanding the everyday lives entwined with tower houses. My father’s recollections as a gamekeeper near Smailholm, told around countless kitchen tables and hillside walks, bring into relief the continuity between past and present. His stories of land stewardship, seasonal rhythms, and community gatherings are woven as tightly into local identity as the stones themselves. These accounts capture nuances that formal histories often miss: the sense of belonging that comes from working the same fields, walking the same paths, and listening to the same stories as generations before.

### 6.2 Community and Cultural Continuity

Tower houses have survived not only through masonry and mortar, but through the vigilant care of local people. Annual reunions, storytelling events, and restoration efforts are testament to their enduring relevance. Community stewardship—whether through oral history projects, school visits, or practical conservation—continues to shape the significance of these sites today. When a village mobilizes to repair a broken parapet, or elders gather children for tales of old raids, the towers are being kept alive in the truest sense.

## 8. Future Research and Community Engagement

The study of Scottish tower houses continues to offer rich possibilities:

- **Dendrochronological and materials analysis:** Many lesser-known towers await detailed structural analysis, which will refine chronologies and deepen understanding of regional variation.
- **Expanded oral history initiatives:** Systematic engagement with current and former residents—as well as custodians and local descendants—can preserve stories at risk of fading.
- **Digital mapping and landscape modelling:** Reconstruction of historical sightlines and estate networks using modern technologies will augment traditional research methods.
- **Community-led conservation and education:** Sustained partnerships between heritage professionals and local volunteers will ensure knowledge is transferred and valued.
- **Interdisciplinary collaborations:** Bringing together historians, architects, archaeologists, and ecologists will foster more nuanced interpretations and open new avenues for inquiry.

---

## 9. Conclusion

The Scottish tower houses of Dumfries and the Borders stand as more than silent relics; they are layered testaments to human ingenuity, adaptation, and memory. Through architectural evolution, sensitive engagement with the land, and the living presence of oral tradition, these structures continue to inhabit their landscape and communities in dynamic ways. A holistic approach—rooted in archival study, rigorous fieldwork, and the voices of local people—is essential to honoring and sustaining their legacy. Continued multidisciplinary research and active community involvement will ensure these towers remain vital emblems for generations to come.
