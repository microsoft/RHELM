Discussion and Decisions

1. Fieldwork Methodology and Objectives

We began by clarifying our main goals for the Hermitage Castle visit: producing a thorough visual and contextual record of the site’s medieval architecture, features, and the surrounding landscape. To achieve a well-rounded documentary record, we agreed on a multi-modal approach combining:

  • Photographic survey: High-resolution images for both overall structure and detailed stonework.
  • Site sketches: On-site architectural and landscape sketches to supplement photographic evidence.
  • GIS mapping: Precise locational data using handheld GPS units for integrating survey information into broader landscape records.
  • Archival Analysis: Follow-up with relevant historic documents and maps, triangulating current site observations against archival records.

This approach is consistent with the protocols recommended by Historic Environment Scotland and documented in recent peer-reviewed literature, emphasizing the importance of triangulating visual and documentary sources and maintaining rigorous site diaries during fieldwork [1].

2. Transport Arrangements

Given Hermitage Castle’s remote location and limited public transport links, we evaluated travel options in detail. The University of Glasgow’s minivans have demonstrated strong reliability over the past five years (over 95% according to internal fleet reports) [2], making them the preferred choice for transporting the main field team and equipment.

For team members traveling independently, ScotRail remains a feasible backup, especially for those commuting from Newcastle or Carlisle. Historical service data for the Newcastle–Carlisle line indicates above-average reliability [3]. In terms of cost, the minivan falls within departmental budget, while ScotRail’s group return fares are approximately £38 each, with potential discounts for bulk bookings. These estimates are in line with previous CWAAS excursions.

We also considered past fieldwork experiences at Hermitage Castle, noting that after 2015, most teams resorted to private transport due to poor connectivity. Our solution reflects ongoing adaptations to persistent logistical constraints at this heritage site.

3. Photographic Equipment and Technical Documentation

After a detailed equipment review, we confirmed the following primary components for historical site documentation:

  • Camera: Nikon D750 DSLR (24.3 MP FX-format sensor), chosen for robust performance in low-light environments, common within medieval stone interiors.
  • Lenses:
  • NIKKOR 24–120mm f/4G ED VR for general architectural and landscape shots.
  • NIKKOR 16–35mm f/4G ED VR for wide-angle interior photography, especially in confined or vaulted spaces.
  • Tripod: Manfrotto MK190XPRO4, providing stability for long-exposure photographs essential in poorly lit interiors.
  • Lighting: Godox LEDP260C Panel, equipped with adjustable color temperature for balanced illumination and shadow mitigation.
  • Ancillary items: Spare EN-EL15 batteries, high-capacity SD cards, lens cleaning kit, and silica gel packs to control humidity.

Our selections are aligned with Historic England’s standards for digital recording and monument surveys [4], and reflect best practice protocols outlined in recent scholarship [5]. Wide-angle lenses, portable lighting, and robust data storage are all determined to be essential in capturing high-quality images for digital heritage archiving.

4. Technical Action Plan for Lens Fogging

Dr. McEwan highlighted ongoing challenges with lens fogging—particularly when moving equipment quickly between cold, damp exteriors and fluctuating interior conditions. Drawing from Nikon’s manufacturer guidelines [6], we agreed to implement a bag acclimatization protocol: placing the sealed camera bag inside the site for 30+ minutes before use to minimize condensation risk.

To reinforce this approach, silica gel packs will be distributed in all camera bags, following recommendations from current best practices [7]. Our plan includes arriving at least 45 minutes before photo survey to account for necessary acclimatization.

Historical Context and Fieldwork Observations

Hermitage Castle remains a formidable site in the Scottish Borders, well-known for its isolated setting, challenging environmental conditions, and complex architectural features. Chronic dampness and inconsistent lighting are a recurrent challenge, and were prominent in Dr. McEwan’s previous fieldwork at the site—in particular, his 2016 team faced heavy seasonal fog and delays in receiving access permissions.

Fiona added insights from recent interdisciplinary mapping projects, which highlight the value of adaptive field protocols and flexible equipment setups in climate-affected historic sites. Archiving digital photographs under persistent humidity and dim lighting requires both technical preparation and methodological flexibility, as underscored in field-based studies on medieval monument documentation [5][7].

As such, we have built adjustments into our protocols to ensure reproducibility of results and digital integrity of our records. Early arrival, acclimatization periods, and agile equipment usage are all critical steps to maximizing efficiency and data quality during this upcoming fieldwork.
