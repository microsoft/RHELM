## 1. Screening Overview

On 28 June 2024, I attended a comprehensive physiotherapy screening at Dumfries Health Centre. The assessment included a series of mobility, balance, and gait tests, all designed to evaluate my current physical condition—particularly in relation to my regular hillwalking routines and research activities that require extended periods of standing and walking.

## 2. Health and Mobility Improvements: Analytical Summary

### Quantitative Metrics

Recent interventions have led to notable improvements across several physical parameters:

- **Gait Analysis:**  
  My average stride length has increased from 1.12m to 1.20m. Stance phase symmetry rose from 88% to 94%, indicating smoother and more balanced gait. Cadence improved, moving from 102 to 108 steps per minute. Mid-stance lateral sway also decreased, dropping from 13cm to 9cm as measured by inertial sensors.

- **Joint Flexibility:**  
  Both knees now show improved flexion (right: 110° to 125°, left: 112° to 126°). Hip extension increased from 14° to 18° bilaterally, and ankle dorsiflexion advanced from 10° to 14°.

- **Strength & Balance (Timed Up and Go Test):**  
  My time improved from 11.2 seconds to 9.4 seconds, reflecting greater functional mobility and reduced risk of tripping or falling.

### Qualitative Observations

I’ve noticed a marked reduction in muscle soreness after hillwalking sessions, especially in the quadriceps and muscles along my posterior chain. There’s a genuine increase in confidence when navigating uneven terrain or climbing over stiles—previously, these posed occasional challenges. During both field surveys and days spent at archives, I’ve experienced enhanced endurance and less reliance on scheduled rest periods. My physiotherapist observed significant gains in gait efficiency, a decrease in compensatory upper body movements, and greater proprioceptive stability.

## 3. Current Hillwalking Routine & Physiotherapist-Recommended Adjustments

A major part of maintaining my health involves regular hillwalking and standing-walking days at research archives. The following table outlines both my current practices and the targeted adjustments recommended by my physiotherapist:

| Route Name           | Typical Duration | Elevation Profile            | Current Frequency          | Recommended Adjustment                                 | Stretching/Prehab Protocol                |
|----------------------|------------------|------------------------------|---------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| Criffel Circular     | 3 hrs            | Steep ascent/descent (570m)  | 1x/week                   | Alternate weekly with gentler routes such as Mabie Forest | Dynamic warm-up, post-walk stretching    |
| Mabie Forest Trails  | 2 hrs            | Rolling terrain (200m)       | 2x/month                  | Increase to 1x/week                                   | Pre-walk hip mobility & glute activation |
| Screel Hill Loop     | 4 hrs            | Mixed terrain (344m)         | 1x/month                  | Limit to 1x/month; focus on shorter routes post-archive | Mobility drills, balance exercises       |
| Archival Days (Glasgow/Edinburgh) | 6–8 hrs standing/walking | Urban/flat                 | 2x/month                   | Incorporate micro-break mobility & standing stretches | Lumbar & calf stretches during breaks    |

**Adjustment Highlights:**  
- **Route Alternation:** I now rotate between more demanding high-elevation routes and gentler rolling terrain week by week. This allows my tissues to recover and helps guard against overuse injuries.
- **Prehabilitation Program:** Each session begins with dynamic drills targeting my hips and hamstrings, followed by calf, quad, and lumbar stretching after walks. I work in single-leg balance and proprioception exercises three times weekly, complemented by glute bridges and core stability work twice a week.

These recommendations mirror protocols from the Chartered Society of Physiotherapy [1] and align with British Journal of Sports Medicine guidelines for lower limb injury prevention and healthy activity in older adults [2].

## 4. Reflective Analysis: Impact on Fieldwork and Academic Activities

The evolution of my physiotherapy protocol is directly benefiting both the physical demands of my research and the efficiency of my work schedule.

- **Mobility and Endurance:** Improved stride mechanics and increased joint range are reducing my fatigue during protracted hillwalks. I can now comfortably extend outdoor surveys and traverse challenging landscapes with less strain than before.
- **Injury Mitigation:** By regularly alternating strenuous routes with easier ones and following strict stretching/self-care regimens, I'm actively reducing the likelihood of injuries, which, in the past, sometimes led to missed research days.
- **Flexible Scheduling:** Recovery is quicker, so I can fit archival visits more closely after hillwalks, shrinking downtime between these physically and cognitively demanding activities.
- **Archive Days:** Incorporating micro-breaks and mobility exercises throughout long sessions of standing and walking keeps my joints and muscles resilient. This reduces the risk of cumulative aches and pains that may otherwise slow my progress when reviewing materials.
- **Sustained Research Output:** Consistent physical well-being is translating to more reliable engagement with my research projects, thus supporting the maintenance and expansion of my publication record and the completion of long-term archival surveys.

## 5. Implementation Plan and Adherence Strategies

To maintain these improvements, I’m integrating several practical measures into my routine:

- **Self-Monitoring:**  
  I track weekly physical activity and adherence to stretching, either using digital logs or a notebook. Monthly assessments of gait, flexibility, and balance help me stay aware of my progress.
- **Regular Physiotherapy Check-Ins:**  
  I’ve scheduled monthly visits with my physiotherapist to assess progress and adjust protocols where needed. Every six months, a comprehensive review—including repeat gait analysis and updated joint flexibility assessment—will provide a detailed overview.
- **Feedback Loops:**  
  Before each hillwalk, I jot down a brief note about how I feel—energy, readiness, any minor aches. Afterward, I record observations about fatigue or discomfort. After busy archival weeks, I seek feedback from my physiotherapist regarding muscle soreness or recovery trends.
- **Reminders and Digital Supports:**  
  Mobile alerts prompt me to complete specific stretches before and after walks. I also use step counters and balance apps to support daily movement goals and keep motivation high.

These strategies are informed by the Scottish Government’s physical activity framework [3] and best practices in self-monitoring technology from Physiotherapy Canada [4], ensuring that adherence remains practical and sustainable within the context of my daily life and academic responsibilities.
