# Comprehensive Report: Dr. Alastair McEwan’s Keynote Lecture  
**Spring Symposium, Cumberland and Westmorland Antiquarian & Archaeological Society (CWAAS)**  
**Venue:** Tullie House Museum, Carlisle  
**Date:** 20 February 2024  
**Presenter:** Dr. Alastair McEwan, Senior Historian, University of Glasgow; Research Committee Member, CWAAS

---

## Executive Summary

Dr. Alastair McEwan’s keynote address at the CWAAS Spring Symposium, held at Tullie House Museum in Carlisle on 20 February 2024, offered substantive new perspectives on the history of communities along the Anglo-Scottish border. Focusing especially on manuscript research and the integration of recent photographic survey data, Dr. McEwan illuminated the adaptive strategies, resilience, and evolving identities of borderland populations, with particular attention to the River Esk region. His approach, centered on the rigorous analysis of underutilized primary sources, provided fresh evidence of community responses to environmental change and highlighted innovative methods for combining archival documents with modern survey technology.

The lecture prompted lively interdisciplinary debate regarding research methodologies and the future direction of local historical studies. Attendees contributed targeted questions and expert feedback, especially on topics such as environmental adaptation and artifact provenance. As a result of the session, CWAAS established protocols for acquiring rare regional pamphlets, advanced collaborative projects on cross-border river management, and outlined a clear path for integrating digital and traditional archival practices in ongoing research.

## Keynote Lecture: Objectives and Structure

### Objectives

Dr. McEwan aimed to:

- Re-examine the historical responses of borderland communities to social, environmental, and political shifts, with a particular focus on the River Esk.
- Showcase the value of integrating original manuscripts—many previously overlooked or inaccessible—with fresh photographic surveys of the local landscape and historic features.
- Stimulate debate on how local adaptation fits within broader British historical narratives, moving beyond conflict to emphasize resilience and cooperation.

### Central Themes

- **Adaptation Along the River Esk:** Dr. McEwan traced the interplay between efforts at environmental management, local economic adjustments, and the formation of community identity, using the River Esk as a primary case study.
- **Harnessing Primary Sources:** The lecture featured a detailed review of 16th–18th century records, including parish registers, estate papers, and regional pamphlets, to shed light on the mechanisms of local adaptation.
- **Historiographical Reinterpretation:** Dr. McEwan challenged the traditional “Border Reivers” narrative, arguing for a more nuanced view that foregrounds community adaptation over persistent conflict.
- **Material and Visual Culture:** Maps, sketches, and current high-resolution photographic surveys were juxtaposed with written documents to enrich the historical context and understanding of riverine adaptation.

### Lecture Structure

1. **Introduction**  
   Dr. McEwan began by outlining the need for a revisionist approach to Border history, emphasizing the underappreciated role of collaborative adaptation.
2. **Case Studies**  
   He presented a detailed analysis of the River Esk, drawing on parish records and recent field survey data to elucidate patterns of response to flooding, shifting river courses, and resource management.
3. **Manuscript Review**  
   The lecture included an in-depth exploration of notable manuscripts—including a newly digitized 1587 Scottish volume—and rare pamphlets that document both civic and environmental management.
4. **Methodological Reflection**  
   Dr. McEwan contrasted traditional archival research with emerging imaging and survey techniques, demonstrating how these combined approaches produce more holistic historical insights.
5. **Conclusion and Panel Discussion**  
   The session concluded with a panel that synthesized audience feedback and integrated suggestions for future acquisition and research protocols.

### Methodologies

Dr. McEwan’s lecture was grounded in:

- **Historiographical Analysis:** Situating local adaptation strategies within the broader context of British and Scottish historical scholarship.
- **Primary Source Research:** Drawing directly from parish records, unpublished Scottish manuscripts, estate papers, and recent photographic surveys commissioned by CWAAS.
- **Interdisciplinary Collaboration:** Incorporating perspectives and methods from archaeology, environmental science, and archival studies to deepen the analysis of riverine communities.

The question and answer session highlighted several key areas of interest:

- **Environmental Adaptation:** Fiona MacLeod’s question on historical responses to flooding along the River Esk led to an engaging discussion. - **Integrative Methodology:** Professor Ewan Sinclair commended the seamless integration of photographic survey data with manuscript evidence and suggested deeper exploration of environmental context in future studies.

## Source Analysis and Selection

### Primary Sources and Survey Materials

Dr. McEwan’s research drew upon a range of newly-accessible and often underused sources:

- **Eskdale & Canonbie Parish Registers (1580–1720):** Provided demographic data and evidence of migration and population shifts in response to environmental pressures.
- **Scottish Manuscript (1587):** Recently digitized, offering detailed insights into cross-border civic agreements and collaborative riverine management strategies.
- **Seventeenth-Century Pamphlet (“Flood Pamphlet”):** Unpublished document chronicling community responses to flooding, with acquisition negotiations currently underway.
- **Photographic Survey Data (2022–2023):** Commissioned by CWAAS, comprising high-resolution images that document river course changes and adaptation structures in the landscape.
- **Buccleuch Estate Papers (1650–1720):** Manuscripts detailing land tenure, river rights, and principles of border management.

#### Rationale for Selection

Each source was chosen for its originality, direct relevance to adaptive themes, and capacity to shed light on aspects of border history that have remained largely unexplored. The integration of recent photographic survey data enabled Dr. McEwan to test historical interpretations against contemporary environmental evidence, underscoring the dynamic relationship between past and present.

## Panel Discussion Highlights

### The 1587 Scottish Manuscript

Dr. McEwan led a focused segment on the digitized 1587 Scottish manuscript, which documents unique cross-border initiatives to manage the River Esk’s environmental challenges. This source provides rare evidence of collaboration and mutual adaptation between English and Scottish authorities and communities.

- **Content and Analysis:** The manuscript details agreed emergency protocols, physical barriers, and strategies to mitigate damage from flooding—offering a vivid portrait of civic pragmatism in the face of environmental uncertainty.
- **Discussion Points:** Attendees debated the manuscript’s provenance, with Mr. Graham urging exploration of private family collections for related municipal records that might broaden the evidentiary base. The group agreed on the value of expanding research to neighboring rivers and towns to assess whether similar adaptation strategies were adopted elsewhere.
- **Research Actions:** CWAAS resolved to initiate a digital catalog documenting adaptive techniques found in regional manuscripts, aiming to facilitate comparative studies and public engagement.

## Outcomes and Forward Strategy

### Key Findings

- The conjunction of manuscript evidence and modern survey data has revealed a pattern of resilience and locally-driven response among border communities, emphasizing decentralized adaptation rather than mere reaction to distant authority.
- Previously neglected artifacts—such as unpublished pamphlets and estate papers—are now reconceptualized as vital resources that fill gaps in the historical narrative of environmental and social adaptation.

### Acquisition and Partnerships

- Work has begun to secure and digitize the 17th-century “Flood Pamphlet,” in discussions with a local collector.
- Partnerships have been established or strengthened with the Cumbria Archive Centre and the National Library of Scotland to enable continued access to regional archival materials.

### Future Directions

- **Expanding Research Horizons:** The team will broaden historical analysis to include adjacent river systems and intensify efforts to record oral histories, recognizing the value of living community memory alongside archival documentation.
- **Developing Digital Resources:** Plans are underway to create a fully searchable, publicly accessible digital repository, encompassing surveyed sites, historical maps, and high-resolution facsimiles of manuscripts.
- **Strengthening Collaboration:** Formal joint projects will be established with hydrologists, environmental historians, and archival technologists to ensure multidisciplinary rigour and innovation.
- **Securing Funding and Engagement:** Applications are in progress for the Heritage Lottery Fund, with the goal of supporting ongoing fieldwork, digitization, and enhanced public involvement in local history initiatives.
