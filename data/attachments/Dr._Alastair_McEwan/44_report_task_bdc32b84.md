## Executive Summary

This report presents a comprehensive account of a weeklong field research trip across four significant medieval sites in Brittany: Saint-Thégonnec, Locronan, Château de Fougères, and a previously undocumented rural chapel. Our approach combined rigorous on-site documentation and careful critical analysis, blending photography, architectural survey, and study of archival sources. The findings not only highlight the complexity and regional variety of Brittany’s religious and military buildings but also bring to light previously unrecorded inscriptions and architectural interventions with the potential to revise existing narratives about the area. We encountered several methodological challenges—particularly regarding digital data capture and consistency of environmental conditions—but these obstacles have already begun to shape a more targeted, collaborative research agenda moving forward. Our work reinforces the importance of firsthand field research and careful engagement with both written sources and physical remains in reconstructing the medieval heritage of Brittany.

---

## 1. Introduction

Our journey through Brittany (14–21 October 2024) was anchored by a commitment to deepening the understanding of the region’s medieval religious and military architecture. Each of the four selected sites offered perspectives on distinct historical phenomena—from parish devotion and funerary practices, to patterns of conflict and fortification. Throughout the trip, we prioritized a transparent methodological framework, thorough site analysis, and clear documentation of newly discovered materials. Core objectives included the production of detailed analytic summaries, methodical disclosure of procedures, documentation of as-yet unrecorded features, and the establishment of clear connections with current academic discourse. In doing so, we aimed not just to catalogue findings but to contextualize them within broader debates about the evolution and diversity of medieval life in northwest France.

## 2. Research Methodology

### 2.1 Equipment and Software Used

Our documentation relied on equipment and workflows designed to maximize accuracy and reproducibility:

- **Photographic Documentation:**  
  We used a Nikon D850 digital SLR, alternating between the Nikkor 14-24mm wide-angle lens for full façades and interiors and the 50mm lens for details. Images were shot in RAW (NEF), with ISO adjusted to conditions and aperture priority selected for depth of field. All photos included GPS metadata, which streamlined our archival and cataloguing processes.

- **Measured Drawings and Notes:**  
  Observational sketches were made using Staedtler Mars Lumograph pencils in A3 Moleskine sketchbooks. A Leica DISTO D2 laser measure provided precise dimensions, which were cross-referenced with GPS coordinates (Garmin GPSMAP 65s). We digitized and annotated all sketches using an iPad Pro with Procreate and Notability, allowing for immediate integration of field observations and measurements.

- **Primary Source Correlation:**  
  Access to digital archival materials, including paroissial cartularies, regional hagiographies, and correspondence from departmental archives (Finistère and Ille-et-Vilaine), enabled a close tie-in between tangible remains and documentary evidence. Key documents were translated as necessary using ABBYY FineReader and specialized translation modules for Latin and French.

- **Data Management & Software:**  
  Adobe Lightroom Classic (v13.0) served as our main image management tool. To ensure redundancy, all data were backed up immediately on a Samsung T7 Shield SSD and uploaded daily to encrypted ArcGIS cloud storage. File integrity was secured with SHA-256 checksums, and all sources were organized within a Zotero project library with full bibliographic cross-referencing.

### 2.2 Data Backup and Record Authenticity Protocols

Consistent data security and verifiability were critical throughout our fieldwork:

- **In-field Backups:**  
  After each site visit, we saved all new files to SSD, followed by synchronization to a desktop backup at day’s end. Checksum comparisons were run to confirm data integrity.

- **Record Control:**  
  We assigned sequential, time-stamped filenames including operator initials and embedded metadata. Notes and sketches were digitized daily, each photo of a physical drawing including a scale and orientation mark.

- **Authenticity Measures:**  
  All primary inscriptions and architectural features were photographed with appropriate scales and color calibration targets. Architectural measurements were taken twice; any discrepancies over 3% were explicitly flagged in the logs. Digital access logs tracked post-processing and sharing, ensuring clear audit trails for collaborators and future reviewers.

## 3. Site Analyses

### 3.1 Saint-Thégonnec

#### Historical Context

Saint-Thégonnec stands as one of Brittany’s most elaborate parish closes, dating from the 16th and 17th centuries. The site encompasses a calvary, ossuary, and an ornately decorated church, exemplifying the broader surge in Counter-Reformation Catholic art and architecture fueled by elite patronage. Parish cartularies (ed. Kerandraon, 1732) document not only the ongoing financial and spiritual investment of the local nobility but also the negotiation between reformist and traditional elements in art and liturgy.

#### In-Field Observations and Historiographical Integration

Our close inspection of the calvary revealed patterns of lichen growth and stone weathering unique compared to nearby Guimiliau, suggesting variances in local microclimate or perhaps differential conservation histories. We identified remnants of original polychromy on several statues, their pigment layers corresponding to findings in recent analytical work (G. Le Bihan, 2021). The ossuary’s inscriptions provided further differentiation—distinctively Franciscan in tone and content, rather than echoing the Benedictine templates more common in the region. These details point to a locally nuanced approach to both spiritual messaging and visual culture.

We identified a faint but clearly legible late 17th-century Latin inscription cut into the ossuary threshold:  
- *Transcription*: “Memento mori: Resurgetis in gloria”  
- *Translation*: “Remember death: You shall rise in glory”

Comparative research confirms the formula is unrecorded at this site in previous inventories, though similar phrases appear in nearby Morlaix parish records.

Dense overcast made it difficult to document pale pastel pigments, even with supplemental lighting. In addition, restricted access during early morning liturgical events required us to adapt our research schedule, returning in the afternoon for certain interior details.

### 3.2 Locronan

#### Historical Context

Locronan has preserved the layout and built substance of a medieval town remarkably intact, encapsulating both civic and religious elements. Its annual Troménie pilgrimage, anchored by devotion to St. Ronan, continues a tradition documented in medieval hagiographies (notably the 12th-century Vita S. Ronani, MS Quimper 1281). Notarial registers and town accounts provide additional context for Locronan’s economic and religious trajectory.

#### In-Field Observations and Historiographical Integration

Surveying the market square, we discovered that several paving slabs had been re-used from religious contexts, as evidenced by the visible medieval carvings. This reflects established patterns of resource optimization in Breton towns during the late Middle Ages.

Within St. Ronan’s Church, the iconography of the east window reflects predominant regional Gothic trends but features an unusual depiction of St. Christopher likely tied to the town’s prominence as a pilgrimage stop—supported by documentary links to a 1484 Papal indulgence. Documentation of devotional graffiti on the nave piers not only confirmed longstanding hypotheses about lay participation but, through systematic cataloguing, suggested a richer-than-expected matrix of personal engagement.

### 3.3 Château de Fougères

#### Historical Context

Château de Fougères is among Europe’s largest and most complex medieval fortresses, its history closely interwoven with both Breton and French royal interests between the 12th and 15th centuries. Our archival investigations drew on municipal sources and focused comparisons with marcher castles in England and Wales. Chronicles such as Froissart’s account of the 1405 siege helped contextualize the observed masonry chronology.

#### In-Field Observations and Historiographical Integration

Analysis of wall stratigraphy on the western curtain confirmed the reuse of blocks during early 14th-century repairs attributed to Philip le Bel. Defensive ditches have been kept partly waterlogged, which has both limited decay and improved our access to the base of the donjon following recent clearing.

A careful examination of multiple arrow slits revealed evidence of later chiseling, supporting the idea that significant adaptations followed the widespread adoption of artillery in the region. The pattern of these architectural modifications aligns with broader European trends in castle defense.

### 3.4 Undocumented Chapel

#### Historical Context

Located about five kilometers southwest of Locronan, this single-cell, stone-built rural chapel had eluded all published inventories and prior regional surveys. Based on architectural details and materials, we attribute its origin to the late 14th century. Oral testimony collected from Jean Le Men, a neighboring farmer, revealed evidence of a burial tradition associated with this chapel, discontinued during the French Revolution and left unmapped in parish accounts.

#### In-Field Observations and Historiographical Integration

The chapel is built of local mica schist, with granite quoins and a notably steep roof pitch, echoing early mendicant designs. Above the simple portal, an incised Christogram (“IHS”) and vine motif decorate the tympanum.

Inside, we uncovered a partially exposed ledger slab engraved with “G.B. 1427”—an inscription unseen in published sepulchral surveys. The initials may correspond to Guillaume Bihan, a local notary referenced in nearby archival records. This dating pre-dates any known written references to the chapel by several decades, suggesting an earlier phase of rural devotional practice.

## 4. Critical Analysis: New Hypotheses, Debates, and Next Steps

The patterns emerging from our fieldwork invite a series of new lines of inquiry. Saint-Thégonnec’s use of Counter-Reformation iconography and funerary inscriptions diverges in subtle but significant ways from neighboring sites, suggesting a more locally modulated response to broader Catholic reforms. We recommend targeted pigment sampling and further comparative studies on conservation approaches.

The documentation of devotional graffiti at Locronan substantiates the idea of widespread lay engagement and points to socio-religious practices that have left few written traces. Expanding similar surveys across Cornouaille could reveal a broader pattern of ritual graffiti tied to pilgrimage and communal intercession.

At Fougères, newly recognized masons’ marks and architectural modifications make a stronger case for a regionally distinctive response to the arrival of artillery, warranting comparative study with other major Breton fortresses. Meanwhile, the discovery of the undocumented chapel, along with its early 15th-century burial stone, underscores the richness of rural religious life beyond formally recorded parish structures; it also highlights the value of oral testimony in identifying sites overlooked in textual sources.

Moving forward, we propose the following research priorities:
1. Detailed pigment and stone analysis at Saint-Thégonnec and Locronan to refine our understanding of material usage and conservation histories.
2. A wide-ranging project cataloguing graffiti and inscriptions across key Cornouaille sites, utilizing 3D scanning to facilitate nuanced comparative work.
3. Renewed archival research focused on the evolution of parish boundaries and the history of small rural chapels.
4. Closer collaboration with local conservation teams to develop uniform standards in documenting and preserving at-risk features uncovered during field surveys.
