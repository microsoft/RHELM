**Examination of a 1587 Scottish Manuscript—McNaught’s Antiquarian Books**  
**Date: 25 March 2024**

---

### Manuscript Overview

On 25 March 2024, I examined a Scottish manuscript dated 1587, acquired from McNaught’s Antiquarian Books. This entry documents my in-depth assessment of the manuscript’s provenance, physical features, notable marks, and broader significance within sixteenth-century Scottish manuscript culture.

---

#### Provenance

The manuscript displays a robust combination of features consistent with Scottish origin, as supported by reference materials from the National Library of Scotland, the British Library, and the Scottish Record Office. Although I did not find an exact match in existing catalogues, several key indicators strengthen the regional attribution:

- **Historical Annotations:** Notes within its pages reference feudal land holdings, ecclesiastical appointments, and legal statutes characteristic of the late sixteenth century Scottish context.
  
- **Marginal Script:** The marginalia are written in Scottish Secretary Hand, a style I confirmed by comparing letterforms to specimens dating from 1560–1595 found in Scottish palaeography guides [4]. These notes often touch on Kirk-related affairs and legal proceedings.

- **Watermarks:** Many folios bear watermarks—most notably a crowned thistle and an interlaced knot—that match symbols documented in Briquet’s *Les Filigranes*, which were prevalent in Scottish paper production circa 1550–1600 [1].

Further signs of provenance include handwritten catalogue numbers in Secretary Hand on the inner cover, following a custom found among privately held manuscripts in the Lyon Court archives. The absence of any modern library stamp supports the manuscript’s retention in private hands through successive generations.

Chemical analysis reveals the ink matches the profile of locally produced Scottish iron-gall ink; its deep brown hue and consistent oxidation patterns align with manuscripts stored in fluctuating humidity typical of the region [2].

---

#### Condition and Material Features

**Binding:**  
The manuscript is bound in calf leather, which shows age-related wear but also evidence of professional restoration carried out in the nineteenth century, particularly along the spine. Original sewing supports and hand-stitching have survived, matching known Scottish binding techniques from the late sixteenth century [3].

**Script and Layout:**  
Text throughout is rendered in Scottish Secretary Hand, distinguished by sharp, angular forms. Humanist script appears selectively in titles and certain marginal notes. Comparison with palaeographic samples from the National Library of Scotland supports a late sixteenth-century date [4].

**Paper and Parchment:**  
Most folios are composed of a medium-thickness paper with pronounced chain and line marks, in addition to the noted watermarks. Two types—the crowned thistle and interlaced knot—are especially significant for Scottish attribution. Occasionally, parchment inserts mark legal documents; these are notably lustrous, with visible folds suggesting they were frequently referenced.

**Ink:**  
The ink’s deep brown color, iron-gall composition, and signs of modest oxidation and feathering are all typical of Scottish manuscripts. The wear and mild bleeding in places hint at environmental changes over centuries, consistent with prolonged regional storage.

---

#### Distinctive Features and Content

- **Marginalia:** Both Latin and vernacular Scots marginal notes provide vivid snapshots of contemporary issues, from feudal disputes to ecclesiastical proceedings. Their reference to themes from the Register of the Great Seal of Scotland [5] ties the manuscript to the wider administrative and social changes of the era.

- **Annotations:** Several folios contain detailed notes on land divisions and feudal disputes involving recognized Scottish families, sharpening the possible lines of provenance.

- **Decoration:** Heraldic motifs, including thistle and fleur-de-lis, embellish key sections of the manuscript. These symbols are highly representative of sixteenth-century Scottish heraldic style [6].

### Reflections, Implications, and Forward Steps

The evidence supports the manuscript’s status as a rare record from a transitional era of Scottish ownership and authentication. The uniqueness of the owner’s mark reinforces its value in tracing private heraldic identification in the century following the Scottish Reformation. The manuscript’s blend of private symbols, state imagery, and alliance motifs paints a vivid picture of how such documents became vehicles of family identity and social negotiation.

Opportunities for further research are clear:

- I plan to undertake more granular analysis of the ink and paper composition, collaborating with specialist laboratories for authentication.
- High-resolution imaging of the owner’s marks will be submitted to the Lyon Court and local historical societies for peer verification.
- Expanding searches to less-accessible private family archives may reveal further examples or related documentation.
- I will investigate whether the initials and heraldic symbols correspond to documented events or transactions in feudal Scotland, and survey contemporary manuscripts for parallel marks and motifs.

Collaboration will be central to this next phase. I aim to consult directly with experts in Scottish heraldry and manuscript studies at the National Library of Scotland and Lyon Court, share preliminary findings for peer review, and ultimately publish a detailed palaeographic and iconographic analysis in accordance with ISBD/EAD standards.
