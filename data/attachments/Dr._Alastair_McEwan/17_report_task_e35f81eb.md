## Archival Activities at McNaught’s Antiquarian Books

### 1. Overview

Today’s session at McNaught’s Antiquarian Books was dedicated to systematically identifying and cataloguing 17th-century marginalia within selected folios from the shop’s collection. Working closely with Dr. Alastair McEwan and Isobel Grant, I focused on preserving and interpreting marginal evidence of early modern readership, ownership, and interaction with texts. Throughout our work, I adhered to the highest archival standards, referencing best practices from both UK and international guidelines.

### 2. Methodology

Our approach combined careful physical examination with digital documentation:
- **Identification & Description:** I assigned folio numbers to every item bearing marginalia, then described each inscription in terms of language, content, provenance, and script characteristics. Careful attention was paid to distinguishing individual handwriting traits—something Dr. McEwan, with his palaeographical expertise, was especially adept at highlighting.
- **Photographic Documentation:** Each annotated folio was photographed in high-resolution, uncompressed TIFF format. I followed recommendations from The National Archives and the Society of American Archivists, ensuring that every image included both embedded and external Dublin Core metadata fields for ease of cataloguing and future retrieval.
- **Preservation:** I implemented a rigorous three-copy backup policy (3-2-1), with local secure server storage, institutional cloud backup, and geographic redundancy by saving copies off-site.
- **Cataloguing:** All details were entered into a digital register designed around DACS (Describing Archives: A Content Standard), capturing both the essential and the contextual.

### 4. Digital Imaging and Metadata

For each folio, I used:
- **TIFF files for archival quality; JPEGs for day-to-day access.**
- **File Naming Convention:** [Institution]_[Folio Number]_[File Type]_[Sequence], e.g., McNaughts_Folio017r_TIFF001.tif.
- **Metadata:** Every image received thorough Dublin Core metadata—title, creator, description, date, language, identifier, provenance, format.
- **Backup:** I maintain at least three digital copies in distinct storage environments to safeguard against data loss.

These procedures stand in line with international standards and current thinking on the interpretive value of marginalia in book history [3][4][5].

## Culinary Notes: Local Produce for the Spring Gathering

### 1. Overview

Following the morning’s archival work, I spent the afternoon gathering ingredients for a planned spring gathering. I sought out local vendors who maintain a tangible connection to Dumfriesshire’s culinary history, choosing ingredients rooted in traditions that reach back to the 17th and 18th centuries. With every purchase, I found myself reflecting not just on the flavours, but on the deeper connections between today’s foodways and those of centuries past.

### 2. Historical Context

Dumfriesshire’s regional cuisine has long centered on oats, mutton or lamb, root vegetables, dairy products, and honey. This is well attested both in household accounts from the period and in manuscript cookbooks. I intentionally sought out vendors whose premises either date back to these eras or continue practices first established then. It gave the provisioning process a unique resonance—a sense that this was more than shopping but, in a way, a reenactment of shared community traditions.

## Reflections and Next Steps

### 1. Major Findings and Insights

Today's discoveries underscored the enduring value of careful, primary research. Among the marginalia, I located two annotations not previously catalogued: one, an economic comment on the price of oats in a difficult year, offers rare and direct testimony to the local impact of crop failures. Entries like this connect economic hardship with the lived experience of book readers in early modern Dumfries [4].

The recipe note about treating sore throats with honey and barley water perfectly exemplifies the intersection between domestic medical knowledge and everyday cooking, echoing recent scholarship on the social environments in which such knowledge was exchanged and preserved.

Meanwhile, the produce I purchased for the spring gathering is not only a culinary decision but also a demonstration of resilience and continuity. Many of the shops where I sourced my ingredients keep traditions alive from the 18th century or earlier. It was easy, while carrying the local honey and oats, to feel the presence of past generations participating in the same yearly rituals.

### 2. Gaps and Future Directions

Some intriguing questions emerged from today’s work:
- **Marginalia Attribution:** Several ownership inscriptions and comments require more definitive attribution. I plan to compare these signatures and hands against known examples from local Dumfries families—particularly those connected to local ministries and merchant houses.
- **Digital Humanities Expansion:** With the groundwork for systematic digitization now established, further development could include full-text transcription and network analysis of annotation data. This would enable much richer collaborative research and open the archive up to a broader scholarly audience.
- **Foodways and Oral Histories:** There is great potential in tracing how traditional recipes were adapted or preserved in Dumfries households. Incorporating oral histories or memory studies could reveal powerful links between present-day practice and the documented habits of earlier centuries.

### 3. Professional Development

Today also reaffirmed the value of meticulous archival and digital protocols. The approach—especially in file preservation, metadata, and redundancy—sets a strong standard for comparable antiquarian archives [1][2]. Collaborating with Dr. McEwan and Isobel Grant further deepened the analysis, merging local historical expertise with academic rigour. This multidisciplinary synergy shone through in both the interpretation of marginalia and the contextual understanding of provenance.
