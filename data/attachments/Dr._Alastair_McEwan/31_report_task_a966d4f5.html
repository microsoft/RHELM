Archival Digitisation Report: Lochmaben Parish Register (1692)

Project Title: Digitisation of the Lochmaben Parish Register (1692)
Date of Digitisation: 17 June 2024
Archivist: Alastair McEwan
Institutional Affiliation: University of Glasgow

Executive Summary

The Lochmaben Parish Register from 1692 is a foundational document for the study of both ecclesiastical and social history in Dumfriesshire, Scotland. By digitising this register to strict archival and historical research standards, we have greatly improved both its accessibility and long-term preservation. The project safeguards a fragile and vital resource, ensuring that researchers from diverse disciplines—including history, genealogy, conservation, and regional studies—can fully engage with its contents.

Dumfriesshire has long held a pivotal role in Scotland’s religious and civic developments, particularly during the turbulent late seventeenth century. The Lochmaben register captures far more than just lists of baptisms, marriages, and deaths; its marginalia and notes provide direct insight into local disputes and community realities, such as the reference to a lost chapel. These records not only trace demographic and liturgical patterns but also document the shifting landscapes of local governance, spiritual life, and social cohesion—making the register an indispensable resource for both academic research and community heritage.

Physical Condition and Conservation Procedures

Preserving a seventeenth-century parish register presents many challenges, requiring both expertise and careful planning. On initial assessment, the register displayed common issues typical of its age: iron gall ink corrosion, foxing, water damage, faded marginalia, and previous repair attempts using non-archival materials.

Throughout preparation and digitisation, all procedures followed both PAS 198:2012 guidelines and the rigorous conservation protocols of the University of Glasgow. Only trained staff handled the register, using Tyvek supports, unbuffered tissue, and controlled filtered lighting (kept below 50 lux). Each session was documented in detail, and inventory checks ensured no loss or misplacement.

Preservation Challenges in 17th-Century Scottish Ecclesiastical Records

These registers commonly exhibit several vulnerabilities:
- Iron gall ink corrosion, often resulting in the loss of marginal notes and annotations. - Fluctuating temperature and relative humidity which increase the risk of biological damage from mould and pests, as well as parchment cockling. - Progressive deterioration of leather bindings, frequently leading to the loosening of spines and structural instability. - Remnants of older conservation treatments, such as the use of acidic adhesives or non-archival repair materials that can no longer be reversed.

Further technical details and conservation standards are referenced in Scottish Archives and Conservation Practice.

Digitisation Methodology

The digitisation process was designed with particular attention to both fidelity and conservation.

Equipment and Settings

  • Camera: Phase One XF IQ4 150MP
  • Lens: Schneider Kreuznach 80mm LS
  • Lighting: LED panels at CRI > 95, 3200K temperature, equipped with polarising filters to minimise glare and risk
  • Copy Stand: Kaiser RS 2-XA archival stand
  • Resolution: 600ppi for full-page scans, ensuring sharp detail and archival quality
  • Colour Profile: sRGB/AdobeRGB with custom targets (X-Rite ColorChecker Passport) for accurate colour rendition
  • File Formats: Uncompressed TIFF for archival masters, JPEG/JP2 for access

Protocols and Standards

All imaging adhered to FADGI 4-star and ISO 19264-1:2017 standards. Every scanned image incorporated colour charts, a scale ruler, and page-specific metadata. For fragile folios, we used a cradle set to a minimum angle, and glass plates were used only where safe and strictly necessary to flatten pages. Post-processing was limited to essential cropping and orientation; no image retouching or artificial enhancement was undertaken.

Metadata Schema

Metadata was fundamental to the project’s goals—each image is catalogued with Encoded Archival Description (EAD) fields, integrated with Dublin Core descriptors for maximum searchability. Metadata includes item-level reference, capture date, script, preservation condition, and notes on any observable marginalia. This approach supports both long-term preservation and broad dissemination across academic platforms.

Lighting and Environmental Controls

Throughout digitisation, exposure was kept below 50 lux, monitored with a Sekonic C-800 spectrometer, preventing both UV and IR damage. Ambient temperature and humidity were logged for each session (maintained between 18–22°C and 45–55% RH), further reducing conservation risks.

Historical Findings and Analysis

Digitisation has provided new opportunities to examine the register’s contents in greater detail, including several previously overlooked features.

Marginal Note on Lost Chapel (Folio 32v)

A remarkable discovery emerged on folio 32v—a marginal note, rendered in Secretary hand, which reads:

"Anno 1692. The Chapel by the moss, now lost, reported by elders at session."

This short annotation provides primary evidence for the existence of a chapel that does not appear in known secondary literature. The choice of script suggests authorship by either a session clerk or one of the kirk elders. Its reference to "the chapel by the moss" offers a novel point of inquiry, revealing further nuance in Lochmaben’s religious landscape during the late seventeenth century.

Context and Significance

  • The note aligns with regional accounts of post-Reformation disappearance of smaller chapels, a phenomenon evidenced in Dumfriesshire but not directly documented for Lochmaben prior to this.
  • Session records from the period reflect shifting places of worship, often triggered by property disputes and population shifts in the aftermath of the Covenanter period.
  • Existing county histories make no mention of this specific chapel, marking the annotation as an important primary source. Its discovery encourages renewed archival and genealogical examination in both local and national contexts.
  • The faded state of the marginalia also illustrates how vulnerable such historical data can be, underscoring the rationale for careful preservation and digitisation.

Paleographic Observations

Analysis of the register’s handwriting shows primary entries in Scots cursive, with occasional marginalia and annotations in Secretary hand, likely scribes or elders. Marginalia have suffered significant fading as a result of ink corrosion, but careful digital enhancement has recovered notable detail and improved scholarly access.

Recommendations for Further Research

To fully realise the register’s scholarly and heritage potential, several avenues merit exploration:

  • Cross-Referencing Archival Sources: A systematic study comparing the Lochmaben register with Church of Scotland synod minutes, session records, and medieval charters is advised, particularly to triangulate references to "the chapel by the moss" and clarify its historical trajectory.
  • Genealogical Integration: Linking register data with Dumfriesshire family records can support both academic research and community genealogy projects, presenting opportunities for comprehensive demographic mapping.
  • Conservation Science Collaboration: Continued investigation into ink corrosion and earlier repair materials would benefit from partnerships with local heritage conservators, informing best practices for future preservation.
  • Digital Humanities Initiatives: Engagement with the National Records of Scotland to advance OCR technology and paleographic analysis could significantly enhance the accessibility and interpretability of similar historical documents.
  • Publication and Exhibition: Findings—particularly around unique marginalia and preservation insights—are suitable for submission to the "Scottish Historical Review" and for digital exhibition, ideally in collaboration with the University of Glasgow and Dumfries & Galloway Archives.
