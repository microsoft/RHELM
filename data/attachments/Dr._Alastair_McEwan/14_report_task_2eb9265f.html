Formal Investigation into the Provenance of a Medieval Scottish Borders Manuscript: Owner's Marks, Laird Custodianship, and Regional Historiography

Introduction

Tracing the provenance of medieval manuscripts offers valuable insights into the history of ownership, the circulation of texts, and their cultural significance—especially in regions distinguished by complex local dynamics such as the Scottish Borders. This report presents the findings of a detailed investigation into a manuscript believed to originate from the Borders, with special attention to owner's marks and their associations with local laird families. Through a combination of meticulous cataloguing techniques and careful archival comparisons, the study reconstructs the custodial history of the manuscript and assesses its influence on regional historical narratives. The aim is to provide both a comprehensive account of the manuscript’s journey and suggestions for future research directions.

Context and Research Objectives

The Scottish Borders region, shaped by a rich tapestry of minor laird families and fluid allegiances, has long depended on private manuscript ownership as a means to consolidate local identity and historical memory. Personal owner's marks—ranging from signatures and heraldic symbols to cryptic marginalia—are essential for tracking the circulation of manuscripts among these families and reconstructing networks of custodianship.

The investigation set out to achieve the following goals:

  • Systematically document the manuscript’s physical and scribal characteristics.
  • Identify, catalogue, and analyze all owner's marks present in the manuscript.
  • Match these markings against signatures and custodial marks preserved in local archives, including charters and ledgers.
  • Assess the authenticity and historical relevance of these marks as evidence of laird custodianship.
  • Situate the findings within the broader context of regional historiography and ongoing debates about medieval manuscript provenance.
  • Provide recommendations for expanded archival and field research.

Methodology

Documentation and Cataloguing

Each aspect of the manuscript was documented using established paleographical and codicological practices:

  • Foliation: Careful analysis of folio structure and numbering.
  • Material Type: Verification of the writing substrate, identified as fine Scottish parchment.
  • Scribe Identity: Evaluation of script features using hand analysis and colophon inspection.
  • Illuminations: Detailed inventory of decorated initials, miniature illustrations, and color application methods.
  • Marginalia: Systematic recording and interpretation of notes, symbols, and distinctive annotations written in the margins.

Both handwritten and digital records were maintained to ensure completeness and preservation. Magnification (with a 10–20× hand lens) was used to study ink composition and stroke patterns, revealing micro-level details often missed by the naked eye.

High-Resolution Imaging

To supplement manual cataloguing, high-quality photographs were captured using a Nikon D750 DSLR equipped with a macro lens. Images of owner’s marks and codicological features were given precise reference identifiers, directly correlating with manuscript folio numbers and locations. This created a robust visual archive supporting the written documentation.

Comparative Archival Research

Owner’s marks and signatures catalogued in the manuscript were compared against local archival material, specifically regional charter books, estate ledgers, and inventories relating to Border laird families. This comparative technique was crucial for confirming attributions and establishing the historical authenticity of the manuscript’s owner’s marks.

Documentation of Data and Process

Observations, measurements, and preliminary interpretations were recorded throughout research, with meticulous cross-referencing to the photographic record and archival comparison charts. This ensured that every finding was supported by both physical and documentary evidence.

Discussion

Attribution to the Pringle Family and Contextualization

The convergence of owner's marks—the Pringle’s inked signature, heraldic shield, and personalized marginal notes—directly tie the manuscript to the Pringle family of Smailholm, a historically significant but relatively minor laird family in the Borders. Archival comparisons confirm that the inked signature matches that found on regional charters dated to 1489, while the heraldic motif corresponds with family arms recorded in late 15th-century estate documentation.

Marginal notes in vernacular Scots, such as “This be longs to me, J.P.,” reinforce the manuscript’s use as a personal object rather than merely an institutional or formal text. The inscription dates match entries in estate ledgers, providing further support for the manuscript’s Pringle custodianship.

These findings reflect broader patterns observed in provenance research: laird families actively used manuscripts to reinforce social status, maintain genealogical continuity, and shape local authority. Instances of annotated owner’s marks suggest deliberate, intergenerational transmission, in line with known inheritance practices and manuscript keeping traditions of the period.

Impact on Regional Historiography and Provenance Scholarship

Documenting the personal ownership of manuscripts among minor laird families contributes significantly to understanding the circulation of texts in the Borders. The evidence underscores how owner's marks help shape regional historical narratives—demonstrating the persistence and agency of individuals and families in preserving and transmitting texts outside monastic or royal circles.

By firmly locating the manuscript’s provenance within Border laird archives, this investigation adds nuance to debates about the nature of medieval manuscript custodianship in Scotland. The research also demonstrates the value of integrating traditional paleographic description with modern digital methods. High-resolution imaging, partnered with archival comparisons, enables both scholarly rigor and preservation for future research.

Conclusion and Recommendations

Analysis of the manuscript’s owner’s marks, archival comparisons, and physical characteristics strongly supports its provenance with the Pringle family of Smailholm during the late 15th century. Owner's marks—inked signatures, heraldic motifs, and personal marginalia—provide a robust and authentic trail of evidence for laird custodianship and manuscript transmission within the Borders.

To build upon these findings, further research should involve a comprehensive survey of additional Pringle family and regional laird archives, with attention to incorporating unpublished charter books and estate ledgers. Wider comparison with other contemporary Borders manuscripts may clarify changing patterns in owner’s mark usage and deepen understanding of how such texts influenced local historical consciousness.

Expanded fieldwork, including collaboration with local archives and historical societies, will help to refine provenance mapping and contribute to a richer, more nuanced picture of manuscript culture in the Scottish Borders.
