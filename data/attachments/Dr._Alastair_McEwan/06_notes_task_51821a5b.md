## II. Expanded Activity Details & Observations

### A. Morning Hillwalk by River Nith

The crisp air this morning was invigorating. I walked the familiar path along the River Nith, observing the subtle changes in plant life since last week. Wild garlic was just beginning to emerge, mingling with established hazel and alder at the water’s edge. The presence of five mute swans gliding upstream and two darting kingfishers made for an unexpectedly lively scene. Tracking the course of the river, I paused near the abandoned salmon station—a landmark steeped in history according to the *Statistical Account of Scotland*. Photographing the bank’s new erosion features, I wondered if recent rainfall patterns are accelerating seasonal changes. My intention is to match these visual records with maps and reports from the Dumfries Museum to clarify where agricultural use may have altered riverside land over the years.

### B. Breakfast Preparation: Steel-cut Porridge with Honey & Brambles

After my walk, preparing breakfast felt ritualistic. I carefully measured the oats—a staple in my pantry and in Scottish history—letting them simmer while the kitchen filled with a heartening aroma. Drizzling in fresh honey from Robertsons Apiary always feels like a small tribute to the region’s beekeepers. The brambles I’d foraged were deeply colored, and folding them into the porridge brought back memories of my childhood breakfasts. Consulting early cookbooks and talking with older locals affirms that these simple ingredients have sustained families for generations. Honey, too, is praised in regional folklore and referenced as a healing ingredient in oral histories and the Dumfries Folklore Archives. Eating this meal always leaves me feeling connected to local tradition and provenance.

### C. Annotation Session: Medieval Treatises

My research desk was crowded with manuscripts for today’s annotation session. Focusing on *Vita Sancti Columbae* and *Chronica Gentis Scotorum*, I identified themes of place, political narrative, and the significance of environmental features—especially rivers, roads, and trade routes in local histories. Color-coded annotation allowed me to track recurring motifs. The process echoed medieval monastic methods, where careful scholarly notes ensured knowledge would endure. Comparing my observations with published secondary literature provided deeper context for differences in transmission and commentary across time. I’m planning to expand this analysis further during my next library visit.

### D. Video Call with Professor Sinclair

Connecting with Professor Sinclair always feels like energizing the research process. We delved into sedimentary analysis along the River Nith, aligning our field observations with archive-based maps. Our discussion on collaborative digital mapping sparked new ideas—perhaps incorporating oral history contributions from the community to track changes in river heritage. We also agreed to co-author a journal article on the findings, with a focus on methodologies and their implications for landscape reconstructions. To maintain transparency, I promptly circulated call minutes to both our teams, ensuring all institutional procedures are observed.

### E. Rare Book Cataloguing Session

The rare book cataloguing revealed several fascinating details today. A previously undocumented volume of Scots Psaltery featured a hand-written marginalia and evidence of mid-19th-century readership through an old school library stamp. Another acquisition—a collection of regional poetry—had a note tucked inside, likely from its first owner. Systematic cataloguing, using MARC21 standards, allowed me to cross-reference these treasures against existing library pedigrees. I’m still tracing the provenance of a manuscript treatise whose author isn’t listed; this requires further archival digging. Documenting the chain of ownership and historical context is vital for establishing the value and authenticity of each item and will significantly enhance both research quality and future exhibitions.

### F. Evening Lecture Note Drafting

I dedicated the evening to preparing lecture notes for next week’s seminar. The intersection of Scottish river systems, manuscript traditions, and local economies offers a compelling narrative. Drawing on primary and secondary sources, I traced how rivers shaped trade—and by extension, the movement and preservation of texts. The idea that environmental and geographic networks fostered the growth of early academic culture is gaining traction as I prepare for group discussion. I’ve outlined questions for students to explore the link between landscape transformation and intellectual activity across centuries.
