## Morning Lecture: The Evolution of Tower Houses in the Scottish Borders

Today’s seminar on Scottish Borders tower houses drew a full room and lively engagement from my third-year undergraduates. The discussion spanned the transformation of these structures from the late medieval period into the early modern era, highlighting ongoing historiographical debates, regional architectural characteristics, and the significance of border dynamics.

**Historiographical Debates**  
We began by dissecting the traditionalist perspective, which casts tower houses primarily as fortified residences built in response to the region’s chronic instability. R.D. Oram’s and M. Coventry’s recent works invited the group to move beyond this militaristic frame and examine tower houses as markers of social status, kinship, and economic change. The students were particularly sharp in their analysis of primary estate records and travelers’ accounts from the National Records of Scotland, expressing skepticism about the reliability of sources shaped by contemporary anxieties and self-interest.

I encouraged them to consider the question of chronology, as historians continue to disagree over when these buildings proliferated. Some tie the surge directly to the post-Flodden climate of fear after 1513, while others see a more gradual process unfolding from the fourteenth century, shaped by shifting landownership and the rise of new local elites.

**Architectural Typologies**  
We explored the typological distinctions between rectangular keeps, L-plan towers, and the distinctive “peel towers” characteristic of the Borders. Students examined the physical evidence—barmkins, battlements, hall arrangements—drawing on recent digitized photogrammetry surveys. The conversation touched upon the use of local materials like whinstone and sandstone, shaped by the region’s geology and the ever-present proximity to English territory. I referenced several recent architectural surveys to illuminate the way adaptation to local conditions often produced hybrid forms, complicating neat typological categories.

**Regional Influences**  
With the Borders’ tumultuous political climate—a patchwork of alliances, shifting loyalties, and endemic raiding—tower house design and placement rarely followed simple patterns. The riding families, especially the Elliotts, Scots, and Kerrs, provided instructive case studies in how architecture reflected (and reinforced) territorial claims and family networks. Our discussions of rural settlement morphology underscored that tower houses anchored farming communities and shifted in function as concepts of lordship evolved.

**Student Engagement**  
I was delighted by the students’ willingness to interrogate both archaeological and archival sources, often challenging the asserted motivations behind tower house construction. One group asked how the development of Borders tower houses compared with Irish analogues—an avenue worth further exploration in future seminars. Another posed the question: were tower houses fundamentally defensive, or were they primarily status symbols? Their ability to synthesize digitized charters, local inventories, and vernacular chronicles led to thoughtful research hypotheses that integrated written, architectural, and landscape evidence.

## Office Hours Consultation: Ailsa Kerr – Dissertation Discussion

In the afternoon, I met with Ailsa Kerr to discuss the evolving scope of her dissertation, which examines kinship networks and the social functions of tower houses in Teviotdale between 1350 and 1600.  

Ailsa’s project grounds tower houses firmly within their agrarian and familial contexts, drawing on both written sources and physical remains. She expressed concern about source criticism, particularly regarding the bias and incompleteness of sixteenth-century legal documents and the selective nature of estate inventories.

I advised her to triangulate evidence by cross-referencing charters, inventories, and recent architectural surveys, emphasizing the necessity of understanding each source’s provenance, authorship, and historiographical context. To help clarify her methodology, I encouraged Ailsa to develop an annotated source grid and to deepen her engagement with both “functionalist” (defensive/status-oriented) and “networked society” frameworks, citing key works by Glendinning and Fawcett.

Ailsa responded with characteristic enthusiasm, agreeing to draft an annotated bibliography and a comparative matrix highlighting architectural variants across different family networks. She outlined plans to incorporate GIS-based spatial analysis to trace the relationships between tower house sites and kinship patterns, and expressed interest in examining newly digitized manuscripts catalogued by the University Library—materials referenced in Professor Sinclair’s notes. Her initiative and analytical rigor bode well for the eventual strength of her dissertation.

## Lunch Meeting: Digitized Manuscript Provenance Updates

During lunch, I caught up with Professor Ewan Sinclair over coffee in the Senior Common Room. Our conversation centered on the recent digitization and improved cataloguing of medieval Scottish manuscripts in the University Library.

The library’s archives now include several previously miscatalogued estate correspondences from the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries, many relating directly to Borders tower houses. The implementation of new cataloguing standards has clarified provenance chains—some manuscripts originated in the Douglas family archives and were only transferred to university custody after 1920. With digitization, remote research has become markedly easier and more reliable, especially for comparative architectural and social studies. We discussed the potential for collaborative metadata annotation and agreed that this initiative presents a golden opportunity for interdisciplinary research.

## Archival Research: New Findings Near Jedburgh

I devoted a good portion of the afternoon to archival work in the University Library Special Collections, focused on rare documents connected to lesser-known tower houses around Jedburgh.

Within the “Scottish Borders Estate Collections,” I came across a remarkable 1622 letter (SBEC/JED/17-112) from Sir Thomas Kerr to “James of Ancrum.” The letter provides candid commentary on maintenance issues, local disputes, and the strategic importance of “the old tower near the water’s bend”:

> “…the old house at the bend must be kept sure, for the wester winds and the restless borderers give us none rest. Its walls stand yet strong, though folk in Jedworth speak not much of it…”

This excerpt offers compelling evidence for the continued survival and regional significance of a previously undocumented tower house on Jedburgh’s edge. The material calls into question current settlement typologies and mapping—for example, Coventry’s _The Castles of Scotland_ omits this site altogether, highlighting a clear oversight in existing inventories. The find aligns with other documents from the archive, each reinforcing the persistent, evolving role of tower houses well beyond simple defensive architecture.

## Evening Preparation and Scholarly Reflection

As daylight faded, I updated my research notes with the Jedburgh findings and refined the slide deck for the upcoming CWAAS talk, incorporating both archival excerpts and mapping data. I composed several seminar discussion questions to challenge students to probe deeper into the historiography and regional particularities of Borders tower houses. The day closed with revisions to my annotated bibliography, ensuring new sources and discoveries are seamlessly integrated.

These research experiences reinforced how reconstructing medieval settlement landscapes demands attention to microhistorical detail in archival sources. The progress in digitization is significant, yet inconsistent provenance and patchy metadata remain obstacles that require ongoing vigilance. Discovering a neglected tower house in Jedburgh is a vivid reminder of how fieldwork and archival research must continue to inform each other, perhaps supplemented soon by geophysical surveys to confirm the site’s location and features.

Some pressing scholarly questions emerged from today’s discoveries:

- How do newly uncovered archival references challenge or reshape established chronologies and typologies of Borders tower houses?
- What systems should be put in place to enable effective cross-verification of digitized archival records?
- In what ways can field archaeology build on spatial perspectives derived from documentary GIS mapping?
