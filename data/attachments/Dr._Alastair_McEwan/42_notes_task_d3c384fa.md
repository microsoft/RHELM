## Executive Summary

On 27 September 2024, a meticulous backup operation was completed for a suite of newly digitised materials central to medieval Scottish history. The files, gathered from prestigious academic repositories and national heritage collections, were securely ingested into the institutional digital archive with robust metadata to ensure enduring scholarly utility and provenance.

Each file was safeguarded using the internationally recognised 3-2-1 digital preservation model: three copies were created, stored across two different media types (local SSD and secure cloud), with one copy held offsite to mitigate geographic risks. Data integrity checks, including cryptographic checksums and encryption for confidential content, were systematically applied. The backup process concluded with comprehensive validation for each file—no errors or data losses were detected, and all retention and access policies were fully documented.

This approach adheres strictly to established protocols for historical research data preservation. Multi-layered documentation and distributed redundancy are essential foundations for risk mitigation, enabling reliable academic review and supporting ongoing scholarship and formal committee audits (e.g., CWAAS). By following these best practices, the integrity and accessibility of Scotland's medieval heritage are preserved for future generations.

## Metadata and Traceability Standards

Each record incorporates multiple layers of metadata for scholarly robustness and compliance:

- **Author Identification:** Derived either directly from the original manuscript or reconstructed via paleographic and archival assessment.
- **Provenance:** Details the document’s historical custody, including repository of origin and specifics of digitisation.
- **Period Determination:** Established using archival continuity, cataloguing references, and (where necessary) expert paleographic dating.

Additionally, standardised metadata schemas—PREMIS (Preservation Metadata), Dublin Core, and CWAAS protocols—were applied within the repository. These allow for future audits, enable tracking of preservation actions, and facilitate transparent version control for academic review.

## Best Practice Recommendations for Digital Archival Preservation

### 1. Implement the 3-2-1 Backup Principle

Any digital historical materials should always exist in three copies, on two separate media (cloud and local SSD recommended), with one copy stored offsite. This approach is essential for disaster resilience, long-term viability, and risk mitigation, especially in academic environments where unique sources cannot be easily replaced.

### 2. Select Trusted Repositories and File Formats

Deposit materials into repositories that are aligned with the OAIS Reference Model (ISO 14721), and certified as trustworthy (CoreTrustSeal, ISO 16363). Priority should be given to stable, non-proprietary file formats—such as PDF/A for documents, TIFF for images, and XML or UTF-8 encoding for texts—as recommended by national archives and preservation coalitions. These formats ensure enduring access and flexible migration.

### 3. Enrich Metadata to Strengthen Traceability

Each file should be accompanied by detailed metadata conforming to PREMIS, Dublin Core, and institutional cataloguing standards. Metadata ought to account for provenance, file format rationale, digitisation procedures, rights management, and any transformation events. This documentation safeguards academic rigour and simplifies future curation, migration, and committee review.

### 4. Protect Sensitive Sources

For files containing restricted or confidential information, backups must be encrypted both at rest and during transfer. Implementing role-based access controls and maintaining thorough usage logs is essential to uphold institutional policies and adhere to data protection regulations.

### 5. Routinely Validate Integrity and Refresh Media

Integrity-checking routines—including regular checksum and fixity audits—are non-negotiable aspects of digital stewardship. Periodic replacement or refreshing of storage media is crucial to counteract data degradation (“bit rot”) and preserve accessibility over time. All preservation actions should be logged for accountability.

### 6. Document Preservation Actions and Compliance

Consistent and transparent logging of all backup events and technical audits supports formal peer review and institutional accountability. Following CWAAS committee guidance and national/international standards ensures compliance and enables smooth transitions during archival handovers or research audits.
