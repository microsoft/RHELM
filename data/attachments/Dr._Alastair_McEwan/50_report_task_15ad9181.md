## Meeting Proceedings

### 1. Historical Context and Rationale for Digitisation Grant Proposal

Dr. McEwan began the meeting by introducing the university’s broader strategy for archival digitisation, referencing longstanding efforts within both UK and global contexts. He highlighted the British Library’s Digital Collections as a leading example, noting their improved preservation and expanded access, which have been pivotal in shaping historical research. The group discussed Millar’s “Archives: Principles and Practices,” which positions digitisation as an essential response to ongoing threats of physical deterioration and the growing need for remote access.[1] 

Within the University of Glasgow, institutional priorities are increasingly oriented towards open scholarship and digital innovation. McEwan shared a compelling anecdote in which undergraduate researchers, given digital access to previously fragile manuscripts, were able to incorporate rare primary sources into their dissertations—demonstrating tangible benefits that extend across teaching and research. This led to a consensus that a structured and sustainable digitisation programme is necessary, both for preservation and to maintain the university’s reputation as a leader in scholarly innovation.

The team outlined four primary motivations for the proposal:
- Safeguarding vulnerable documents and materials
- Broadening access for researchers worldwide
- Keeping pace with current funding imperatives in digital humanities
- Furthering the University’s status and influence through exemplary digital practice

Professor Sinclair emphasised how recent digitisation projects, particularly those focusing on early Scottish municipal records, have provided new avenues for postgraduate research and enriched professional development opportunities.

### 2. Expected Impact and Institutional Priorities

Attention then turned to the practical outcomes anticipated from a successfully funded digitisation initiative. The group agreed on a set of realistic goals, aiming to:
- Substantially increase annual engagement with the archives (with precise targets to be determined)
- Integrate digitised assets into the university curriculum, supporting innovative teaching methods such as digital pedagogy modules and project-based learning
- Strengthen the international profile of Glasgow’s collections, fostering collaborative links with European and global heritage networks

Discussions referenced case studies from peer institutions, noting significant boosts in both research visibility and public engagement following similar digitisation efforts. The committee recognised the value in tracking citation rates and broader impact metrics, underscoring the university’s commitment to data-driven evaluation.

### 3. Strategies for Community Engagement

A central theme of the meeting was the need to foster meaningful connections with community stakeholders—an objective widely endorsed as critical for both transparency and impact. Drawing on models developed by the Community Archives and Heritage Group, Dr. McEwan encouraged direct involvement of local historical societies, whose expertise in oral history and contextual interpretation has proven invaluable in prior collaborative projects.[2]

Proposed strategies included:
- Organising consultation events to establish dialogue between university staff, community groups, and regional archives
- Implementing participatory methodologies, allowing stakeholders to contribute directly to metadata enrichment and contextual annotation
- Inviting community representatives to serve as external advisors on the project steering group

Professor Sinclair pointed to guidelines from the International Council on Archives, recommending the creation of a community advisory committee. This additional layer of oversight would ensure inclusivity, transparency, and mutual learning throughout the project lifecycle.

### 4. Metadata Standards and Best Practices

The group then reviewed digital archiving protocols, agreeing on the importance of robust, internationally recognised metadata frameworks. Dublin Core was selected as the primary schema, supplemented by ISAD(G) for enhanced descriptive detail and digital provenance, and incorporating elements from ISO 15489 to ensure comprehensive records management.[3] 

Dr. Sutherland’s expertise in the area was recognised as a major asset. She is expected to guide the adoption of JISC-compliant standards and facilitate ongoing quality assurance, leveraging her experience in semantic web technologies. The literature underpinning these choices—including Millar’s “Archives: Principles and Practices” and recent JISC guidance—were cited as foundational to the project’s methodological approach.

The committee agreed to establish regular peer review cycles focused on metadata quality and interoperability, referencing recommendations from "Archival Science". This process will support continual enhancement of both technical protocols and user experience.

### 5. Integration Plan for Dr. Ingrid Sutherland

To ensure that technical workstreams proceed efficiently, Dr. Sutherland’s integration as co-investigator was mapped to several vital project roles. Her responsibilities will include leading the finalisation of metadata frameworks by 12 January 2025, designing and running quarterly seminars on digital curation, and serving as a liaison between project staff and the university’s IT services.

Additionally, Dr. Sutherland will oversee the evaluation and refinement of digitisation protocols, working closely with UKRI research collaboration teams and adhering to university policy. The team expressed confidence that her leadership in the Digital Humanities Hub will offer important capacity-building opportunities for early career researchers and technical staff.

## Institutional References and Historical Anecdotes

During the meeting, Dr. McEwan brought forward the story of an 1878 letter from a Glasgow city archivist—recently digitised—which played a pivotal role in inspiring last year’s local heritage exhibition. This concrete example underscored the practical community impact and scholarly benefits of making historical documents accessible. Professor Sinclair also highlighted the digitisation of Scottish Burgh records, describing this as a transformative advancement for the study of municipal history and its alignment with both national research priorities and broader European initiatives.

The committee reaffirmed that all decisions made are based on up-to-date literature, institutional policy, and best practice standards, with careful attention to compliance and scholarly rigor.
