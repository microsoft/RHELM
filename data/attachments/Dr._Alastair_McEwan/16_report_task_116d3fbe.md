## Executive Summary

This report presents an in-depth provenance annotation and bibliographical analysis of the manuscript *De Ordine Monachorum Scotiae*, drawing on both historic and contemporary cataloguing standards as of 13 April 2024. The assessment encompasses comprehensive documentation of all known ownership marks, detailed biographical research into significant custodians, and comparative analysis with related ecclesiastical material across Scotland.

Through systematic investigation—including folio-by-folio physical examination, paleographic study, and research in national and regional archives—the manuscript’s journey across several centuries has been clarified. Evidence indicates that it passed through both institutional monastic hands and private clerical ownership in Dumfriesshire during the 18th century, before entering the collection of a late 19th-century bibliophile. New provenance details, particularly concerning its association with Alexander Stewart, provide crucial context for its trajectory.

Recommendations for updating the catalogue record are included, ensuring that recent findings are integrated and the manuscript’s historical significance is accurately represented for ongoing and future research.

### Analysis

The manuscript bears a series of ex libris marks that reflect its complex chain of custody. The earliest, inscribed in flowing 18th-century round hand on folio 1r, clearly identifies Alexander Stewart as owner in 1778. Comparative paleographic study matches this inscription with Stewart’s correspondence housed in Dumfriesshire County Archives, supporting attribution beyond reasonable doubt.

The armorial stamp on folio 34v, displaying a Lion rampant and the legend "Bibliotheca Monachorum Scotiae," is characteristic of mid- to late-18th-century Scottish monastic institutional marks. This design closely resembles those found in the Glasgow University Hunterian Manuscript collection, specifically those attached to titles sourced from pre-Reformation religious houses.

The pencil annotation "S. McC. 1891," seen on folio 68r, documents its later transition to the McCulloch family, active collectors noted in National Library of Scotland records.

Beyond these formal marks, the manuscript’s margins preserve layers of annotation in different hands, indicating active scholarly use and suggesting regular circulation between ecclesiastical, private, and academic readers. Each transition in ownership aligns with shifts in the manuscript’s function and accessibility—from ecclesiastical reference to private study—allowing for a nuanced reconstruction of its provenance over the past three centuries.

## Profile: Alexander Stewart—18th-Century Clerical Custodian

### Biographical Overview

- **Full Name:** Alexander Stewart  
- **Birth:** Circa 1745, Kirkmahoe Parish, Dumfriesshire  
- **Clerical Career:** Ordained curate at St. Michael’s Church, Dumfries (1773–1785)  
- **Research Sources:**  
  - Kirkmahoe Parish Registers, Dumfriesshire Archives  
  - *Fasti Ecclesiae Scoticanae*, Vol. 2, p. 317  
  - Diocesan correspondence in Dumfriesshire County Archives  

Stewart’s period as curate coincided with an active era of antiquarian interest in Dumfriesshire. Parish and diocesan records document Stewart’s engagement with both local and monastic traditions. His bookplate within *De Ordine Monachorum Scotiae* marks him as a participant in efforts to preserve ecclesiastical memory following the disruptive changes wrought by the Scottish Reformation.

His stewardship of the manuscript fits with parish-level practices of integrating former monastic volumes. Diocesan letters frequently reference Stewart’s library and his facilitation of manuscript exchanges among neighbouring clergy. In this period, rural Scottish curates were often central to the informal conservation and study of historical materials, and Stewart’s acquisition and annotation of this manuscript reflect these broader patterns. His legacy is further evident in the continued scholarly preservation of works once held by individual clergymen in the region.

## Methodology

Research proceeded through several key steps:

- **Descriptive Cataloguing:** AACR2/RDA standards were applied, ensuring consistency with the National Library of Scotland’s cataloguing and shelfmark conventions.
- **Physical Examination:** Each folio was inspected for evidence of ownership marks, changes in handwriting, and codicological features suggesting use or transfer.
- **Paleographical Analysis:** Handwriting was systematically compared to established regional models, with reference to *Fasti Ecclesiae Scoticanae* and existing library provenance reports.
- **Archival Research:** Manuscript and institutional records from both national and Dumfriesshire archives provided context and confirmed linkages among recorded owners.
- **Documentation:** All marks were photographed at high resolution, transcribed by two independent experts, and incorporated into digital cataloguing records according to NLS protocol.

## Conclusions and Recommendations for Catalogue Enhancement

Substantial evidence now traces *De Ordine Monachorum Scotiae* from its presumptive monastic origin through 18th-century clerical stewardship and ultimately to late 19th-century private collectors, marking it as an artifact of both religious and bibliographic transition in Scotland.

To reflect this enriched provenance, the following catalogue updates are recommended:

- Incorporate the Alexander Stewart ownership inscription, together with a biographical note cross-referenced to parish records and *Fasti Ecclesiae Scoticanae*.
- Document the monastic provenance suggested by the Lion rampant armorial device, linking this evidence to comparative holdings at Glasgow University and the National Library of Scotland.
- Append the chain of subsequent ownership, detailing transfer to the McCulloch family and referencing supporting archival materials.
- Update the manuscript description to provide a comprehensive account of ownership marks, marginalia, and transitions in use, ensuring optimal accessibility for future research.

These recommendations will bring the manuscript’s record in line with the highest standards of UK and Scottish provenance practice, significantly enhancing its value for scholarly inquiry in historical, bibliographical, and ecclesiastical studies.
