The West March Border Towers

Keynote Lecture – CWAAS Symposium

Dr. Alastair McEwan
3rd February 2024
Cumberland & Westmorland Antiquarian & Archaeological Society (CWAAS) Annual Symposium

Lecture Outline

Introduction

The border towers of the West March are enduring symbols of resilience, practical adaptation, and evolving authority along the turbulent Anglo-Scottish frontier. From the late medieval era into the seventeenth century, these towers shaped — and were shaped by — local responses to near-constant threats, ranging from cross-border raids to dynastic disputes. Far more than mere defensive structures, they anchored communities, provided platforms for local governance and justice, and served as visible assertions of identity and status in a divided landscape.

The peculiar character of this region, with its tangled webs of kinship, shifting loyalties, and the competitive ambitions of English and Scottish crowns, is reflected in every stone. Major original sources including the Calendar of Border Papers and personal letters from figures such as Sir Robert Carey render vivid accounts of daily life marked by raids and negotiations. More recent scholarship — notably Rachel Armstrong’s analysis of Borderland identity — encourages us to view the towers not only as military outposts, but as nodes of negotiation in the wider processes of state formation and local strategy.[1][2][3][4]

Historical Context

The rise and development of border towers in the West March emerged both as an organic response to chronic instability and as a product of deliberate state intervention. By the fifteenth century, violent factional rivalries and shifting power dynamics among local families — notably the Maxwells, Armstrongs, and others — led to the proliferation of fortified homes. These structures served both to protect their inhabitants during periods of acute unrest and to provide sanctuary for the wider community in times of crisis.[5][6]

During the Wars of the Roses and the Marian crisis, the rapid changes and uncertainties necessitated new administrative structures. The establishment of the Warden Courts, whose detailed records (such as BL Cotton MSS Caligula B/X and NRS GD224/452) chronicle the use of border towers, brought together legal, military, and social functions under one roof. These towers frequently hosted sessions of law, mediated disputes, and organized musters for local defense.

Familial alliances often dictated allegiances across the border, as illustrated by estate maps (RHP series) and correspondence preserved in state papers. Marriages served both to consolidate power and reinforce defensive networks, ensuring that towers functioned as points of connection as much as bastions of refuge.

Strategic placement was critical. Towers were typically sited to command river crossings — for example, those at Lochmaben and Annan — or stand sentinel over important fords and routes. This combination of architectural advantage and social authority was continually renegotiated as the frontier zone moved between peace and turmoil.

By the seventeenth century, a gradual decline in cross-border violence and the expansion of agriculture saw many of these towers adapted for peaceful use. Former fortresses were transformed into farmsteads or private residences, with traces of their martial origins lingering in their floor plans and fabric.

Architectural Analysis

The architecture of the West March border towers reveals a careful blend of defensive innovation, regional adaptation, and evolving domestic needs. Systematic field surveys and recent archaeological research have reconstructed their typical features:

  • Layout: Towers most commonly followed rectangular or L-shaped plans, with walls often exceeding 2 meters in thickness for formidable resistance.
  • Basements: Vaulted undercrofts or cellars provided shelter for both livestock and people during times of danger, reflecting the region’s agricultural base and endemic conflict.
  • Parapets and Walkways: Crenellated parapets and roof walks gave defenders a commanding view and protection during sieges or attacks.
  • Materials: The towers were built from locally quarried sandstone, set with lime mortar, and reinforced internally with oak beams and doors — materials chosen for their availability and durability.[7][8]

Distinctive architectural variations are notable between Dumfriesshire and North Cumberland. Dumfriesshire towers, such as Hoddom, frequently feature spiral staircases and “shot holes” for firearms. In contrast, North Cumberland constructions, like Netherby Hall, present more pronounced domestic arrangements and larger window openings, indicative of later phases focused on comfort and status rather than pure defense.

This analysis has depended on the integration of archival documentation — including antiquarian sketches, estate maps (BL King’s Maps), and architectural diagrams — with modern archaeological surveys. The continuous collaboration between historical and field evidence has clarified our understanding of shifting priorities in tower construction.

Anecdotal Insights: Local Memory and Oral Tradition

“In the summer of 1947, my father recalled guiding livestock past the stunted stone edifice of Tower Brae, remarking on how its cellar had once harbored both cattle and ‘refuge from the riding Scots.’”
— Oral testimony, Alastair McEwan Sr., 1947 (interview transcript 2018)

Oral traditions are invaluable supplements to the written and archaeological record, adding color and depth to our understanding of daily life in and around these towers. Memories collected through projects such as the Michaelmas Oral History Project deepen our appreciation of the towers’ flexibility — from defensive refuge during raids to their long-standing role in local agricultural practices.[3][9]

Residents’ recollections frequently mention the practical adaptations made by their families, recalling stories of sheltering livestock and neighbors during heightened tensions or repurposing ancient basements as storage and workspaces long after the days of violence had passed. These everyday accounts illuminate aspects of tower life rarely captured in administrative records or architectural surveys, underscoring their dual roles as both fortifications and economic hubs well into the twentieth century.

Mapping the Border: Cartographic Perspectives and Comparative Evidence

Sixteenth-century maps of the West March provide critical evidence of the towers’ importance, their strategic placements, and changes in the landscape. Each map brings its own insights:

Saxton’s work, for example, provides the earliest systematic English county mapping, fixing towers as key elements in the administrative landscape. In contrast, manuscript materials such as GD420/2 and the Cotton Map carry more bespoke, practical functions for contemporary administrators and magistrates. Taken together, these cartographic sources enable comparative analysis of how perceptions of the frontier changed over time, both in official planning and in local consciousness.

Methodologies and Historiographical Perspectives

Research into border towers must blend source triangulation — weaving together oral history, archival documentation, and field archaeology — with contemporary methods such as digital imaging, GIS mapping, and microhistorical analysis. While antiquarian methods contribute a rich foundation, newer techniques offer precision and broader geographic context.

Debate persists within scholarship regarding the prevalence and nature of “border lawlessness.” David Reid’s recent study, Border Law and Order, refines our understanding of social stability and negotiated governance, challenging older narratives that overstated chaos and disorder.[8] These discussions highlight the need to balance stories of violence and instability with evidence of cooperation, economic exchange, and evolving local administration.

Most of the most valuable primary data is preserved in the National Records of Scotland (GD series, RHP maps), the British Library’s Cotton Manuscripts and maps, Durham University Special Collections, and the growing oral archive of the CWAAS.

Conclusion

West March border towers are more than mere remnants of medieval conflict; they are living testimonies to the ability of border communities to adapt, negotiate, and thrive in the face of danger. Their transformations from military bastions to centers of domestic and agricultural life reflect wider shifts in political culture, social organization, and rural economy.

For researchers, these towers are gateways to the microhistory of the Anglo-Scottish frontier — sites where architectural analysis intersects with political power, kinship networks, and local ingenuity. The continued integration of archival sources, archaeological evidence, cartographic analysis, and oral tradition ensures a more nuanced, vivid, and evidence-driven understanding of this region.

Looking forward, interdisciplinary research — blending digital spatial tools, comparative architectural studies, and expanded oral history collection — will further uncover the cross-cultural influences and subtle transitions embodied in these towers. Collaborations with organizations such as Historic Environment Scotland and international peer societies will be crucial in deepening our perspective and preserving both the structures and their stories for future generations.
