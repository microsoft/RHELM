### Meeting Proceedings

#### 1. Review of Archival Protocols

The meeting began with a brief recapitulation of committee protocols focused on safeguarding rare materials. Both attendees underscored the necessity of thorough provenance documentation and adherence to best practices in cases of lost archival items. Dr. McEwan referred to recent training updates and stressed the importance of cross-checking chain-of-custody records whenever an item is reported missing.

#### 2. The 1623 Pamphlet: Historical Relevance

Dr. McEwan emphasized the exceptional scholarly value of the 1623 pamphlet, noting its status as a rare primary source for seventeenth-century Scottish religious and social history. Its unique annotations had become vital references in several faculty and graduate projects, particularly those examining early modern debates and community life. Losing access to such firsthand materials interrupts ongoing inquiries and poses challenges for the integrity of future research publications.

Ailsa Kerr added context, sharing details about the pamphlet’s provenance and the extensive efforts invested in cataloging rare items. She acknowledged both professional responsibility and a deep sense of personal investment in the collection. Ailsa’s remarks highlighted the wider significance of the pamphlet—not only to current research but to the university’s archival legacy.

#### 3. Impact on Current Research

The loss of the pamphlet has created tangible setbacks across multiple research streams. Dr. McEwan explained how several projects, including his own, are now in limbo due to the missing original document—especially those dependent on its unique marginalia, which are absent from existing transcriptions or published editions. The disruption has extended to collaborative research, publication schedules, and grant accountability, with several co-authored works now requiring significant revisions or delays.

As the discussion unfolded, it became clear that the pamphlet’s absence represents not just a singular loss, but a broader interruption to the university’s scholarly activities. Both attendees agreed that maintaining open lines of communication with affected teams would be crucial in managing expectations and facilitating support.

#### 4. Emotional Context and Archival Responsibility

Throughout the meeting, a strong sense of accountability was evident. Ailsa voiced her frustration and distress over not having recovered the pamphlet despite thorough searches and strict adherence to established protocols. Dr. McEwan expressed concern and empathy, recognizing the heavy responsibility borne by archival staff and the broader committee. Both participants reiterated their commitment to transparent communication and mutual support, acknowledging the emotional pressure accompanying their professional obligations.

#### 5. Recovery Strategies and Next Steps

The committee discussed a practical, multi-pronged recovery plan:

- **Review Recent Acquisitions:** Ailsa will oversee a comprehensive audit of recent entries and check-in logs to confirm whether miscataloging or shelving errors occurred.
- **Physical vs. Digital Catalog Reconciliation:** Dr. McEwan will cross-reference the digital catalogue with physical holdings to identify discrepancies that might signal the pamphlet’s location.
- **Outreach to Peer Institutions:** Ailsa will initiate direct contact with partner archives to establish whether the pamphlet was transferred, loaned, or misdirected during past collaborative exchanges.
- **Incident Documentation:** Both participants agreed to file a detailed report following University of Glasgow archival recovery protocols, documenting all search efforts and preliminary findings for future reference and accountability.
- **Committee Reporting:** Dr. McEwan will prepare a comprehensive incident report and submit it for peer review and publication by early December, ensuring transparency and scholarly integrity.
