## Morning Lecture: Medieval Scottish Fortifications

Today’s lecture offered a compelling exploration into the evolution of medieval Scottish fortifications, moving chronologically from the early motte-and-bailey castles—which emerged under Norman influence during the 12th century—through to the robust stone keeps and, eventually, the characteristic tower houses that dotted the Scottish landscape from the late 14th century onward.

We began by examining the motte-and-bailey castles: simple earthwork mounds constructed as early assertions of authority, often using timber halls. These structures, though relatively modest, signaled the imposition of royal or magnate power in regions formerly fragmented. Over time, defensive demands and technological advancements pushed castle-builders to embrace stone. Stirling and Edinburgh Castles, with their imposing keeps and fortified curtain walls, exemplify this evolution—not only in their resilience but also in their monumental scale and refined architecture.

The seminar highlighted the proliferation of tower houses, particularly in border regions. These fortified residences reflected ongoing threats from feuding clans and repeated English incursions. Their compact yet defensible design responded pragmatically to the realities of persistent conflict, and their endurance over centuries testified to their utility and symbolic potency.

### Strategic Context and Siting

One aspect that struck me was the deliberate placement of castles at key strategic locations—borders, river crossings, and on defensible hills. These fortifications functioned on many levels. Militarily, they served as strongholds during the Anglo-Scottish Wars of Independence in the late 13th and 14th centuries. Administratively, they hosted courts and local governance, acting as seats of justice and facilitating royal authority. The examples of dramatic sieges, like that of Stirling Bridge in 1297, underscored the volatility of control and the negotiation—both military and diplomatic—that determined the fate of such strongholds.

### Castles and Social Power

Our discussion naturally expanded into the broader socio-political significance of fortifications. Castles were never merely military installations; they became tangible symbols of clan identities, royal consolidation, and shifting allegiances. Through analysis of charters and medieval chronicles, we traced how castle construction often mirrored the consolidation or fragmentation of local power, influenced by the ceaseless dynamics between Scottish and English interests.

### Pedagogical Reflections

The seminar itself was unusually lively. Visual aids—including detailed floorplans and period illustrations—anchored our architectural analysis, revealing both technical details and patterns of daily life. Reading firsthand extracts from John of Fordun’s *Chronica Gentis Scotorum* prompted an animated debate about the reliability and intentions of medieval chroniclers. Students responded particularly well to the anecdote of James IV’s innovative siege machinery at Norham Castle, which led to a fascinating conversation about technological advancements and their broader implications. Many in the room raised questions about the transmission of engineering knowledge and the movement of skilled artisans across contested borders.

We also delved into historiographical trends, especially those articulated by Charles McKean and Matthew H. Hammond, who challenge traditional defensive-centric narratives with interdisciplinary approaches. Their argument that fortifications increasingly embodied status symbols, not just military outposts, sparked further inquiry [1][2].

## Office Hours: Conversations with Ailsa Kerr on the Border Reivers

During office hours, Ailsa Kerr brought remarkable enthusiasm to a discussion on the Border reivers. She shared a rare annotated pamphlet from circa 1670, “A True Relation of the Occurrences in the Borders,” formerly owned by Sir Walter Scott—a detail I found especially delightful given Scott’s own fascination with Border history.

The pamphlet’s marginalia included pointed corrections to tales of reiver violence, with annotations that offered a more nuanced—and perhaps apologetic—view of local customs, blackmail (protection payments), and the complex loyalties of March Wardens. Its blend of commentary and amendment highlighted the negotiation between self-policing border communities and the often sensationalized reputation that reivers acquired. I was struck by how these scribbled notes, likely penned by someone close to the action, challenge sweeping generalizations found in printed sources.

This interaction illuminated broader questions about source reliability and regional stereotyping, themes at the heart of Anna Groundwater’s recent research. Her work, which positions reivers as both disruptors and agents of local order, provided excellent context for Ailsa’s arguments [3]. The conversation left me considering how communal self-regulation and external perceptions have shaped Border identity over centuries.

## Manuscript Study: 1587 Scottish Parliamentary Record at McNaught’s Antiquarian Books

My visit to McNaught’s Antiquarian Books uncovered a fascinating 1587 Scottish vellum manuscript, a rich blend of legal, parliamentary, and regional documentation.

### Physical Description

The manuscript itself, written in a crisp secretary hand common to late medieval Scottish administrative records, features rubricated headings and organized marginalia. The vellum remains surprisingly supple despite some staining near the spine, and the original calfskin binding, complete with functioning clasp, speaks to its careful preservation.

A beautifully illuminated initial adorns the section entitled “Act of Parliament touched upon the Scottish Marches,” while the signatures of three distinct notaries from Roxburghshire lend authenticity and provide avenues for prosopographical study.

### Content and Research Opportunities

Within its pages are parliamentary acts concerning border governance, legal customs on land tenure, and records of dispute arbitration—each revealing the intricate relationship between central legislation and local realities. Particularly intriguing is the detailed tax roll, which outlines expenditures for castle repairs and suppression of reiving. Minutes from council meetings discuss the fiscal challenges of fortification construction and maintenance, presenting a rare glimpse into government efforts to maintain order in a contested region.

The presence of multiple official signatures suggests collaborative administration and offers material for genealogical mapping. This manuscript appears to be an ideal source for investigating the practical underpinnings of Scottish border policy, mechanisms of peacekeeping, and adaptation of legal frameworks to local conditions.

## Musical Reflection: Maxwell Davies’s ‘An Orkney Wedding, with Sunrise’

Later in the day, I listened to Sir Peter Maxwell Davies’s vibrant orchestral work, ‘An Orkney Wedding, with Sunrise.’ Its lively narrative—portraying a traditional Orkney celebration—resonated deeply with themes from today’s lectures. As the music built to its evocative apex, with bagpipes heralding the sunrise, I felt reminded of how communal festivities and folk traditions have historically anchored Scottish identity, especially during turbulent times.

The piece’s folk melodies and dance rhythms echoed my earlier reflections on castles functioning not only as defensive structures but as hubs for social life and resilience. The sun rising over the festivities seemed to symbolize a renewal of collective spirit, linking art and history as parallel forms of cultural affirmation. I was left pondering how such traditions—musical, oral, and communal—intertwine with documentary history to shape regional narratives [4].
