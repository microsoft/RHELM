## Progress Report: Digitisation of the Lochmaben Parish Register

**Overview**  
This afternoon in the Dumfries University Archives, I continued work on digitising the Lochmaben Parish Register—one of the region’s most significant records for ecclesiastical and social history. The digitisation process strictly follows UK archival best practices and international standards to ensure both the preservation of original materials and the production of reliable digital surrogates.

### Technologies and Workflow

**Equipment and Imaging Approach**  
I used a Phase One XF IQ4 150MP medium format camera paired with a Schneider Kreuznach 80mm LS prime lens to capture high-resolution images of each folio. Lighting was carefully managed with LED panels (CRI > 95) set to a constant 5600K, diffused through archival-grade filters to reduce risk of photochemical damage. Camera settings were standardised at f/8, 1/60s, and ISO 100 for sharpness and grain control, saving master files at 3000 dpi in TIFF format, with JPEG derivatives generated for wider access.

**Conservation and Handling**  
Handling procedures remain a priority; I wore nitrile gloves and used a cushioned, V-shaped cradle set at a gentle 110° angle, supporting fragile leaves with polyester overlays. Prior to imaging, I inspected the register for any binding stress, unstable ink, or leaf fragility, addressing minor warping with a dry, calibrated support box.

**Digitisation Workflow**  
The digitisation process unfolded in several carefully managed stages:

1. **Preparation**: Conservation inspection covered binding, leaf integrity, and ink condition.
2. **Image Capture**: Each page was photographed sequentially. I annotated any physical damage or obscured text directly during capture.
3. **Metadata Entry**: Every image received session-specific metadata via Dublin Core, supplemented with collection codes and subject keywords for searchability.
4. **Quality Control**: After capture, I reviewed each file onsite, checking for colour fidelity, sharpness, and completeness.
5. **Archival Storage**: On completion, the register was immediately returned to its climate-controlled storage to limit exposure.

### Metadata Standards

Throughout the session, I applied the full suite of Dublin Core elements—Title, Creator, Date, Format, Language, Identifier, Source, Description, Coverage, Rights—to each image. These standard fields were complemented by collection-specific codes (“DUA/LPR/1462-1710”) and event notes detailing conservation actions and annotation events.

## Adherence to Best Practice and Session Challenges

I monitor the digitisation protocol against UK Archives and Records Association guidelines, ensuring routine checks for document handling and conservation standards, especially important with the notably fragile 16th–17th century parchment leaves. Several recurring challenges emerged this session. A number of folios exhibit advanced edge degradation, which complicates full-frame imaging. Faded marginalia and ink instability in older sections point to the need for future multispectral imaging. Additionally, minor warping from fluctuating humidity required temporary countermeasures using a drybox for register support during capture.

## Notable Discovery: Marginal Note on Undocumented Chapel Site

### Description and Context

While reviewing folio entries dated circa 1574, I noticed a marginal note written in faded brown ink, distinctly different from the main entry’s script. The note reads: “Item: capella apud Rydale croft, pro decimis colligendis, servitium factum ut moris est.” This reference to a chapel at Rydale croft does not appear in any currently known inventories. The content suggests the register recorded tithe collection for the chapel, indicating its active religious role in the late medieval period.

### Historical Significance

This brief marginalia expands our understanding of ecclesiastical geography in Lochmaben. The region, divided historically between the dioceses of Galloway and Annandale, is scantily documented for minor chapels prior to the Reformation. The reference ties local income directly to the Rydale croft site, providing rare insight into parish-level economic and administrative practices in the late 16th century.

Verification of the site’s activity could add a new location to Dumfriesshire’s known medieval religious landscape, opening doors for further research into settlement patterns and church functions.

### Wider Implications

- **Gaps in Current Inventories**: The note questions the completeness of existing chapel lists for Scotland and highlights the potential for further discoveries in parish registers.
- **Support for Ongoing Research**: The evidence aligns with initiatives by the Cumberland and Westmorland Antiquarian & Archaeological Society (CWAAS) focused on refining ecclesiastical boundary maps and studying lay religiosity.
- **Future Directions**: The discovery prompts interdisciplinary collaboration, including potential architectural and archaeological surveys of Rydale croft.

## Reflections and Future Directions

This digitisation session highlights the importance of maintaining rigorous archival standards while adapting to the challenges presented by fragile historic documents. The discovery of the previously undocumented Rydale croft chapel, recorded in a simple marginal note, is a compelling illustration of the value in detailed, systematic digitisation. Such finds have immediate relevance for regional historiography, offering new perspectives on the religious and social fabric of late medieval Dumfriesshire.

The tithe reference sheds light on the local interaction between laity and church, complementing ongoing research into parish-level practice and support systems. The evidence also signals promising pathways for further research—field surveys, boundary analysis, and oral history collection at Rydale croft could enrich our understanding of the chapel’s role and legacy.

Findings from today’s work will directly contribute to ongoing CWAAS mapping and inventory projects, as well as broader scholarship on the evolution and documentation of religious sites. The integration of digital, archival, and archaeological approaches ensures that each discovery—no matter how modest in appearance—is reliably secured and ready for collaborative investigation.
