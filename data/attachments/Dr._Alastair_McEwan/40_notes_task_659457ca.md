## Overview

This report presents a detailed itinerary for my upcoming research trip to Orkney, running from 23 to 29 September 2024. Throughout, I have woven opportunities for analysis, discussion, and reflection, aiming to maximize both collaborative exchange and personal synthesis of new findings. All arrangements are based on institutional sources and established contacts, ensuring both academic rigour and a well-supported research experience.

## Daily Schedule and Reflections

### Monday, 23 September: Arrival & Orientation

I arrived in Kirkwall in the afternoon, settling quickly into the Lynnfield Hotel which would be my base for the week. Eager to immerse myself in the local environment, I spent the later afternoon wandering the historic streets, taking initial photographs and notes on the distinctive stone buildings and town layout. The visit to the Orkney Museum offered a valuable introduction to the region’s urban development—its architectural typologies, and how shifting social needs have left their marks on the built landscape.

That evening, over dinner at the hotel, I joined a group of local scholars from the University of the Highlands and Islands (UHI). Our discussion covered the trajectory of Orkney's architectural forms and set the stage for more focused research in subsequent days. I appreciated both the warmth of their welcome and the energetic exchange about Orkney’s urban morphologies.

### Tuesday, 24 September: Medieval & Norse Architectural Heritage

This morning, I devoted myself to an in-depth study of St. Magnus Cathedral—one of Scotland’s most remarkable Norse-built ecclesiastical structures. Walking among the soaring arches and rich, red sandstone, I reflected on the Romanesque style and the fusion of Scottish and Norse influences that shaped its design. With the guidance of Dr. Ingrid Sutherland (UHI), I gained privileged archival access to cathedral plans and historical documents, broadening my perspective on the evolution of religious architecture in medieval Orkney.

The afternoon saw me exploring the Bishop’s Palace and Earl’s Palace, both reflecting the Renaissance era’s stylistic transitions and social complexities. Lunch at Judith Glue Café was restorative; the evening gathering, over dinner with Dr. Sutherland and oral historian Magnus Rendall, deepened my understanding of the Norse-Scottish cultural exchanges and their enduring impact on architectural forms.

### Wednesday, 25 September: Vernacular & Ecclesiastical Comparison

Wednesday was dedicated to fieldwork outside Kirkwall. Traveling by bus to Birsay, I visited St Magnus Church and the Earl’s Palace, taking detailed notes on their construction and the landscape context. The juxtaposition of Norse settlement sites and later Scottish buildings provided a vivid sense of the region’s layered histories.

Later, in Stromness, I toured merchant houses and held conversations with staff at the Stromness Museum, focusing on the relationship between architecture and social networks in smaller towns. The day’s discoveries, from rural churches to bustling townhouses, underscored how local traditions adapted over centuries. Lunch at Birsay Bay Tearoom offered a moment of quiet reflection. That evening’s dinner at the Stromness Hotel brought together museum staff and fellow researchers, and our conversation explored the influence of social dynamics on building traditions in rural Orkney.

### Thursday, 26 September: Archival Research & Social History

With Kirkwall as my focus, I spent much of Thursday in the Orkney Archives. Reviewing municipal, parish, and architectural records was absorbing; I was struck by the depth and continuity of the local documentation. Anne Sinclair, the archives manager, provided invaluable guidance in working through historic plans and personal records. I took the opportunity to annotate field notes during brief walks through Kirkwall’s lanes, cross-referencing the archival evidence against changes evidenced in the present-day streetscape.

Lunch at the archive’s tea room was informal and collegial—I welcomed the opportunity to discuss approaches and initial findings with UHI postgraduate researchers. Our dinner that evening was a collaborative affair, centering on archival methodologies and the initial insights emerging from Orkney’s local sources.

### Friday, 27 September: Estate Survey & Social Hierarchies

On Friday, I visited Skaill House, a stately 17th-century manor whose architecture reveals traces of layered social occupation. The guided tour with the curator was both comprehensive and engaging; I made detailed notes on the fabric of the house and its spatial organization. Exploring adjacent archaeological sites expanded my understanding of estate landscapes and how power was expressed through built form.

Lunch was taken at the house’s onsite café, allowing for informal conversation with museum staff. In the evening, I returned to the Lynnfield Hotel for a thematic gathering centered on the complex relationships between estates, patronage, and local communities. Discussions with Isobel Grant and Magnus Rendall illuminated the nuances of estate organization and its effect on regional history.

### Saturday, 28 September: Independent Research & Reflection

After five intensive days, Saturday was reserved for follow-up activities—additional time in the archives, secondary interviews, and reviewing emergent themes. I took advantage of the flexibility to revisit sites in Kirkwall, return to Stromness for supplementary discussions, and comb through archival material that had surfaced during the week.

Throughout the day, I made space for reflection—organizing notes, drafting preliminary analyses, and considering next steps for the project. The evening was spent in conversation with Isobel Grant over dinner, reviewing preliminary findings and discussing strategies for deeper analysis and future collaboration.

### Sunday, 29 September: Departure & Final Review

My final morning began with a farewell breakfast at the Lynnfield Hotel, joined by Dr. Ingrid Sutherland and Anne Sinclair. We reflected on the outcomes of the week, shared highlights, and identified promising areas for continued research. Before departing for Kirkwall Airport, I conducted an informal exit interview, consolidating feedback and outlining approaches to maintain professional ties with Orkney’s academic community.

## Transportation Logistics

Kirkwall served seamlessly as the central hub for the week—accommodation, archives, and key historic sites were all within walking distance or a quick taxi ride. For field trips to Birsay, Stromness, and Skaill House, I relied on Stagecoach bus services, which ran reliably and allowed me to plan my daily movements efficiently.

- **Sample routes:**
    - Kirkwall–Birsay: Stagecoach bus (~40 min)
    - Kirkwall–Stromness/Skaill House: Bus (~35 min)
    - Local sites: walking or short taxi rides

Advance bookings for taxis ensured flexibility when timetables shifted, and I found that traveling by public transport offered its own insights into the rhythms of island life. The landscape itself—open, windswept, and marked by centuries of travel routes—added a tangible historic context to the journey between research locations. Up-to-date timetables are available from [Stagecoach Orkney](https://www.stagecoachbus.com/plan-a-journey).
