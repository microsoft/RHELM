## Discussion Highlights

### 1. Finalising Educational Materials for the Dumfries Heritage Walk

Dr. McEwan and Fiona MacLeod began by thoroughly reviewing the latest drafts of the educational brochure and guide for the Heritage Walk. The priority was to ensure historical accuracy and authenticity. Dr. McEwan drew directly from primary sources, including the Dumfries Burgh Records (1812–1837) and the “Johnnie Armstrong Ballad” from the National Library of Scotland’s digital archive. These primary materials not only informed the selection of map waypoints, but also deepened the narrative elements featured in both the printed guide and digital resources.

There was a focused conversation about integrating further primary documents: the Maxwelltown Heritage Trail pamphlet and original town planning sketches from the 19th century, archived at Dumfries Museum. To enhance engagement, the team agreed to incorporate quotes from local oral histories—gathered during prior community sessions. This approach adds a personal dimension and meets public history project standards expected of university-led initiatives.

Fiona pointed out the importance of ensuring the materials align with the Scottish Curriculum for Excellence, referencing best practices laid out in “Heritage Learning in Practice” by Historic Environment Scotland. The aim is to create resources accessible to a broad audience—both secondary school pupils and adult learners—by employing annotated timelines that visually track key historical events, such as the 1745 Jacobite uprising and Robert Burns’ residency in Dumfries. The materials will be formatted for clarity, include ample visual content, and facilitate deeper exploration.

### 2. Enhancing Pedagogical Value and Technical Components of Interactive Maps

The conversation turned to the pedagogical opportunities of interactive mapping tools in heritage education. Drawing on the study “Using GIS in Heritage Education” (University of Edinburgh, 2021), both agreed that an interactive map could foster inquiry-based and active learning. The chosen platform, StoryMapJS, offers multimedia integration and mobile compatibility—vital for both onsite engagement and classroom use.

Key features discussed for the interactive map include:
- Embedded archival photographs and audio stories at each waypoint, providing historical context and personal perspectives.
- Geolocation to support real-time navigation and layered site exploration.
- Metadata tagging to enable differentiated learning—allowing users to filter waypoints by theme, period, or difficulty level.
- Technical requirements: Fiona is leading on mobile responsiveness, ensuring seamless use on both Android and iOS devices, and overseeing full compliance with WCAG 2.1 accessibility standards.

To support active learning and reflection, the map will offer digital quizzes and prompts at selected sites, drawing evidence from findings in “Active Learning in Heritage Trails” (British Educational Research Journal, 2020). Both agreed to pilot these features with history students in the initial rollout for feedback on usability and educational impact.

### 3. Pilot Session Scheduling and Implementation Plan

The pilot phase will be carried out in two stages:
- **Phase 1 (8–12 July):** University history undergraduates (15 participants) will complete the walk, providing initial feedback.
- **Phase 2 (22–26 July):** Secondary pupils (S3 cohort, Dumfries High School; 20 participants) will engage with the resources in partnership with faculty.

This structured approach maintains manageable group sizes and offers a balanced mix of academic and community perspectives. The timeline aligns with school term dates and leverages more predictable summer weather, as recommended in “Heritage Walks: Optimizing Engagement” (University of Glasgow Education Insights, 2023).

Data collection will be comprehensive:
- Pre- and post-session surveys to assess learning outcomes.
- Observational notes on user interaction with the interactive map.
- Anecdotal feedback from field facilitators and participants.

This evidence base will directly inform revisions to both the educational materials and technical tools before the public launch scheduled for autumn.

## Historical Context Note

The educational resources for the Dumfries Heritage Walk are grounded in a synthesis of 19th-century town development records, local oral histories, and detailed accounts of Burns’ life in Dumfries (1791–1796). The mapping and interpretative content for each waypoint are directly supported by primary materials such as the Dumfries Burgh Records, Maxwelltown Heritage Trail, and the “Johnnie Armstrong Ballad.” The project continues to draw on the guidelines outlined in “Heritage Learning in Practice” to uphold rigorous standards of historical accuracy and public engagement.
