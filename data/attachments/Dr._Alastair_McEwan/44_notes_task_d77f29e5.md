## Introduction

In preparation for my research trip to Brittany focused on its medieval history, I have developed a detailed and systematic strategy for daily fieldwork documentation. This approach aims to support both the immediate demands of research—ensuring that I accurately record and analyze all relevant data during each site visit—and the longer-term goal of producing a publishable record that will underpin future collaborative projects on Brittany's medieval heritage. The following plan integrates established scholarly standards with the protocols observed across French and Breton heritage institutions, emphasizing thoroughness, reliability, and reflexivity throughout the research process.

---

## Daily Field Notes: Structure and Rationale

To maintain historical precision and ensure preservation of primary data, I will structure each day’s field notes according to the following core elements:

### 1. Weather Summary

At the start of each day, I will record a concise weather overview using both current local meteorological reports and historical climatology data. This will help me contextualize my observations—particularly noting how elements like rain or fog impact accessibility, conservation status, or interpretive context at each site. Where pertinent, I’ll compare this day’s weather to documented medieval climate trends in Brittany, allowing me to reflect on environmental continuity or divergence.

### 2. Site Documentation in Historical Context

Every site visit will be documented with detailed descriptions of architectural features, cultural significance, and historical relevance within the wider fabric of medieval Brittany. I will trace connections between features observed—such as fortified walls, chapels, or civic buildings—and broader historical themes like ecclesiastical power, feudal governance, or regional trading links. Where possible, I will include references to relevant historical scholarship or primary sources to anchor interpretations.

### 3. Material Culture and Documentary Evidence

My notes will systematically record discoveries ranging from artifacts and masonry details to inscriptions and decorative motifs, as well as archival documents encountered during the trip. Alongside straightforward descriptions, I will provide analytical commentary on craftsmanship, stylistic trends, possible provenance, and preservation status, always referencing local archival records or authoritative historiographic sources for contextual support.

### 4. Photographic Highlights

For each day, I will select several key photographs that best capture noteworthy features, artifacts, or inscriptions. Each image will be annotated with file names and contextual notes explaining its significance for research, publication, or documentation. I’ll aim to provide enough detail in these captions to clarify why each photograph is relevant—a rare or ambiguous feature, evidence related to current scholarly debates, or instances of previously undocumented heritage.

### 5. Records of Professional Interactions

I will log all interactions with local experts, archivists, clergy, scholars, and other stakeholders. Alongside names and roles, I will record organizational affiliations and the context of each encounter—highlighting any valuable contributions, local knowledge, or insights shared. For example, if Laurent Dubois offers archival expertise on parish records, I’ll detail both the conversation and the implications for my research.

### 6. Discoveries, Hypotheses, and Discussion Points

Significant findings will be documented—whether it’s a newly identified inscription, an architectural anomaly, or a promising archival source. I’ll articulate hypotheses and record scholarly debates as they arise, anchoring my reasoning in the day’s observations and archival evidence. Gaps, uncertainties, and areas requiring further investigation will be noted to foster a dynamic and inquisitive approach to fieldwork.

### 7. Structured Table of Daily Activities

Each day will include a timetable capturing:

- Time of each activity
- Description and purpose
- Location
- Participants (with expertise and affiliation)
- Short notes on progress, challenges, and context

This table ensures transparency, facilitates review, and maintains a clear record of methodological rigor.

### 8. Data Management and Reflection

At day’s end, I will document all data backup actions—specifying devices, storage media, organizational methods, and file structures. I’ll also include reflective notes on methodological effectiveness, unexpected challenges, and emerging questions, prioritizing the use of primary sources and local archives to uphold research integrity.

## Best Practices for Medieval Fieldwork and Heritage Documentation

Drawing on recognized standards in medieval studies and heritage management in Brittany, the documentation strategy emphasizes the following practices:

- **Immediate, On-Site Recording:** I will capture observations live in the field using notebooks, audio dictation, and digital photography to ensure real-time documentation of perishable or context-contingent data.
- **Archives Consultation and Protocol Adherence:** Engagement with local and departmental archives (e.g., Archives départementales, DRAC Bretagne) will be conducted according to established protocols for document handling, digitization, and citation.
- **Collaborative Documentation:** Every interaction with local experts will be assessed for both data integrity and contribution to contextual understanding, with careful recording of oral histories or unpublished resources.
- **Robust Data Backup:** Each day’s data—including notes, photographs, and audio—will be backed up to several storage media (SSD, cloud repository, external hard drive), organized by date, site, and data type, and cross-indexed for retrieval.
- **Structured Reflection:** Daily reflective entries will critically assess data collection methods, responsiveness to field challenges, and the identification of new research directions or areas of uncertainty.

## Methodological Reflection and Directions for Further Research

Each evening, I will assess the effectiveness of my documentation, noting how well the day’s conditions supported research goals and whether all desired data were collected. I’ll identify logistical or interpretive challenges (such as restricted access to certain archival materials) and consider new questions emerging from the day’s discoveries. For example, the absence of documentation on some chapels may suggest a wider pattern of sociopolitical marginalization, while stylistic anomalies in iconography could prompt further archival investigation. These reflections will guide subsequent site visits and inform ongoing hypothesis development.
