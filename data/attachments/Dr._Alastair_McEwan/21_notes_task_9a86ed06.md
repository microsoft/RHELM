# Field Research Journal Entry: Dr. Alastair McEwan – Morning Walk Along the River Nith

---

## 1. Journal Header

**Date:** 7 May 2024  
**Time:** 07:20 – 09:10 BST  
**Location:** Eastern bank of the River Nith, Dumfries and Galloway, Scotland—stretching from the Old Stone Footbridge northward toward the Quay Path  
**Historical Context:**

The River Nith has been a central artery in Dumfries and Galloway for centuries, shaping the region’s patterns of settlement, trade, and agriculture since at least the medieval period. The Old Stone Footbridge—also known as the Nithsdale Footbridge—has stood since about 1777 and once served as a vital crossing for townsfolk and traders. The adjacent Quay Path remains something of a living artifact, retaining its medieval alignments and documented in 18th-century parish records as a market route connecting riverbank communities. Many features along this river, including the meadows and woodland banks, reflect layers of historical land use embedded in the landscape.

## 2. Weather and Environmental Observations

Stepping outside early this spring morning, I was greeted by a brisk 8°C, which gradually rose to a pleasant 14°C as I made my way along the riverbank. Overhead, thin clouds created a soft diffused light, punctuated by brief periods of sunshine typical of “May Gray.” A gentle southwesterly breeze pushed along the river corridor, barely stirring the canopy.

During the walk, I noted that the previous night’s minor rainfall had raised the river’s level slightly—consistent with spring patterns historically termed “fresh”—but the water was still within seasonal averages (Hydrological Records, 1886). Light silt was visible in the slow-moving stretches, yet the river’s clarity remained fair, permitting easy observation of submerged vegetation and early fish activity.

The eastern bank, sheltered by a fringe of riparian woodland and old meadows, felt damp underfoot, with humidity levels near 80%. Dense undergrowth beneath the canopy maintained a cool, shaded microclimate. This section of the river has traditionally felt protected; the layout of woodland and meadows here echoes descriptions recorded in weather diaries and local journals dating to 1808 and 1874.

## 4. Rare Discovery: Wood Anemone Stand

While tracing the curve of the eastern bank just north of the footbridge, I came across an unexpected cluster of **Anemone nemorosa**—wood anemone—gently nodding beneath the emerging canopy. I paused for several minutes, feeling an almost palpable sense of history in the presence of these delicate white flowers. This stand hadn’t appeared in any recent botanical maps of the river, and my own prior surveys had found only sparse, isolated patches further upstream.

Wood anemone serves as a reliable indicator of ancient woodland, persisting where ecological continuity has been maintained for centuries. Local records from 1884 document sporadic populations along the lower Nith, but post-1920 agricultural expansion and mid-20th-century riverbank restructuring fragmented these sites. Most wood anemone populations in the parish have vanished from accessible vantage points and now survive only in difficult-to-reach copses—a trend confirmed in Ordnance Survey supplements from 1905.

The isolated cluster I found could be the remnants of an older population, or perhaps it is expanding anew, responding to subtle changes in management or microclimate. For instance, alterations in mowing practices on the floodplain, increasing woodland succession after the 1978 flooding regime shift, or changes in riverbank use could be involved (Hydrological Records, Dumfries Library Collection). Given the plant’s sensitivity to disturbance and slow vegetative spread, the proximity to the historic pathway and bridge evokes studies of “woodland corridors,” suggesting a link between established rights-of-way and botanical continuity (CWAAS, 1949).

I plan a follow-up focusing on conservation and historical ecology: comparing these findings with archival botanical lists, management minutes, and remaining woodland stands to determine continuity and change in local woodland flora.

## 5. Personal Reflections

As I walked familiar ground—mapping the riverbank and crossing sun-dappled meadows—I found myself recalling surveys from spring 2022, 2016, and much earlier. Discovering the wood anemone here for the first time filled me with a kind of quiet excitement: I lingered to sketch and photograph the patch, mindful of how the riverbank’s composition, though inevitably shaped by decades of encroaching agriculture and periodic floodplain restoration, remains remarkably resilient.

Not far from the anemone patch, I spotted lesser celandine and bluebells thriving in the same woodland hollow described by John McGill in his diary from 1888. The echo between my observations and those of past naturalists is a powerful reminder of this landscape’s endurance. The banks remain dotted by prominent alder stands, which local river traders in the late Victorian period considered landmarks during spring freshets—stories that still circulate among older residents.

Along the Quay Path, crack willow now crowds areas formerly occupied by open meadows, hinting at subtle but persistent ecological transitions—responses, perhaps, to modern flood control, land development, and sustained stewardship efforts by local community groups. Each species encountered tells both a botanical and human story, woven together by the river’s continuous passage.

The emergence of wood anemone at this historic crossroads feels significant—not just as a rarity, but as a marker of the ecological threads binding past and present. Walking away, I felt a renewed sense of connection to both the landscape and the long chain of observers who have walked before me.

## 6. Research Questions and Archival Directions

In light of today’s discoveries and continuing changes along the River Nith, I intend to pursue several lines of inquiry in upcoming fieldwork and archival research:

**Historical Ecology:**
- How has the distribution of wood anemone along the River Nith changed with shifts in 20th-century land management? Estate maps and old botanical surveys may offer evidence of woodland continuity or fragmentation.
- Are there overlooked or unpublished field notebooks in the Dumfries Museum archives noting wood anemone stands prior to industrial riverbank modifications?

**Archival Research:**
- Can parish meeting minutes from Dumfries (1800–1950) shed light on documented changes in riverbank management or woodland policies that influenced floristic diversity?
- Reviewing the Cumbria and Westmorland Antiquarian and Archaeological Society field journals may yield useful methodologies for integrating historical land-use records with botanical change, and for comparative study across similar regional river systems.
- What insights might adjacent county records—particularly from Kirkcudbright—provide for understanding broader regional flora dynamics and cross-county conservation strategies?

**Methods Recommendations:**
- I plan to cross-reference historical tithe and estate maps with current GPS-tagged botanical observations to clarify changes in species distribution.
- I will reach out to the CWAAS archive custodians to discuss access to digitized field research reports for comparative analysis.
- Repeat seasonal surveys and systematic photographic monitoring of rare plant stands should be established as part of the institutional research archive and ongoing flora conservation efforts.
