## Executive Summary

This roundtable brought together a cross-section of policymakers, industry, civil society, and academic voices to discuss the shifting landscape of EU technology governance, with a specific focus on advancing and defending the principles of an open Internet amidst evolving legislation. The discussion emphasized the intersection of new digital rulebooks—primarily the Digital Services Act (DSA), Digital Markets Act (DMA), and the impending AI Act—with ongoing normative commitments to fundamental rights and cross-border interoperability.

**Key themes addressed included:**
- **Digital Sovereignty vs. Open Internet:** Tension between the EU’s drive for digital sovereignty and its historical commitment to an open, interoperable Internet.
- **Intermediary Liability and Systemic Risk:** Persistent ambiguity around the operationalization of “systemic risk” in the DSA and concerns about practical enforcement.
- **Interoperability and Regulatory Layering:** Complications arising from overlapping requirements, particularly for very large online platforms (VLOPs) and gatekeepers.
- **Transparency and Proportionality:** The need to balance enhanced transparency provisions with the resources available to smaller platforms, so as not to stifle competition or innovation.
- **Implementation Gaps and Divergences:** The growing risk of member state divergence, especially regarding new obligations on content moderation, data sharing, and approaches to dispute resolution.
- **Evidence from Research and Practice:** Reference was made throughout to academic and policy research identifying unintended consequences, such as possible chilling effects and burdens felt disproportionately by smaller market actors.
- **Commitment to Collaborative Action:** Agreement on targeted next steps, including the clarification of DSA Article 39 (out-of-court dispute settlement mechanisms), and the establishment of a cross-sectoral working group on interoperability standards for open Internet architecture.

## Analytical Commentary

### Legislative Context: DSA, DMA, and AI Act

The European Union's digital policy is shaped by a complex and interconnected array of new legislation. The adoption of the DSA and DMA, alongside negotiations on the AI Act, establishes a comprehensive regulatory framework targeting the accountability of online platforms, digital competition, and the relationship between human rights and data-driven innovation. These regulations collectively strive to set global benchmarks, but also pose significant implementation and harmonization challenges.

Critical evidence and analysis from organisations such as EDRi, as well as legal scholarship (e.g., Keller, 2023), draw attention to the importance of calibrating new obligations in a way that upholds fundamental freedoms without overburdening intermediaries or fragmenting the single digital market.

### Balancing Digital Sovereignty and Openness

There was extensive discussion on how the EU’s aspiration for digital sovereignty interacts with its commitment to open internet principles. While greater European control over digital infrastructure and regulation may be justified on grounds of security and autonomy, this trajectory, if pursued in isolation, risks producing a fragmented digital regulatory environment. Insights from De Filippi and colleagues underline the delicate balance between safeguarding European values and preventing ‘regulatory balkanization’ that undermines global interoperability and openness.

### Operationalizing Systemic Risk and Platform Accountability

Participants highlighted persistent uncertainties around the definition and practical implementation of “systemic risk” measures contemplated by the DSA, particularly for VLOPs and gatekeeper platforms under the DMA. Stakeholders stressed the need for precise technical and procedural guidance to ensure a consistent approach across member states, referencing work by the Center for Democracy & Technology. The group stressed that the absence of such clarity increases both business risk and the likelihood of regulatory arbitrage.

### Proportionality and Compliance for Smaller Platforms

Another core concern related to the unintended consequences of well-intentioned transparency and accountability mandates. Evidence presented, including from Becker and Buhmann (2023), indicates that while transparency is critical, its costly implementation tends to reinforce the dominance of large incumbents, making market entry more difficult for new and smaller actors. The roundtable called for a proportional, risk-based approach to compliance that would protect fundamental rights without unduly burdening startups or niche platforms.

### Cross-Border Consistency and National Divergence

Implementation gaps and divergent national approaches are emerging, particularly on issues like online content moderation and out-of-court dispute settlement under Article 39 DSA. Mapping by the EU Agency for Fundamental Rights (FRA) has already revealed interpretive variances in national transpositions that may impact user rights and legal certainty. This was widely viewed as a risk to the intended harmonization of the EU’s digital single market, necessitating greater Commission leadership and timely clarification.

### Stakeholder Positions

- **Broad Agreement:** There is strong consensus on the value of the open internet and the importance of defending this principle as a core EU value—though opinions vary on how best to achieve it within the new regulatory landscape.
- **Differing Priorities:** Industry representatives emphasized pragmatic, staged timelines and the risks of overregulation, while civil society and academic voices advocated for more robust commitments to data portability and non-discrimination, cautioning against regulatory capture by dominant platforms.
- **Technical Standardization:** All parties recognized the urgent need for technical standardization on interoperability, yet acknowledged the challenges involved in balancing speed, practicality, and stakeholder input.

### Longer-Term Implications

The outcomes of this roundtable have significant bearing on several major European digital dossiers under active development, including the European Interoperability Framework and revisions to eIDAS. The phenomenon of legislative layering—where obligations arising from DSA, DMA, AI Act, GDPR, and the Data Act intersect—underscores the importance of comprehensive, anticipatory governance and policy coherence to avoid regulatory gaps or overlaps.

## Jonas’s Reflections and Recommendations

### Reflections

The discussions revealed how rapidly regulatory complexity is escalating. The lack of uniform definitions—especially around "systemic risk"—could create uneven enforcement across Europe. Without detailed Commission guidance and close coordination, national divergent interpretations threaten legal certainty and undermine the harmonization project at the heart of the EU’s digital strategy. 

I am increasingly concerned that the greater regulatory burden, unless properly calibrated, may inadvertently entrench the market power of tech incumbents, narrowing space for innovation and the emergence of smaller platforms. This risk is no longer theoretical; it’s manifesting in live compliance decisions and the operational realities faced by both emerging and established platforms.

### Action Priorities

**Immediate Measures:**
- Work with peers and relevant networks to ensure that the stakeholder working group on interoperability and systemic risk includes diverse technical, consumer, and civil society perspectives.
- Request access to early drafts of Commission guidance on systemic risk to provide timely, constructive rights-based input during the formative stages.

**Short-Term (by August–September):**
- Initiate a collaborative research agenda with academic partners, targeting the empirical assessment of transparency-related compliance costs. Results from such studies would provide essential data in support of proportionality arguments during ongoing legislative consultations.
- Closely monitor Member State developments on DSA Article 39 dispute settlement, collaborating with national regulators and utilizing FOI mechanisms to gather practical insights on early legal challenges.

**Medium-Term (by Q4 2024):**
- Undertake a comparative mapping exercise to identify and address overlaps and potential conflicts across major regulatory instruments (DSA, DMA, AI Act, eIDAS, Data Act). This will involve desk research and targeted interviews with institutional contacts.

### Areas for Ongoing Attention

Several issues require continued, focused scrutiny:
- The scope and operational meaning of “systemic risk,” and the potential for expansive or restrictive interpretations by regulators.
- The future trajectory of digital sovereignty, particularly whether the pursuit of national or European regulatory autonomy is compatible with preserving an open, global internet.
- Risks associated with divergent implementation at national level, including the possibility of forum-shopping by intermediaries to exploit uneven regulatory environments.
