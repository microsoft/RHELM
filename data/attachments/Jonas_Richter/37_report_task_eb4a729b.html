National Digital Rights Conference 2024

Conference Session Summary Report

Kongresshalle am Zoo, Leipzig – September 27, 2024

Executive Summary

The 2024 National Digital Rights Conference gathered leading figures from government, technology, education, and civil society to tackle the rapidly shifting challenges of digital rights governance across Europe. Key moments included Jonas Richter’s compelling keynote on defending open internet policy, a dedicated youth panel on digital literacy, and the formation of new partnerships aimed at expanding privacy education.

Central policy takeaways from the conference included:

  • Prioritizing Open Internet Safeguards: Participants underscored the pressing need to protect open internet principles as pressures rise for internet fragmentation and increased state-level filtering.
  • Emphasizing Youth Digital Literacy: There was a strong consensus that equipping young people with digital literacy is fundamental to building resilience against misinformation and fostering digital inclusion.
  • Aligning with Evolving EU Legislation: Attendees stressed the strategic importance of aligning national practices with the Digital Services Act (DSA) and Digital Markets Act (DMA), ensuring both harmonized governance and meaningful protection of rights.
  • Expanding Privacy Education Networks: The conference advanced actionable proposals for scaling privacy education, using multi-stakeholder models and cross-border partnerships.

In addition to shaping ongoing policy debates, the event provided real-world lessons in risk mitigation—both technical and organizational—and laid the groundwork for collaborations that are expected to influence national and European digital rights priorities in the year ahead.

1. Keynote: Defending the Open Internet (Jonas Richter)

Jonas Richter opened the conference with a thorough analysis of why maintaining an open internet is foundational for digital rights and democratic participation. He drew attention to new regulatory frameworks, such as the Digital Markets Act, analyzing how they can both reinforce openness and paradoxically pose risks of regulatory overreach.

Key Policy Highlights

  • Foundational Role of Open Internet: Richter described unrestricted internet access as essential for participation in public life, innovation, and economic development.
  • Illustrative Evidence: He referenced the “Network Neutrality in Practice” report from the Czech Republic, demonstrating positive outcomes where strong regulation existed. Richter also discussed recent examples of state-imposed internet throttling outside the EU, warning that such practices threaten fundamental rights. The rising prevalence of discriminatory data handling by dominant online platforms reinforced the need for harmonized policy responses.
  • Policy Recommendations:
    • Swift, robust transposition of the EU DSA into national legislation with meaningful civil society oversight.
    • Legal requirements for ISPs and platforms to publish regular, transparent risk reports.
    • Development of cross-border mechanisms for real-time risk and traffic management monitoring, fostering collaboration across EU states.

Discussion and Audience Engagement

Dr. Petra Schulze inquired about protecting against overblocking and the risk of censorship as national governments implement the DSA. Richter recommended that legislative processes include independent audits and procedural safeguards, supported by regular multi-stakeholder reviews.

Students from Leipzig University asked about the relevance of open internet principles to future technologies like AI-driven content filtering and 5G data slicing. Richter responded that openness must be ‘future-proofed’ in the legislative language, ensuring that evolving technologies are explicitly addressed and subject to protection standards.

Feedback and Impact

Post-session feedback reflected appreciation for Richter’s direct, practical recommendations, especially his blend of regulatory insights and real-world examples. Attendees valued his focus on civil society oversight and technological evolution. There were some concerns about the resource implications of DSA oversight for smaller ISPs, suggesting this is an area requiring further policy development.

Survey results:
- 92% of respondents found the keynote “directly relevant” to their work.
- 81% agreed that the proposed measures would help strengthen national digital rights frameworks.

Implications for European Governance

Richter expressed strong support for Germany to take on a leadership role in EU-wide digital risk monitoring and policy harmonization. Aligning national implementations with European Data Protection Board guidance was highlighted as crucial to prevent regulatory fragmentation and maintain a unified European digital services market.

2. Youth Digital Literacy Panel: Summary and Outcomes

The youth panel showcased a multi-dimensional dialogue on equipping the next generation with the skills needed to navigate and shape the digital world.

Panel Contributions

  • Jonas Richter: Connected open internet advocacy to youth empowerment, pointing out that young people’s informed engagement is essential to a resilient and participatory digital future.
  • Anja: Presented evidence from pilot digital literacy workshops demonstrating increased student engagement, improved understanding of basic privacy, and greater confidence in recognizing misinformation.
  • Leila: Provided the lived perspective from youth, focusing especially on challenges experienced by marginalized groups, including migrants and refugees. She emphasized the necessity for tailored resources to prevent digital exclusion and ensure equitable access.

Key Discussion Themes

  • Curriculum Integration: The panel agreed that digital literacy should be systematically incorporated across formal and informal educational settings.
  • Equity and Inclusion: Strategies to overcome disparities, such as lack of device access or gaps in support for underserved communities, were prioritized.
  • Youth Agency: The importance of engaging young people in co-designing and leading digital literacy programs was underlined, acknowledging their unique perspectives and energy.
  • Measuring Impact: Calls for robust, longitudinal data to assess actual outcomes—including knowledge retention, behavioral change, and improved misinformation resilience—were recurrent.

Pilot Workshop Results

Between April and June 2024, 520 students from six schools participated in pilot workshops. Post-program surveys showed a 27% jump in self-reported ability to identify misinformation online. Teachers also observed more engaged discussions about digital privacy and online consent.

Advocacy and Recognition

The panel’s op-ed, published in the September 2024 issue of Open Internet Journal, argues forcefully for universal digital literacy access as a democratic right and calls for coordinated action and EU-level funding to drive the agenda forward.

Recommendations for Action

  • Broaden successful pilots to reach more under-resourced schools, using targeted funding.
  • Create a standing youth digital rights advisory council for continuous content and outreach development.
  • Embed digital literacy indicators into education assessment systems nationwide.
  • Establish international teacher-training partnerships using Erasmus+ and similar EU mechanisms.
  • Systematically integrate critical thinking and privacy-by-design concepts into all learning modules.

3. Technical Infrastructure: Incident and Resolution

Smooth technical operations are essential for any event’s success. During the opening session, a presentation was briefly interrupted by a faulty HDMI cable that disrupted video output. The technical support team identified the issue through quick diagnostics, replaced the cable using dedicated repair toolkits, and restored full system function within 11 minutes. The session resumed with minimal disruption.

To reinforce resilience for future events, the following best practices were agreed:

  • Conduct thorough pre-event testing of all critical AV and IT components.
  • Maintain accessible supplies of backup cables, connectors, and diagnostic tools.
  • Empower in-room moderators with practical troubleshooting checklists.
  • Record and review incident reports post-event, in line with established IT service management standards (e.g., ITIL).

4. Networking Outcomes and Policy Progress

The conference facilitated high-impact strategic meetings, translating networking into concrete collaborative initiatives.

  • Collaboration on Privacy Education: Marta Klein (Data4All Foundation) and conference organizers decided to co-develop an open-access “Privacy for All” education toolkit. This project will target teachers and NGO facilitators, offering modular resources and a structured webinar series.
  • EU Policy Alignment: Dr. Katarzyna Nowak (EU Cyber Policy Hub) initiated work to align privacy education programs with GDPR and ePrivacy Directives. Together with partners, they plan to develop research to map national curricular differences and exchange best practices for privacy education.

These collaborations establish participants as leaders in national privacy education, and ensure that pilot projects are strategically positioned for upcoming federal and EU funding rounds. Effective partnership building is expected to drive unified privacy standards for youth and educators, helping avoid inconsistencies between policies across the EU.
