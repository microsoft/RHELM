1. Introduction

The internet in Europe currently stands at a crossroads. Never before has the interplay of technology, policy, and fundamental rights been as dynamic—or as contested—as it is today. In this keynote, I want to provide a critical assessment of recent developments shaping internet freedom across Europe, guided by the core principles of openness, privacy, freedom of expression, and democratic accountability.

As rapid legal reforms and the acceleration of digital innovations reshape our societies, these principles are repeatedly tested, sometimes even threatened, by both external pressures—such as shifting geopolitical realities—and internal debates about the very definition of sovereignty in the digital world. My goal is to illuminate the most significant legislative trends of the past year, unpack the European approach to data control, examine the greatest risks to an open digital environment, and reflect on the evolving standards for algorithmic transparency.

To ensure a holistic understanding, I will present comparative case studies and offer pragmatic policy recommendations aimed at safeguarding a truly open internet for all Europeans.

2. The European Digital Policy Landscape: Current Context

Europe has long aspired to set global standards for responsible digital governance, placing fundamental rights, privacy, and user protection at the center of its digital policy debates. The landscape today, however, is deeply characterized by a struggle to strike the right balance between digital sovereignty on the one hand, and the preservation of an open, cross-border internet on the other.

Several major forces shape this environment. Governments are increasingly alert to the risks of foreign tech dominance and digital authoritarianism, while at the same time grappling with the fast-evolving nature of AI, the ever-present threat of cyberattacks, and public anxiety around mass surveillance. At the heart of policymaking, the European Commission, Parliament, and national data protection authorities collaborate and sometimes clash with civil society alliances such as EDRi (European Digital Rights), as well as industry stakeholders, over the future shape of internet regulation.

Within this dynamic, the interplay between national policy preferences and supranational EU initiatives is crucial. While the EU anchors its approach in regulatory ambition and fundamental rights, member states often pursue diverging strategies, whether in the drive for technological self-sufficiency, the pursuit of public security, or the implementation of innovation policies.

3. Recent European Legislative Developments Influencing Internet Freedom

Digital Services Act (DSA) & Digital Markets Act (DMA)

The DSA and DMA, which came into force in 2023, mark a significant milestone in the EU’s attempt to forge a “European way” in digital governance. The DSA introduces a comprehensive set of obligations for online platforms, focusing on responsible content moderation, mandatory transparency for algorithmic processes, and new consumer protections. This regulation aims to harmonize internet governance across the EU and to strengthen fundamental rights—even as it challenges very large platforms to revolutionize their operational models.

In parallel, the DMA squarely targets the market power of dominant (“gatekeeper”) technology firms. By imposing firm restrictions on self-preferencing and abusive conduct, it seeks to foster genuine competition and curb the influence of a small number of US-based tech giants over the European digital economy. Both laws are designed to reinforce accountability, level the playing field, and safeguard democratic values online.

AI Act (Provisional Agreement December 2023)

The AI Act represents the EU’s boldest effort yet to harness the benefits of artificial intelligence while mitigating its risks. Building on a risk-based framework, the Act introduces graduated obligations: high-risk AI systems are subject to mandatory transparency, documentation, and human oversight, while some practices—such as untargeted biometric surveillance—are largely prohibited. By demanding meaningful explanations and viable redress mechanisms for automated decisions, the AI Act addresses longstanding concerns about algorithmic opacity and the erosion of individual autonomy.

NIS2 Directive (Effective October 2024)

With the adoption of the NIS2 Directive, Europe is modernizing its cybersecurity posture. The Directive broadens the scope of regulated entities across key sectors, including cloud service providers and data centers, and elevates standards for risk management and incident reporting. In doing so, it aims to bolster the resilience and security of Europe’s internet infrastructure, a vital prerequisite for ensuring reliable access and trust in the online environment.

Evidence and Key Arguments

Reports from the European Union Agency for Fundamental Rights (FRA) underscore both the progress and current gaps in platform transparency across member states. While civil society organizations have welcomed the introduction of stronger user rights, there are growing concerns about over-removal of content under the DSA’s notice-and-action regimes. These frameworks, while essential for combating illegal or harmful material, risk producing unintended “chilling effects” on freedom of expression if not carefully implemented and monitored.

4. Data Sovereignty: European Approaches and Implications

Europe’s commitment to data sovereignty is rooted in the belief that individuals and organizations should retain meaningful control over their data, guided by EU norms and standards. Central to this ambition is a series of interlocking legal frameworks, led by the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), which continues to set the global benchmark for personal data protection.

Recent years have seen the introduction of additional instruments aimed at reinforcing data governance: the Data Governance Act (enacted in 2023) promotes trustworthy data sharing by creating rules for data intermediaries and stewarding voluntary data spaces, while the forthcoming Data Act seeks to clarify ownership and access rights in the burgeoning landscape of data generated within the EU, especially by connected devices and industrial processes.

Across Europe, national governments increasingly advocate for “cloud sovereignty,” exemplified by initiatives such as Germany’s GAIA-X and France’s “cloud de confiance.” These projects are designed to minimize dependence on non-EU infrastructure providers and mitigate the risks of extraterritorial data access—particularly in the wake of legal uncertainty following the Court of Justice of the EU’s Schrems II decision, which struck down the EU–US Privacy Shield.

Arguments and Evidence

Empirical studies confirm that both public sector organizations and private businesses are accelerating the adoption of EU-based data solutions, motivated by compliance with GDPR and concerns about foreign surveillance. However, this shift has prompted debate about the risks of digital “Balkanization.” Overly restrictive localization measures threaten to fragment the global internet, potentially stifling innovation and undermining cross-border collaboration. The European Data Protection Board (EDPB) continues to monitor state attempts to weaken encryption and warns against any moves that would grant authorities blanket access to personal data.

5. Challenges and Threats Posed by State and Corporate Actors

Internet freedom in Europe is tested by a complex array of threats from both state and corporate actors.

State-Driven Risks:
Governments increasingly invoke public security and anti-terrorism imperatives to justify expanded surveillance powers. Legislative efforts aimed at scanning private communications for child sexual abuse material (CSAM)—such as the EU “chat control” proposal—have caused alarm among privacy advocates, who argue that such measures risk undermining end-to-end encryption and infringing on freedom of expression. High-profile incidents like Hungary’s deployment of NSO Group’s Pegasus spyware and Poland’s use of surveillance tools raise urgent questions about unchecked state power and the absence of robust oversight. Meanwhile, debates in countries like France and Germany around “lawful access” to encrypted data highlight persistent tensions between security prerogatives and fundamental rights.

Corporate-Driven Risks:
Despite new regulatory obligations, dominant internet platforms—including Alphabet, Meta, and Amazon—retain outsized influence over the flow of information and public discourse in Europe. Algorithmic opacity, pervasive targeted advertising, and data mining practices open the door to discrimination, disinformation, and behavioral manipulation. Civil society reports reveal that self-regulatory efforts by these platforms remain insufficient, and the inconsistent implementation of new EU rules on transparency and compliance further compounds the problem.

Empirical Evidence:
Recent findings from the FRA and EDRi document a sharp increase in content takedown requests, initiated both by governments and private actors. However, transparency reporting is inconsistent, and cases of platform lobbying influencing how regulations are applied at the national level are growing concerns.

6. Algorithmic Transparency in Europe: Standards, Initiatives & Regulatory Trends

As complex, data-driven algorithms increasingly mediate online experiences, the demand for clear standards around algorithmic transparency and accountability has never been greater.

Regulatory Initiatives

The AI Act will introduce some of the strictest requirements globally for transparency in high-risk AI systems. These include explainability obligations for automated decision-making processes that significantly affect individuals, detailed documentation and record-keeping, mandatory human oversight, and the establishment of public transparency registers. Parallel obligations under the DSA require very large online platforms (VLOPs) to provide risk assessments, open up their algorithms to independent audits, and facilitate algorithmic explainability not just for regulators, but, to an extent, for users themselves.

Technical Standards

Ensuring meaningful algorithmic accountability also depends on technical standards. European and international standardization bodies, such as CEN-CENELEC and ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42, are actively developing shared frameworks for algorithmic auditing, fairness metrics, and impact assessments. These technical tools are vital to the practical enforcement of new regulatory obligations and to establishing trust in automated decision-making processes.

Key Debates and Empirical Trends

Early pilots of algorithmic audits—in contexts ranging from content ranking on social media to credit scoring and biometric surveillance systems—have demonstrated that while technical transparency is crucial for accountability, it frequently falls short of delivering genuine user empowerment. Civil liberties organizations caution that overly technical or incomplete disclosures do not necessarily help ordinary users understand—or challenge—algorithmic impacts on their lives. Continued collaboration between policymakers, technologists, and advocates is essential to calibrate these mechanisms for both effectiveness and accessibility.

8. Actionable Policy and Advocacy Recommendations

As the digital environment grows more complex, I propose the following actions to reinforce internet freedom in Europe:

1. Strengthen Algorithmic Transparency and Accountability:
Mandate regular, independent audits of high-risk AI and recommendation systems, and require that transparency disclosures are accessible to all users, not just experts. Invest in algorithmic literacy initiatives within education and civil society to raise awareness and foster critical engagement with automated systems.

2. Safeguard End-to-End Encryption:
Vigorously oppose legislation mandating general scanning or government “backdoors” in private communications. Uphold encryption as a non-negotiable element of personal security and freedom of expression in the digital age.

3. Enhance Enforcement of the DSA and DMA:
Build the capacity and independence of regulatory authorities at the EU and national levels. Promote effective cross-border cooperation, and protect whistleblowers to close enforcement loopholes or discrepancies in implementation.

4. Advance Data Sovereignty While Preserving Internet Openness:
Encourage federated and interoperable data frameworks to enable innovation and cross-border data flow without undermining GDPR principles. Ensure that moves towards data localization do not splinter the internet or hamper collaboration.

5. Insulate Policy-Making Against Undue Influence:
Enforce stringent transparency requirements for lobbying activities and regulatory drafting. Broaden public and civil society participation in digital policy debates to prevent the capture of policymaking by vested interests.

6. Institutionalize Ongoing Policy Review and Redress:
Establish regular review cycles for digital regulation, involving periodic impact assessments, public consultation, and the adaptation of policy frameworks in light of evolving technological and social realities.

These recommendations are grounded in the belief that safeguarding internet freedom is not a one-time legislative achievement, but an ongoing societal commitment requiring vigilance, participation, and adaptation.
