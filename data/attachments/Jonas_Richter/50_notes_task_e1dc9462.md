## 1. Discussion Overview

On November 14, 2024, I met with Leila, head of strategic outreach at a civil society NGO focused on open internet advocacy, and Marta, an EU policy advisor, for a working lunch at Café Einstein in Berlin. ## 2. Outreach Strategy Proposals

To ensure clarity, we tagged our proposed strategies using the following annotations:  
- **[BP]**: Best Practice in open internet advocacy  
- **[PD]**: Aligns with ongoing technology policy debate  
- **[SG: X]**: Identifies specific stakeholder group (NGO, Policymaker, Industry, Public)  
- **[EU]**: Direct connection to EU-level advocacy or regulation  
- **[TS]**: Time-sensitive / urgent actions

### 2.1 Consolidated Proposals and Annotations

1. **Coordinated Policy Briefings for Policymakers** [BP][PD][SG: Policymakers][EU]  
   Prepare concise, accessible policy memos outlining the risks and benefits of an open internet, tailored for Members of the European Parliament (MEPs) and national digital affairs ministries. These briefs will focus particularly on Digital Markets Act (DMA) and Digital Services Act (DSA) enforcement, cross-border data transfers, and evolving connectivity regulations. We agreed on the importance of providing regular, up-to-date analysis in advance of upcoming EP committee hearings.

2. **Multi-Stakeholder Roundtables** [BP][SG: Policymakers, Industry, NGOs][EU]  
   Organize inclusive roundtable forums before significant policy events, such as new AI Act proposals or Digital Decade updates. Inviting internet service providers, digital rights groups, industry representatives, and civil society helps foster transparency, mitigates the risk of regulatory capture, and strengthens coalition-building.

3. **Thematic Public Webinars and Workshops** [BP][SG: Public, NGOs][PD]  
   Host educational webinars and workshops centered on digital rights, digital sovereignty, and how EU internet policy shapes daily life. Programming will target university students, journalists, and civil servants, especially those working outside Brussels, to broaden awareness and engagement.

4. **Policy Tracker and Open Consultation Hub** [BP][SG: NGOs, Public][EU][TS]  
   Develop an online dashboard to centralize updates on internet policy reforms, public consultations, and advocacy deadlines across the EU. This resource will lower barriers for grassroots stakeholders—particularly in underrepresented EU member states—to follow and engage in policy developments. Marta highlighted the urgency of responding to open consultations on cross-border data flows, with a key deadline of December 10.

5. **Targeted Media Engagement Strategy** [BP][SG: Public, Policymakers][PD][TS]  
   Coordinate media outreach, including op-eds, press releases, and toolkits, to counter misinformation related to the DSA. Given imminent public controversies around issues like platform takedown orders and content moderation, we plan to prioritize swift and accurate messaging.

6. **Stakeholder Mapping and Relationship Database** [BP][SG: NGOs, Policymakers, Industry][EU]  
   Systematically map out key stakeholders—MEPs, regulatory officials, industry representatives, and allied NGOs. Track advocacy positions and past collaborative actions to lay the groundwork for new coalitions and speed up coordination in response to emerging policy windows.

7. **Rapid Response Collaboration Model** [BP][PD][TS][SG: NGOs, Policymakers]  
   Establish a template for drafting and endorsing “open letters” that can be activated quickly (within 48 hours) in reaction to sudden regulatory threats or opportunities—such as developments during legislative trilogues. The model will include modular endorsement sections to streamline broad support.

8. **Social Listening and Narrative Analysis** [PD][SG: Public, NGOs]  
   Implement social listening tools to monitor public sentiment and narratives in internet policy debates, particularly around DMA implementation and AI risk framing. These insights will inform our public communication and help us shape effective, timely advocacy responses.

9. **Locality-Specific Advocacy Campaigns** [BP][SG: Public, NGOs][EU]  
   Launch regional advocacy campaigns customized to member state contexts—for instance, addressing rural connectivity challenges in Eastern Europe or advocating for platform transparency in France and Belgium. Partner with national digital rights organizations to ensure relevance and outreach.

10. **Engagement in EU Regulatory Sandboxes** [BP][PD][SG: Industry, Policymakers][EU]  
    Actively participate in EU-led digital innovation “sandboxes” to present open internet use cases and directly identify regulatory bottlenecks with both regulators and industry innovators.

## 4. Analytical Remarks and Follow-Up

### 4.1 Key Observations

- **Unified Messaging:** It became immediately clear that consistent messaging across civil society groups is crucial. Divergent narratives risk diluting our collective impact, especially before major legislative phases like the ongoing DSA and DMA implementation. We need a common advocacy language—one that resonates both in Brussels and with wider EU publics.

- **Policy Windows:** With several consultations and legislative initiatives on topics such as cross-border data flows now open, we must prioritize and coordinate engagement. The urgency is underscored by looming consultation deadlines and the evolving regulatory framework.

- **Bridging Geographical Gaps:** Both Marta and Leila emphasized the persistent underrepresentation of NGOs and civil society from Central and Eastern Europe in EU internet governance. To address this, we agreed to develop more tailored engagement strategies—outreach that respects both linguistic and local policy realities.

- **Coalition-Building with Integrity:** In building long-term, multi-stakeholder coalitions, we must guard against “whitewashing” by industry interests and ensure that civil society has the resources and space for meaningful input.

### 4.2 Critical Follow-Up Questions

- How will we define and measure the success of our outreach campaigns? Specifically, do we focus on qualitative shifts, concrete policy outcomes, or changes in public awareness?
- Which groups or communities are still missing from our engagement and how can we best reach them?
- What internal coordination mechanisms are needed to keep Brussels-level and member state advocacy teams aligned and efficient?
- As digital policy moves rapidly—particularly on files like the AI Act and the Data Governance Act (DGA)—how do we ensure ongoing monitoring and proactive outreach?

### 4.3 Policy Implications

- **Responsiveness and Opportunity:** The current policy landscape brings significant risk of overregulation and threats to online freedoms, alongside real opportunities to embed digital rights more deeply in EU law. Civil society’s ability to react quickly and thoughtfully will shape the outcome.
- **Evolving Advocacy Practices:** Best practice now demands rapid, transparent, and genuinely inclusive collaboration—leveraging regulatory sandboxes, real-time policy mapping, and continuous feedback loops.
- **Inclusivity as a Core Standard:** Our advocacy must consciously address linguistic, local, and digital divides. Only by making campaigns accessible and relevant across all EU member states can we credibly represent public interest under the EU framework.

### 4.4 Next Steps

- Prepare and circulate an advocacy calendar synchronized with the EU and member state legislative and consultation cycles, ensuring timely action.
- Build a shared repository for campaign materials, stakeholder contact data, and rapid response templates to streamline communication and resource sharing across teams.
- Designate lead contacts for ongoing monitoring of high-impact regulatory initiatives and assign responsibility for gathering and distributing key updates.
- Schedule quarterly coordination calls with all involved partners to review strategies, share learnings, and adapt tactics as needed.
