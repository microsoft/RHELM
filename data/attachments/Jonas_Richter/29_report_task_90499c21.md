## Introduction

This report synthesizes recent developments and thinking around the concept of digital sovereignty in the European Union, drawing on academic and policy literature published from late 2023 through mid-2024. Through a critical assessment of major frameworks, legislative milestones, and current debates, the analysis explores how the drive for digital sovereignty interacts with foundational values such as privacy, interoperability, and the openness of the Internet. These insights are intended for technology policy analysts, privacy professionals, and stakeholders participating in privacy-focused workshops, providing clear recommendations and practical guidance for navigating an increasingly complex regulatory environment.

## Analytical Commentary and Key Themes

### The Central Role of Data Governance in EU Digital Sovereignty

The central theme across recent literature is the EU’s ambition to assert leadership in digital rule-making and reduce strategic dependencies on non-EU actors. The European Commission’s framing of digital sovereignty emphasizes independent decision-making in digital policy, particularly regarding data governance, infrastructure security, and the competitiveness of the EU technology sector. This vision faces practical constraints, given the deeply interconnected nature of the global digital marketplace and persistent reliance on extraterritorial service providers. Even as the EU advocates for autonomy, seamless independence is elusive due to entrenched external dependencies and the technical realities of a globally networked Internet.

### Navigating the Tension Between Data Localization and Open Internet Values

One of the most persistent debates centers on the trade-off between data localization—prioritizing the storage and processing of data within domestic jurisdictions—and the foundational principles of an open and interoperable Internet. The drive to localize sensitive data and enhance cybersecurity is weighed against the risk of erecting new digital borders, which can undermine interoperability, slow innovation, and fragment the Internet ecosystem. European regulatory approaches strive for a measured middle ground: GDPR, DSA, DMA, and the Data Act all encourage cross-border data flows where possible, while implementing strategic restrictions when justified by public interest, national security, or citizens’ rights.

### The Evolution of Privacy Governance Through the Lens of the GDPR

The GDPR remains a benchmark for global data privacy regulation, marking its fifth year with both celebrated achievements and continuing obstacles. The framework has raised the bar for data protection worldwide, yet has also introduced rigidity that, at times, stifles innovation and complicates data sharing between the EU and external partners. Ongoing policy adaptations aim to streamline cross-border data transfers (notably via updated Standard Contractual Clauses and the EU-U.S. Data Privacy Framework) while ensuring robust protection of personal data. Disparities in enforcement between member states and the need for practical interoperability solutions remain central challenges for policymakers and organizations alike.

### Platform Regulation, Competition, and Regulatory Complexity

The introduction of the Digital Markets Act (DMA) and the Digital Services Act (DSA) represents a bold attempt to reshape the digital marketplace, targeting so-called “gatekeeper” platforms and aiming to even the competitive landscape. These frameworks promote data interoperability and contestability, but also impose substantial compliance obligations—particularly for companies operating across multiple jurisdictions. While such regulations bolster Europe’s capacity to act independently and safeguard its digital economy, they also risk adding layers of complexity that may inadvertently disadvantage smaller firms and non-European competitors and further fragment global digital markets.

### Pursuing Strategic Autonomy and Normative Leadership

The EU’s evolving digital policy agenda is not only reactive to trends toward geopolitical and digital fragmentation; it also seeks to project normative leadership by championing human-centric and rights-based digital governance. Through legislative design and participation in international standard-setting, the EU aspires to set global benchmarks for ethics, privacy, and openness in technology. However, this leadership must be carefully calibrated to ensure that increasing sovereignty does not come at the expense of the inclusivity and usability of global digital ecosystems.

## Connections with Broader Policy and Scholarly Debates

Contemporary scholarship and EU policy deliberations highlight the nuanced interplay between sovereignty and openness in the digital arena. Some advocates propose “modular sovereignty”—granting autonomy over select policy domains while upholding broader commitments to global Internet openness. European practice reflects this pragmatism: policies have relaxed localization requirements for scientific research and innovation, while retaining strict controls over data flows involving critical infrastructure and sensitive public sector services.

Variations in regulatory interpretation and enforcement among member states underscore the need for ongoing dialogue and harmonization. The GDPR’s uneven application has spurred calls for streamlined guidance and joint oversight mechanisms, ensuring a consistent baseline for digital rights and compliance across the continent.

## Practical Implications for Privacy Workshops

### Adapting to Changing GDPR Frameworks and International Data Transfers

Privacy workshops should address the evolving landscape of GDPR enforcement and guidance, particularly as new adequacy decisions, regulatory clarifications, and sector-specific rules emerge. Topics like the implementation of updated Standard Contractual Clauses, the EU-U.S. Data Privacy Framework, and the implications for organizations engaged in cross-border data processing should be given priority.

### Balancing Data Flows with Principles of Open Internet

Participants must understand recent legal decisions (such as Schrems II) and legislative initiatives (Data Act, Data Governance Act) that influence data transfers with non-EU countries. Workshops should foster critical discussion on maintaining core Internet attributes like interoperability, transparency, and the free flow of information even as sovereignty-driven restrictions become more prevalent. Equipping professionals to navigate and mediate the tension between data protection and digital openness is key.

### Recommendations for Workshop Integration and Organizational Practice

- Incorporate privacy engineering strategies that proactively address data localization and sector-specific requirements.
- Offer practical training on conducting transfer impact assessments in light of evolving DSA and DMA rules, and on integrating privacy-by-design principles into vendor procurement and system development.
- Highlight case studies and scenario-based exercises that illuminate the real-world implications of EU digital sovereignty policies for businesses and public sector entities.

## Conclusion

The pursuit of digital sovereignty in the EU is shaping legislative, regulatory, and organizational approaches to technology governance. While EU policy makers continue to promote strategic autonomy, robust privacy rights, and technological competitiveness, they must also ensure that Europe remains connected to—and an influential leader within—the global digital landscape. For practitioners and stakeholders, staying informed and agile in this dynamic environment is essential. Privacy workshops, policy reviews, and cross-sector collaborations offer vital opportunities to deepen understanding, align best practices, and influence the direction of digital governance in Europe and beyond.
