# Neukölln School Pilot Feedback – Actionable Insights

## Executive Summary

The Neukölln School Pilot marks a pivotal chapter in the modernization of education in Germany, particularly as schools navigate the challenges and opportunities of digital transformation. Stakeholder feedback gathered throughout the pilot offers a wealth of evidence-based recommendations for policymakers, school leaders, teachers, and community partners. Key findings point to the necessity of continuous professional development, reliable technological infrastructure, greater inclusion of student perspectives, transparent communication with parents, dedicated time for staff collaboration, and the alignment of digital strategies with each school’s distinct culture. These insights are not only applicable to Neukölln, but are also highly relevant to other urban districts aiming for sustainable, scalable, and collaborative technology integration across all levels of their education systems.

## In-Depth Analysis of Key Recommendations

### 1. Expanding Digital Literacy Training for Teachers

Frau Becker, an experienced member of the teaching staff and vocal proponent of the pilot, emphasized the ongoing need for structured, comprehensive digital literacy programs for both new and veteran teachers. Many educators expressed anxiety about being expected to use emerging technology without sufficient training or time to develop their skills. International benchmarks, particularly from Finland and Estonia, demonstrate the positive impact of regular, collaborative digital upskilling on teaching quality and student outcomes.

The evidence is compelling:

- OECD data consistently highlight professional development as the key lever for effective technology adoption in schools.
- Pilot results in Neukölln show a tangible connection between teacher digital confidence and student engagement.

Structured, institutionalized professional development—drawing on frameworks like the European Commission’s DigCompEdu—should be a cornerstone of ongoing digital transformation, ensuring all teachers feel equipped and supported as technology evolves.

### 2. Enhancing Infrastructure Reliability

Stable digital infrastructure proved to be a persistent challenge during the pilot. According to the Digital Coordinator, frequent Wi-Fi outages and outdated hardware disrupted lessons and undermined teachers’ willingness to use new digital tools. This experience echoes findings from similar initiatives in the UK, where infrastructure improvements led to a marked increase in the use and effectiveness of digital resources in the classroom.

Key data include:

- System logs indicated classrooms experienced an average of over two technical interruptions per week.
- Teacher surveys placed poor infrastructure as the top barrier to sustainable technology use.

A prioritization of robust, reliable, and up-to-date infrastructure—supported by preventative maintenance and responsive IT support—is vital to ensure digital initiatives translate into meaningful classroom impact.

### 3. Integrating Student Voice into Technology Decisions

Student representatives on the council advocated for a greater role in the selection and evaluation of digital platforms, noting that students and teachers often approach technology differently. Focus group discussions affirmed that students are more engaged when their preferences and usage habits are taken into account. Internationally, participatory approaches to technology governance have been shown to not only boost engagement but also ensure solutions meet actual user needs.

The pilot underlined the benefits of:

- Actively including students in decision-making processes related to educational technology.
- Using feedback mechanisms, such as advisory boards or periodic surveys, to capture student perspectives.

By formalizing student participation, schools can foster a sense of ownership and ensure technology solutions align with both teaching objectives and students’ real-world experiences.

### 4. Transparent Communication on Data Privacy

For parents, the security and privacy of their children’s data emerged as a top concern. The Parent Association called for clear, regular updates about how student data is managed, along with easily accessible opt-out choices. Nearly 70% of surveyed parents wanted in-depth information on data policies, reflecting national trends towards greater family engagement and regulatory compliance with digital privacy standards.

Best practices identified in the pilot involve:

- Scheduling quarterly privacy information sessions, including opportunities for questions and feedback.
- Providing translations and accessible formats to reach all families.

Building trust through transparency not only addresses parental concerns but also strengthens community support for digital initiatives.

### 5. Increasing Dedicated Time for In-Person Staff Collaboration

Teaching staff consistently highlighted the need for protected, regular time within their schedules to collaborate, co-design lessons, and mentor one another on digital best practices. International reforms, from Singapore to Finland, demonstrate that schools with institutionalized staff collaboration blocks see higher adoption rates and more effective use of digital tools.

Evidence from interviews and workshops showed:

- Teachers feel isolated and overburdened without structured collaboration opportunities.
- Peer mentoring accelerates the diffusion of digital innovation more effectively than isolated training sessions.

Integrating collaboration time into the timetable, potentially linked with continuing professional development (CPD), is likely to produce more sustainable and meaningful digital innovation.

### 6. Aligning ICT Strategy with School Culture

School leadership underscored that successful digital transformation cannot be imposed from above, but must be shaped in alignment with each school’s unique culture and community values. Digital goals and initiatives are most effective when developed collaboratively with staff and tailored to the specific context of each school.

This lesson is reinforced by:

- Administrative reports showing that generic, top-down ICT mandates often encounter resistance or fall short of expectations.
- The BMBF’s 2020 review, which advocates for context-informed strategy development.

Structured workshops that engage staff and community members in defining a shared ICT vision can foster commitment and ensure digital projects truly serve the needs of the school community.

## Recommendations for the Next Phase of Educator Outreach

### Strategic Initiatives

- **Peer-Led Workshops:** Foster digital learning communities, making use of staff who have demonstrated strong digital competence to mentor colleagues and lead practical sessions.
- **Comprehensive Needs Assessment:** Regular surveys and interviews should identify staff needs, comfort levels with technology, and perceived barriers. This allows outreach and development resources to be precisely tailored.
- **Curated Resource Toolkit:** Compile and share lesson plans, tool guides, and case studies developed during the pilot. Providing practical, ready-to-use materials accelerates adoption and maintains quality.

### Policy Actions

- **Ongoing Professional Development:** Advocate for the local authority and Länder to mandate annual digital professional development as a core part of teaching contracts, to keep skills up-to-date.
- **Recognition and Incentives:** Link contributions to digital innovation (such as developing resources or mentoring others) to advancement and formal recognition within the school system.
- **Equity Monitoring:** Establish mechanisms to track participation and outcomes among all demographic groups, ensuring no school or student segment is left behind in the digital transformation.

### Monitoring and Impact Assessment

- **Key Performance Indicators (KPIs):** Track metrics such as the rate of digital tool adoption, progress in digital competencies for both teachers and students, and levels of engagement following new initiatives.
- **Continuous Feedback:** Involve teachers, students, parents, and administrators in regular feedback cycles, using their input to refine approaches and address emerging challenges promptly.
- **Comparative Analysis:** Where possible, benchmark outcomes in Neukölln pilot schools against matched non-participating schools to understand the full impact of interventions.
