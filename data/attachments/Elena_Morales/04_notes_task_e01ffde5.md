# February 20th – Growing Curiosity: Children Cultivating Wonder in the Montessori Garden

## Morning Reflections: Experiencing the Montessori Garden

This morning greeted us with a cool mist that felt refreshing—perfect weather for our gardening project with the Montessori students, Amelia by my side. Standing at the garden gate, I could sense the buzz of anticipation in the children’s voices, full of questions and eager energy even before we set foot in the garden. Drawing on Montessori principles, I took extra care to prepare an environment tailored to their needs. Child-sized gloves and tools waited in a basket, and I invited each child to choose their own task, fostering an authentic sense of independence, just as Maria Montessori described in *The Absorbent Mind* [1].

As we began by clearing away the remnants of winter’s mulch, the garden came alive with earthy aromas and the satisfying crunch of wood chips beneath our boots. The children’s delight was unmistakable with every earthworm discovered wriggling in the soil. Their fascination prompted a cascade of questions: “Why do earthworms live under the mulch?” “How do they help our plants?” These moments of curiosity fueled rich, open-ended discussions about soil health, the important work earthworms do to keep our beds aerated, and the interconnected web of life present in our little ecosystem—true to the Montessori emphasis on child-led inquiry.

Each child chose seeds to plant, with peas and radishes catching their attention because of their distinctive shapes and colors. Planting was a collaborative process—groups of two or three worked together to measure rows, nestle seeds into the loamy earth, and gently water their plots. These tasks not only supported practical life skills but also encouraged teamwork and quiet leadership among peers. As one child wondered aloud, “What will our peas look like when they grow up?” we explored the concept of life cycles, patience, and nurturing growth, reframing their questions to deepen their thinking: “What do you think our pea plants need next?” These reflective moments underscored the children’s capacity for careful observation and reasoning.

Throughout, I noticed how the atmosphere encouraged children to slow down, share ideas, and make discoveries at their own pace. Listening to their spontaneous observations—gentle handling of worms, inventive vocabulary describing sprouting seeds, enthusiastic tidying-up—showed me the value of Montessori gardening as an ever-evolving canvas for learning [2]. Documenting these experiences will let us track individual growth and help shape future projects to spark even more wonder.

## Afternoon: Nurturing Community and Shared Learning

After lunch, the garden drew in even more energy as Lila and our volunteers joined us. It was wonderful seeing the space transform into a true community hub, echoing Montessori’s focus on collective responsibility and interconnectedness [3]. We organized our planting tasks together, pairing older students with younger ones, encouraging them to share what they’d learned about making space for new roots and caring for delicate seedlings.

A highlight arrived when a local gardener stopped by with heirloom carrot seeds. The children gathered eagerly around the donated packet, studying these seeds and marveling at how different they looked from those we planted earlier. When a child asked, “What’s an heirloom?” it opened up conversations about seed saving, family traditions, and the importance of protecting biodiversity. I loved watching them negotiate together where and how to plant these carrots, naturally dividing up responsibilities. Their ability to discuss roles, listen, and collaborate so seamlessly reflected true Montessori peer learning in action.

This afternoon’s collaborative work not only deepened the children’s academic understanding but also strengthened our little community. Volunteers modeled respectful communication and inclusive teaching, reinforcing a sense of belonging that is foundational in our Montessori environment [4]. I saw children connecting insights from earlier—such as the importance of healthy soil and patient care of seeds—to these new experiences with legacy seeds and shared responsibility, revealing a growing mastery of both botanical and social knowledge.

## Evening Reflections and Plans for the Future

As dusk settled, I found myself savoring the richness of today. The garden’s lingering scents, children’s hands caked with soil, their laughter drifting from the beds—all these moments filled me with gratitude. And seeing Oliver, still recuperating, smile at the window as we planted carrots reminded me of the importance of honoring each child’s pace and presence, no matter where they happen to be.

Reflecting on everything we learned today, I feel inspired to continue building opportunities for curiosity and connection. For next season, I plan to organize a seed-saving workshop involving families and local gardeners, so our children can learn directly from community experts. I also want to deepen journaling in the garden—perhaps encouraging each child to keep a log, recording their observations, questions, and hypotheses, helping them see themselves as both explorers and scientists. A plot-to-plate project excites me, highlighting food literacy and environmental stewardship in a way that feels tangible and rewarding. Integrating journaling with daily garden work supports independence, keen observation, and scientific reasoning, in keeping with best Montessori practice [5].

Our Montessori garden truly is a living classroom, shaped each day by curious, caring hands. As I sketch ideas for next season, I’m eager to encourage more child-led initiatives, sustained projects, and intergenerational learning—trusting, as today confirmed, in the boundless capacity of children for wonder when given time and space.

## Moving Forward: Inspiring Growth and Action

Reflecting on February 20th, several priorities stand out for nurturing our garden’s role as a space for learning and community:

- Expanding peer-led projects, allowing children to design and direct planting plans
- Introducing regular journaling, inviting children to record garden observations and reflections
- Hosting seed-saving and biodiversity workshops with community members and families
- Growing new plant varieties with diverse textures and scents to heighten sensory exploration
- Continuing to model open-ended questioning and careful observation, linking inquiry to scientific understanding
- Strengthening partnerships with local gardeners and experts to deepen communal learning

These approaches align closely with Montessori principles of independence, practical engagement, and inquiry, ensuring our garden remains a place where curiosity and growth flourish with every season.
