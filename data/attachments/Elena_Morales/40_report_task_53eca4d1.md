# School Garden Companion Planting Plan  
**Date:** November 12, 2024  
**Author:** Elena Morales  

---

## Statement of Purpose

This Companion Planting Plan is designed to create a vibrant, child-centered school garden at our Portland, Oregon campus, rooted in Montessori values. By intentionally pairing plants and inviting children into every step of the gardening process, we support experiential learning, ecological stewardship, and multisensory discovery for our early childhood learners. The garden is envisioned not just as a collection of beds, but as a living classroom—an environment where children can make observations, practice collaboration, and form lasting connections with nature and each other.

---

## Introduction

Last spring, our zinnia beds became a focal point for both beauty and curiosity in our outdoor classroom. Watching children cluster around the blooms—touching petals, counting bees, eagerly comparing notes—reminded me how natural environments spark wonders that structured lessons sometimes cannot. Their questions about which plants grow best together and why certain insects visit specific flowers led us to explore the concept of companion planting.

Inspired by their enthusiasm and the Montessori commitment to hands-on education, this year’s garden plan places intentional plant partnerships at its core. Each pairing is chosen to highlight ecological interdependence, encourage sensory exploration, and provide opportunities for authentic participation. As I often remind my students, “To understand the world, we must participate in it.” This principle is woven throughout our garden’s routines and structure.

Observing seedlings emerge, noting pollinator visits, and engaging in tactile work—these are not just curriculum add-ons but fundamental Montessori practices that foster independence and genuine learning. Through these garden activities, children become active stewards and thoughtful observers, building knowledge from real experiences that are both joyful and meaningful.

## Companion Plant Pairings Table

The table below outlines the main pairings for our upcoming season, tailored to Portland’s temperate climate and our school’s priorities. Each group has been selected for its ecological compatibility, opportunities for sensory and artistic engagement, and alignment with early childhood developmental goals. I've included practical notes for involving children in planting, exploration, and documentation, based on what has worked well in our setting.

| Primary Plant   | Companion Plant(s)       | Benefits                                                          | Child Engagement Notes                                                                               |
|-----------------|-------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Tomatoes**        | Basil, Marigold         | Basil repels pests; marigolds attract pollinators; aromatic foliage supports sensory exploration; observing fruit and flower development invites inquiry | Encourage children to touch and smell basil leaves, describe scents; observe and chart pollinator visits to marigolds                |
| **Zinnias**         | Sunflowers, Nasturtium  | Sunflowers give shade and vertical interest; nasturtiums bring edible flowers and attract beneficial insects; zinnias offer continual blooms and striking colors | Invite children to sketch zinnia blooms; taste nasturtium flowers with supervision; measure sunflower growth each week              |
| **Carrots**         | Radishes, Dill          | Radishes break up soil, aiding carrot growth; dill attracts ladybugs (pest control); varied root shapes provide tactile exploration | Compare carrot and radish shapes during harvest; smell dill together; use harvest for sensory comparison and discussion             |
| **Snap Peas**       | Lettuce, Calendula      | Lettuce shades soil and acts as ground cover; calendula draws pollinators and supplies petals for art and sensory play; peas climb and invite trellis work | Build simple trellises together; count daily pea growth; use calendula petals for crafts and sensory activities                       |
| **Pumpkins**        | Corn, Beans             | Classic “Three Sisters”: beans fix nitrogen; corn provides support; pumpkins’ large leaves shelter soil and wildlife | Have children create plant labels for each “sister”; observe and journal growth rates; discuss how the plants help each other        |
| **Chives**          | Strawberries, Spinach   | Chives deter pests for strawberries; spinach offers quick, leafy harvest; combined, they stimulate taste, touch, and smell | Safely taste chive and spinach leaves; observe and describe strawberry development; encourage language rich in sensory detail         |
| **Beets**           | Bush Beans, Onions      | Bush beans enrich soil; onions deter pests; beets add color and textural variety for exploration | Slice beets for art projects or color discussions; smell onions; learn about soil enrichment and interdependence                      |
| **Kale**            | Nasturtium, Garlic      | Nasturtiums discourage pests; garlic repels aphids; kale is resilient and easy for children to grow and harvest | Feel and describe kale’s texture; record growth stages; sample small leaves and discuss flavor                                        |

## Visual Planning: Garden Sketch

To complement our written plan, I am preparing a hand-drawn garden map that will hang in both the classroom and the garden. True to our early childhood setting—and my own delightfully wobbly drawing hand—the sketch will celebrate imperfection and collaboration. Rather than a polished blueprint, it will capture the genuine excitement I feel (and my students share) when imagining new spaces and possibilities. Children will be invited to annotate, embellish, or add suggestions, ensuring the layout is truly “ours.” This visual tool will prompt discussions around spatial organization, help plan planting locations together, and foster a sense of ownership and pride in our shared environment.

## Reflecting on the Creative Process

Shaping this garden plan has been a journey of observation, adaptation, and shared decision making. Last year’s highlights (such as unexpected pollinator visits and thriving zinnia blooms) guided our initial brainstorming, while some of the season’s challenges—like crowded beds or plants out of sync with the climate—helped refine our choices for this year.

I’ve come to value the continual balance of practicality and creative freedom. Giving children a real voice in decisions, from seed selection to garden bed arrangement, not only builds responsibility but also deepens enthusiasm. The “imperfect” garden sketch was inspired by a child’s suggestion that our first drawing “look more like real life”—an observation that brought the whole process into focus for me.

Throughout, I’ve tried to embed routines of observation and dialogue, encouraging children and colleagues alike to document garden changes, celebrate successes, and problem-solve together as we adapt to what each season brings. In doing so, the plan remains flexible and responsive—reflecting the dynamism of both the natural world and Montessori philosophy.

## Next Steps

To ensure the successful implementation of this Companion Planting Plan, I recommend the following actions moving forward:

- **Classroom Engagement:** Invite children to plant each pairing, create garden labels, and help design the garden sketch. Empower them to make choices and share ideas throughout setup.
- **Ongoing Documentation:** Integrate journals, charts, and photo documentation into routines. Encourage recordkeeping of plant growth, insect sightings, and sensory observations to make learning visible.
- **Curriculum Integration:** Schedule regular garden tasks into daily or weekly routines, tying them directly to Montessori themes (like botany, art, and practical life) and seasonal celebrations.
- **Family and Community Involvement:** Share updates and garden progress through newsletters and special event days. Welcome parents and community members to participate, strengthening multi-generational connections to nature.
- **Continuous Adaptation:** Meet regularly to reflect on what’s working, invite suggestions for improvement, and adapt to unexpected challenges or opportunities that arise as the garden evolves.
- **Professional Growth:** Reference current Montessori gardening resources and my own published work to stay informed about best practices, ensuring the project remains educationally sound and inspiring.
