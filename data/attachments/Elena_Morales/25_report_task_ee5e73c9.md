## Reflection on Recent Online Environmental Education Session

Our most recent online session gave me a wonderful window into how the children engage with local environmental topics when given space to explore at their own pace. The lesson was designed with Montessori principles at the forefront—offering open-ended materials, opportunities for peer collaboration, and encouraging self-directed investigation. Throughout the session, I saw curiosity blossom as the children made sense of new ideas together.

### Key Concepts & Observations

In order to capture their inquiries and engagement styles, I organized my observations into the following table:

| Key Concept                | Sample Child-Led Inquiries                     | Types of Engagement                  | Group Dynamics & Observed Curiosity    |
|----------------------------|------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| Urban Animal Habitats      | "Why do squirrels live in city parks?"         | Drawing local animals, Story-sharing | Strong peer dialog, paired work        |
| Compost & Decomposition    | "What happens to leaves when they disappear?"  | Sensory games, Nature storytelling   | Individual discovery & group sharing   |
| Weather Patterns           | "Where does the rain go?"                      | Outdoor mapping, Weather log         | Group discussion, inviting new voices  |

Curiosity throughout the session was unmistakably high. Students generated their own questions, regularly shared their theories aloud, and frequently looked to peers for discussion and validation. Engagement was multisensory—children drew what they observed, used objects from nature for tactile learning, listened closely to each other’s stories, and asked to revisit materials independently between activities.

Group dynamics seemed especially positive. Smaller groups formed organically, creating a supportive space even for quieter children. These self-selected groups allowed for genuine social inclusion and enabled children to oscillate between independent focus and shared exploration. My facilitation often involved stepping back, listening to the flow of their conversations, and offering gentle prompts when new lines of inquiry emerged.

## New Observations and Emerging Ideas

As part of our outdoor exploration this week, the children and I spent time observing the garden’s shady corners. It was in these overlooked spaces that moss had flourished—resilient and vibrant, thriving quietly where other plants struggled for sunlight. Several children were deeply drawn to the moss, running their fingers over its soft texture, pressing their noses close for a sniff, and asking why it grew where it did.

Reflecting on their interest, I saw immediate alignment with Montessori principles. Encouraging attention to these subtle aspects of the environment deepens their sense of stewardship and fosters independent exploration. Moss, in its simplicity, provides an entry point for rich sensorial discovery—children described its softness, temperature, and fresh scent with a vocabulary inspired by our classroom sensorial materials. Further, our investigation naturally connected to Cosmic Education; as we discussed reasons for moss’s success in shade, children began to theorize about water, sunlight, and interconnected plant life, leading to a broader appreciation for ecological adaptation.

### Ideas for Bringing Moss Inquiry into the Classroom

1. **School Garden Project:** I will invite the children to help create a “moss map” of the school grounds, marking where moss is thriving and brainstorming reasons why those spots are hospitable. This will nurture observation skills and promote scientific thinking through real-world context.

2. **Sensorial Activities:** I plan to assemble a sensorial tray featuring collected moss and a range of other botanical materials. Children can touch, smell, and even describe these specimens, encouraging descriptive language use and early skills of classification.

3. **Peer-to-Peer Teaching:** I will encourage our older children to take the lead in sharing their moss discoveries, perhaps by guiding younger classmates in setting up simple moss microhabitats. This not only supports leadership growth but also reinforces collaborative and cooperative learning.

## Next Steps and Planned Adjustments

Building on these insights, I am organizing several activities to continue supporting the children’s budding environmental interests and independence:

- **Garden Discovery Basket:** I’ll prepare a basket with hand lenses, moss samples, blank paper, and colored pencils so children can closely examine, record, and illustrate their findings while outdoors.
  
- **Silent Observation Walk:** We will have periods of quiet exploration in the garden, with the theme “Who is living where?” Children can draw or take notes on their discoveries, providing space for solitary investigation and focused observation.

- **Group Circle Discussion:** I will hold an open-ended discussion, posing the question: “How does moss survive in the shade?” Children’s responses and theories will be recorded in our group thinking journal, further validating their ideas and sparking deeper conversation.

- **Mixed-Age Peer Groups:** Working in cross-age teams, children will create simple moss terrariums, sharing responsibilities and learning together through hands-on work. This structure promotes mentorship, cooperative problem-solving, and shared stewardship over living things.

- **Classroom Agreements:** Together we’ll revisit our classroom’s agreements about caring for living specimens, reflecting on our experiences with moss. This helps build a shared sense of responsibility and respect for all forms of life we encounter, reinforcing our commitment to gentle, mindful engagement with nature.

## Personal Reflections

Today, I noticed how intentionally slowing the pace of the classroom impacted both the rhythm of our day and the children’s engagement. By allowing more space for silence—during transitions, observation, and even moments of excitement—children seemed calmer and more attentive. Some were able to notice details they might have otherwise missed, such as the quiet presence of moss or a barely audible insect in the grass.

After online sessions, classroom energy can fluctuate dramatically. I find that brief moments outdoors and shared mindful breathing—simple, grounding, and accessible for all—help restore calm and help us transition smoothly. This practice also supports my own equilibrium and mirrors the steady, observant presence I hope to embody as a Montessori guide.

Balancing attentive self-care with an approachable, steady teaching presence remains ongoing and vital work for me. Today’s experiences affirmed that when I practice patience and model a calm, prepared mind, the children are more likely to do the same. This mutual encouragement is at the heart of Montessori teaching and is why I value our shared journey of observation, discovery, and growth.
