Willow Creek Montessori Classroom Activity Log

Lead Montessori Teacher: Elena Vasquez

Classroom: Willow Creek Montessori Primary

Date: 2024-04-15

Subjects Covered: Practical Life, Sensorial Exploration, Nature Studies, Creative Expression, Social-Emotional Development

Overview

On April 15, 2024, our classroom at Willow Creek Montessori focused on integrating Practical Life, Sensorial Exploration, Nature Studies, Creative Expression, and Social-Emotional Development across a variety of activities. Each segment of our day was designed to foster independence, curiosity, and collaboration in our young learners, closely following core Montessori principles.

Morning Circle

This morning, we began with warm greetings and a reminder about our classroom’s shared values: kindness, respectful listening, and care for both materials and each other. As part of our ongoing "nature and creativity" theme for April, I invited the children to share their thoughts about how nature inspires their imagination.

During show-and-tell, Amelia Torres presented her painted rock, explaining how she found it in our school garden and chose its colors inspired by spring flowers. She carefully described each shade—purple for violets, yellow for marigolds, blue for the sky—sharing her creative process and engaging her classmates in conversation.

Samuel and Noor complimented her choice of colors, sparking a discussion about different flowers and natural objects in our garden. Mila expressed a desire to touch the rock, which provided an opportunity to reinforce respectful interaction (“If Amelia is comfortable, you may gently feel it” became a model for honoring others’ boundaries). The group’s spontaneous suggestions for creative reuse of natural materials—including a future rock garden mural—demonstrated how shared dialogue can lead to collective creative plans.

Through this morning circle, the class was actively engaged in Montessori’s cosmic education approach, connecting sensory experiences in nature to art and oral storytelling. The interaction also offered practical opportunities for self-expression and collaborative problem-solving, closely aligned with the values of our learning community.

Developmental Observations: - Children used descriptive vocabulary, articulated open-ended questions, and showed attentive listening. - Amelia responded graciously to her peers’ inquiries, while others negotiated turn-taking and shared ideas freely. - Many described the painted rock using sensory language (“smooth,” “bumpy,” “cool colors”), indicating keen observation. - Several children suggested new art projects, reflecting generative thinking and increasing group engagement.

Sensorial Workstations

Throughout the morning, students participated in four sensorial activity stations, each tailored to support hands-on exploration, creative expression, and accommodation of individual learning needs. Rotating through these stations encouraged both independent work and peer collaboration.

1. Rock Sorting and Texture Table
At the central workspace, children examined garden rocks, using magnifying glasses to observe and describe subtle differences in texture and color. Many took turns sorting rocks by touch, practicing tactile discrimination while expanding their vocabulary (“rough,” “smooth,” “grainy”). Visual cue cards supported our non-verbal learners, and padded seating welcomed those who preferred a flexible working posture.

2. Water Pouring Station
By the window, students poured brightly colored water from pitchers into graduated containers, actively refining both gross and fine motor coordination. The activity prompted lively estimates on volume (“Will the blue water fill this cup to the top?”), integrating foundational math concepts. Adaptations—like lighter pitchers and stools—ensured that every child could participate comfortably.

3. Nature Collage Center
At the horseshoe-shaped table, children created collages using leaves, twigs, flower petals, and paper. This station fostered choice and sequencing, as students freely arranged natural materials while discussing the artistic reasoning behind their layouts. For those with developing fine motor skills, larger papers and pre-cut pieces made collage creation accessible and enjoyable.

4. Scent Matching Workstation
In a quiet corner, jars of lavender, citrus, and rosemary invited children to match scents and record their preferences. Those sensitive to strong smells wore “opt-out” badges and enjoyed quiet mat time with nature journals nearby. Discussions here were particularly rich, as children compared notes and hypothesized about plant origins.

The stations were arranged to facilitate independent exploration, but peer guidance emerged organically—older students often modeled activities for younger ones, supporting cross-age mentoring central to the Montessori method.

Staff Collaboration and Support

Our teaching staff collaborated closely to support each child’s needs throughout the day. Mrs. Patel managed transitions between stations, provided gentle prompts regarding Grace and Courtesy, and lent individualized encouragement to children who were tentative about tactile materials during collage-making.

Mr. Kim was stationed outdoors, ensuring safety during gardening and facilitating shared use of watering cans. His open-ended questions (“What do you notice happening to the leaves as you water?”) spurred scientific conversation and deepened observation skills. He also calmly addressed moments of frustration, helping children regulate their emotions and remain engaged.

As a team, we prioritized open, respectful communication—anticipating children’s needs, validating their feelings, and guiding their choices without criticism. Our use of visual cues and concise verbal instructions maintained a peaceful flow throughout transitions, allowing for spontaneous learning and partnership.

Reflections

The morning circle yielded dynamic exchanges and creative ideation, demonstrating the value of self-expression and community dialogue in our classroom. The sensory stations were actively used, especially the scent-matching corner, where focused observation and peer-led explanations stood out. In the garden, children took on support roles naturally, forming collaborative pairs and demonstrating growing independence and social cohesion.

Several children chose to observe rather than participate actively at certain stations, reflecting the diverse comfort levels within the group. Visual supports proved effective for hesitant learners, and I plan to introduce more tactile prompts and encouragement going forward. Additionally, as the children expressed interest in continuing garden tasks beyond scheduled transitions, I recognize the need to expand our outdoor exploration time.

Emergent interests have surfaced through student proposals, such as expanding marigold planting to create a “flower rainbow.” We will move forward with a collective planting project and introduce color-based plant sorting to build on this enthusiasm.

Adjustments for individual sensitivities—including alternative activities for those uncomfortable with strong scents and extra language scaffolding for quieter children—will remain a focus. The children’s growth in independence, leadership, and respectful collaboration affirms that our classroom environment is nurturing their development according to Montessori best practices.

Future Plans

  • Launch a collaborative Nature Art Mural, inspired by today’s morning circle discussion.
  • Broaden garden observation responsibilities, enabling daily documentation of plant growth to encourage scientific inquiry.
  • Facilitate further opportunities for independent workstation selection, with a rotating peer leader role to foster responsibility and mutual support.

Classroom Dynamics

Increasingly, children are choosing tasks independently and assisting classmates naturally. Peer collaboration and respectful communication are thriving, evidence of consistent Grace and Courtesy lessons. The classroom’s evolving atmosphere is both child-centered and purposeful, driven by observation and a commitment to authentic Montessori pedagogical principles.
