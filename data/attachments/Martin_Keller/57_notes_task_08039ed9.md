## 2. New Discoveries: Aleppo Pepper Blend & Sumac

### Aleppo Pepper Blend

I have long admired Levantine Gewürze’s spice counter (Stall 22), but today I finally picked up their Aleppo pepper blend. The aroma alone—fruity, almost raisin-like with a gentle smokiness—was enough to spark recipe ideas.

- **Culinary Use**  
  This blend has quickly become a versatile staple for me. I love sprinkling it on roasted vegetables, grilled chicken, or just over a dish of yogurt with olive oil. It blends wonderfully into vinaigrettes or as a finishing touch for eggs and simple flatbreads.  
- **Flavor Profile**  
  The moderate heat (about 5,000–10,000 SHU) is balanced by a rounded sweetness and some subtle tang. The addition of sunflower oil and salt keeps the flakes soft and flavors intact. No harsh bitterness, just pleasant fruit notes and a rich, sun-dried color.  
- **Care & Storage**  
  To keep the blend lively, I’ve transferred it into a dark, airtight container in my pantry—out of sunlight, away from any humidity. I expect it to remain vibrant for at least the next 9 to 12 months, though I suspect I’ll use it up much sooner.

### Sumac

Sumac is one of those seasonings I associate with Levantine and Mediterranean food, but I’ve rarely stocked it at home. After tasting a dip at a local café last week, I decided to bring some home today.

- **Culinary Use**  
  This sumac (pure ground berries, no additives) brings a bright tartness in place of lemon juice or vinegar—so useful for balancing rich foods or perking up salads, lentils, fish, and grilled meats. I love adding a pinch to labne, or directly onto tomatoes and onions for a fattoush-esque side.  
- **Flavor Profile**  
  The main flavor comes from its natural malic and citric acids, giving it a clean, almost berry-like acidity. The deep color hints at tannins, which add a slight astringency and balance the overall taste—never sharp or harsh, just fresh and vivid.  
- **Storage**  
  Like the Aleppo pepper, I keep sumac in a tightly closed container out of the light; this retains both its deep color and sprightly flavor for about 6 to 10 months.

Meeting the owner at Levantine Gewürze made this purchase even nicer—a quick chat about their sourcing and freshness standards gave me confidence in the quality.

## 3. Inspiration & Meal Planning: Modular Cooking

Shopping at the Wochenmarkt always puts me in a planning mindset—how can I use these peak ingredients for efficient, flexible meals through the week?

- **Spices for Modular Dishes:**  
  Aleppo pepper and sumac lend themselves to “modular” flavoring. I often prepare a large batch of roasted vegetables or a simple legume salad, then use a finishing dusting of either spice (or both) to create distinct variations in each serving. This way, a single prep session becomes multiple unique meals.

- **Batch-Cooking Produce:**  
  Tomatoes, cucumbers, and eggplants are easy to prep in advance. For instance, I’ll roast the eggplants whole or in cubes, storing them separately to toss into salads, grain bowls, or as a base for a mezze plate. Chopped tomatoes and cucumbers hold well for a day or two.

- **Chickpeas as a Base:**  
  Cooking a pot of dried chickpeas sets me up for several days: some for hummus, some for stews or salads. With simple additions—maybe a splash of olive oil, some feta, and a dusting of Aleppo pepper—each bowl gets its own character.

- **Bread & Feta for Quick Lunches:**  
  The hearty sourdough and feta are perfect for assembling open-face sandwiches, or for building a simple board with olives, veggies, and whatever herbs I have on hand. I like letting everyone at the table add their own sumac or spice mix.

- **Meal Engineering:**  
  I’ve found that designing components to be interchangeable is key to keeping mealtime interesting. Roasted vegetables taste just as good next to grilled chicken as they do with chickpeas for a plant-based plate. I try to batch-cook items I know will hold (like legumes or roasted veg), then brighten them up with a last-minute hit of sumac or Aleppo pepper.

- **Staying Organized:**  
  Prepping ingredients into standardized, labeled containers—usually by type and date—saves me time and mental energy throughout the week. A simple fridge chart reminds me which blends pair best with which base, and keeps food waste to a minimum.

Today’s market visit helped me stock up for precisely this kind of flexible, low-effort but flavorful cooking.

## 4. Next Steps & Ongoing Notes

### New Recipes to Try
Armed with fresh vegetables and my new spices, I’m eager to make:
- **Fattoush Salad** with tomatoes, cucumbers, and plenty of sumac ([Fattoush recipe, Serious Eats](https://www.seriouseats.com/fattoush-salad-recipe))
- **Roasted Eggplant with Aleppo Pepper Yogurt**, inspired by Ottolenghi ([Recipe, The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2011/jun/25/roasted-aubergine-recipe-ottolenghi))
- **Classic Hummus** topped with a swirl of Aleppo pepper oil ([NYT Cooking](https://cooking.nytimes.com/recipes/1016071-classic-hummus))

### Vendors to Revisit
I always keep a shortlist for future trips:
- **Levantine Gewürze (Stall 22):** Always fresh, aromatic spice blends; the Aleppo pepper is a standout.
- **Gemüsehof Bauer (Stall 8):** Consistently great heirloom and organic vegetables, especially now in midsummer.
- **Bäckerei Stein (Stall 3):** The sourdough holds its moisture well for several days, and the rye blend brings a satisfying depth.

### Storage & Kitchen Notes
- All spices are transferred into airtight, light-blocking containers to preserve oils and vibrancy.
- Parsley stays freshest when kept stem-down in a jar with a little water in the fridge—I remember to change the water daily.
- The sourdough is best sliced and frozen if I don’t finish it quickly; it toasts perfectly from frozen.
- Feta keeps best submerged in its brine; I always hold onto any extra brine to avoid the cheese drying out.
