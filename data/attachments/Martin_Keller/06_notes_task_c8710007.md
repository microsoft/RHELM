## 2. Anna’s Controlled Breathing Drill — Technical Details

As part of my ongoing focus on improved endurance and breath control, I incorporated a controlled breathing drill, commonly recommended by swim coaches and outlined in sources like "Swim Smooth" and the American Red Cross manual. Anna’s version emphasizes alternating breathing intervals to challenge CO₂ tolerance and bilateral integration.

**Drill Purpose:**  
This drill targets breath control, increases the body’s capacity to tolerate higher levels of carbon dioxide, and encourages a smoother, more efficient bilateral breathing technique.

**Execution:**
- **Starting Off:** I pushed off the wall in a streamlined position, focusing on controlled exhalation before the first breath.
- **Interval Structure:**  
    1. Swam 25 meters freestyle, taking a breath every three strokes (thus alternating breathing sides).
    2. After the first 25m, switched to breathing every five strokes for the next 25m—purposefully lengthening exhalation and managing the build-up of CO₂.
    3. Alternated between these two breathing rhythms for four sets in total (4 x 50m).
    4. Between each set, I took a deliberate 30-second rest, focusing on deep, full exhalation, restoring my regular breathing pattern before tackling the next repetition.

**Points of Focus:**  
- I maintained an even, careful stroke rhythm throughout each set, paying attention to exhaling thoroughly underwater and resisting the tendency to hold my breath, which only increases tension.
- I kept my neck and shoulders relaxed; when fatigue set in, I consciously neutralized my gaze to avoid unnecessary head lift or eye strain.
- Consistency was key: I made sure there was no form breakdown, particularly during the more demanding five-stroke segments.

**Drill Summary (for tracking):**
```plaintext
FOR each of 4 sets:
    Swim 25m freestyle, breathing every 3 strokes
    Swim 25m freestyle, breathing every 5 strokes
    Rest for 30 seconds, practicing deep exhalation and regular breathing
END FOR
```

**Expected Outcomes:**  
With this drill, I noticed not only a marked improvement in my respiratory control but also a more consistent, relaxed head position, resulting in smoother bilateral breathing and less anxiety when the urge to breathe increased. Over time, I expect these adaptations to translate into greater endurance, more efficient oxygen uptake, and a calmer, more controlled swim, especially during longer distances.

## 3. Personal Reflection and Session Insights

**Physical State and Motivation:**  
I started the session feeling energetic—I'd rate my energy at about 7 out of 10—and settled quickly into a rhythm. By the end, I was experiencing moderate but manageable fatigue, which I’d attribute to the cumulative challenge of the technique and breathing drills. My peak heart rate during the more intense sets hovered around 135–145 bpm (checked via 10-second pulse count immediately after sets and multiplied by six), returning to about 110 bpm during recovery laps and cool-down.

On the Borg rate of perceived exertion scale, I averaged about a 13 ("somewhat hard") during the main sets, with a drop to 9 ("very light") for the gentle laps and stretching, which felt restorative.

**Eye Comfort:**  
Managing eye strain remains a top priority for my indoor swims. The strategies I implemented today—frequent focal shifts, regular anti-fogging, loosening strap tension, and periodic eyes-closed rest—helped a great deal. I finished the session with only mild eye watering and no lingering soreness or discomfort, indicating the management plan is working well.

**Progress Toward Goals:**  
I've set clear targets for this training phase: build aerobic endurance, enhance breath control, and refine my head alignment. Today’s session, particularly through the bilateral breathing drill, reinforced the importance of consistent exhalation and technical discipline under mild hypoxia. I was satisfied with my execution—no major dips in form or technique, and I felt at ease maintaining eye comfort throughout. This gives me confidence to gradually step up either the intensity or complexity of upcoming workouts.

**Motivation and Emotional Response:**  
Halfway through the breathing drill, I felt a familiar tension in my chest and shoulders, which initially tempted me to shorten the interval or cheat the breathing pattern. But pushing through, focusing on deep exhalation and form, brought a surprising sense of calm. Completing all sets as planned left me with real satisfaction and motivation for the next session, knowing improvement is tangible and sustainable. There’s also satisfaction in keeping my eyes comfortable, as previous sessions were hampered by irritation that lingered for hours after.
