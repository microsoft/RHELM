## 1. Ingredients and Detailed Preparation Steps

### Grilled Chicken

| Ingredient                    | Quantity           | Brand/Source               | Notes                                    |
|-------------------------------|--------------------|----------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| Chicken breast fillets        | 640 g (4 pieces)   | Bell & Evans, Whole Foods  | Patted dry                               |
| Olive oil                     | 22 mL              | California Olive Ranch     | Extra virgin                             |
| Lemon juice                   | 20 mL              | Organic, local market      | Fresh-squeezed                           |
| Garlic                        | 10 g (~3 cloves)   | Spice House                | Minced                                   |
| Aleppo Fire spice blend       | 7 g                | Ziyad Local Market         | Main seasoning; see Section 2            |
| Black pepper                  | 2 g                | Morton & Bassett           | Freshly ground; reduced for balance      |
| Kosher salt                   | 6 g                | Diamond Crystal            | Evenly distributed                       |

**Preparation Steps:**

I started by making the marinade: whisking together the olive oil, fresh lemon juice, minced garlic, Aleppo Fire spice blend (using 5 g for the chicken at this stage), black pepper, and kosher salt in a large bowl. The marinade’s aroma immediately had that smoky-citrus fragrance I associate with Levantine food. I added the chicken breasts, turning them until each piece was well coated, then covered the bowl and set it in the fridge for about 40 minutes. I made sure the marinade was distributed evenly; a splash more oil would be added if it ever seemed a bit too thick or dry.

When it was time to grill, I preheated my grill pan to just over 200°C (about 400°F) for a good five minutes. I shook any excess marinade from the chicken (to avoid flare-ups), then laid the breasts on the sizzling pan. They took between 6 and 7 minutes per side for a nice crust. I checked the internal temperature after 14 minutes—my target was 74°C (165°F), and I continued flipping in one-minute intervals until each piece hit that mark. Once done, I tented the chicken under foil for five minutes, letting the residual heat bring the temperature up slightly and the juices redistribute.

**Preparation Steps:**

While the chicken marinated, I set the bulgur in a heatproof bowl and poured 65 mL of just-boiled water over it, covering tightly and letting it steam for about 15–20 minutes—just long enough for the grains to turn tender but still keep their bite. If it ever seemed undercooked, a dash more boiling water and an extra 5 minutes solved it.

Meanwhile, I chopped the parsley and mint (removing tough stems), diced the tomato and cucumber, and sliced the green onions. Once the bulgur was ready, I fluffed it with a fork and combined it with the freshly chopped herbs and veggies in a large bowl. In went the lemon juice, olive oil, salt, and pepper; a careful toss ensured every grain and leaf was dressed. At this point, I always tuck the bowl in the fridge for at least 10 minutes to let the flavors marry and mellow—the tabbouleh is so much brighter for it.

I salted it to taste, then gave it a quick taste test—sometimes a little more harissa or an extra squeeze of lemon helps balance the heat and acidity. Right before serving, I finished the dip with a glossy drizzle of olive oil across the top.

## 2. Evaluating the New “Aleppo Fire” Spice Blend

### Composition, Origin, and Initial Impressions

- **Product Name:** Aleppo Fire Spice Blend  
- **Source:** Ziyad Local Market, Near North Side  
- **Tradition/Origin:** Inspired by Syrian flavors (specifically Aleppo), blended and packed in the USA  
- **Ingredients:** Aleppo pepper flakes, cumin, sumac, sun-dried tomato, smoked paprika, sea salt, dried lemon peel  
- **Flavor Profile:**  
  - Warmth from Aleppo pepper  
  - Sharp, tangy citrus from sumac and lemon peel  
  - Savory, earthy undertones (cumin, sun-dried tomato)  
  - A hit of smokiness and salt, all finely milled for easy blending

Opening the jar for the first time, the aroma was instantly inviting—a punch of dried tomato, subtle smoke, and that distinct sweet-citrus note from sumac. The blend looked vivid, with red flecks balanced by the darker, earthier powders.

### Recipe Adjustments and Rationale

Because the Aleppo Fire blend already includes pepper and salt, I decreased the amount of added black pepper in the chicken marinade from my usual 3 g to 2 g, to avoid overwhelming the other flavors. I chose not to alter the classic tabbouleh recipe, keeping it traditional so I could gauge the spice blend's impact more clearly on the other components. I also used the blend in both the chicken and the yogurt sauce, hoping to test how well it worked across different textures and temperature environments.

### Outcome and Observations

- **Grilled Chicken:** The chicken developed a striking bronze crust scattered with deep red flecks, instantly upping the plate appeal. The flavors after grilling were noticeably more complex than my standard chicken—there was gentle chili warmth, plenty of citrus, and a smoky finish. The meat stayed especially moist, which I credit to the blend’s combo of salt and acidity.
- **Yogurt Dip:** The blend played perfectly against the harissa’s heat, adding tanginess and a subtle smokiness. Visually, the dip took on a beautiful reddish hue, which was incredibly inviting.
- **Versatility and Limitations:**  
  The spice dissolved cleanly in both the oil and the thick yogurt, with no graininess. The overall heat was moderate—a solid baseline for guests, and easy to adjust with extra harissa or the blend itself if necessary. I was particularly pleased with how seamlessly it integrated across dishes.

## 3. Reflections and Opportunities for Future Improvement

Over dinner, I was struck by how balanced everything tasted—the Aleppo Fire blend added real depth and a genuine Middle Eastern flavor without ever overpowering any one dish. The acidity from the lemon and sumac kept each bite lively, while the tabbouleh, with all its fresh herbs, worked as a bright counterpoint and palate cleanser.

The chicken was just how I like it: juicy inside with a crisp, seasoned exterior. The bulgur in the tabbouleh was fluffy and distinct—not mushy—while the vegetables stayed crisp. That herbaceous, lemony tabbouleh is classic for a reason, and it contrasted nicely with the aromatic chicken and spicy, tangy yogurt dip.

As for improvements, I’d like to try a longer marination—perhaps two full hours next time—to see if the spices further permeate the meat. I’m also thinking about swirling in a bit of pomegranate molasses to the yogurt for added complexity and sweetness.

One thing I want to be more consistent about is recording chicken breast thickness and grill pan preheat times, as I think those factors could help standardize texture and doneness. For future cooking sessions, I've made a note to photograph spice blends and finished plates—these visual records would complement my written notes and help with reproducibility. I'm also considering setting up a simple digital log for quick data entry during prep and cooking.
