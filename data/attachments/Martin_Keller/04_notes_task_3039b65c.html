Spicy Mediterranean Dinner: Chicken Tagine with Harissa, Roasted Vegetables, and Couscous

Meal Log & Recipe Notes
Date Prepared: January 13, 2024

Meal Story & Motivation

Dinner on a Chilly January Evening

January always brings with it a sense of renewal, but also the cold that begs for warmth—food that gathers people close and fills the room with spice and the hum of conversation. Tonight, I wanted to counter the chill with dishes that felt both bold and comforting. The menu I chose—chicken tagine kissed with the heat of harissa, a hearty tray of roasted Mediterranean vegetables, and light, fluffy couscous—felt just right.

Gathering friends around the table, I hoped to revive memories of warm places and vibrant flavors. The meal was designed for four, easily adjustable if I ever want to scale up for a bigger celebration or pare down for an everyday dinner. My goal was not only to get the food right, but also to document each step cleanly, both for myself and for anyone who wants to try their hand at it—whether for learning, teaching, or simply enjoying a satisfying meal.

My Step-by-Step Process

Overview of Timing

  • Prep: ~30 minutes
  • Active Cooking: ~1 hour
  • Total: About 1.5 hours (while tagine simmers, I prep and roast the vegetables and steam couscous)
  • Oven: Preheat to 200°C / 400°F for veggies

1. Chicken Tagine with Harissa

  1. Marinate the Chicken
    I always pat the chicken thighs dry first—helps the marinade cling. Then I mix them with salt, pepper, 1 tbsp olive oil, half the harissa, half the garlic, the cumin, coriander, paprika, and cinnamon. I cover this and let it sit for at least 20 minutes (sometimes overnight if I’m prepping ahead; the flavors only get better).

  2. Start Cooking
    In a heavy-bottomed Dutch oven, I heat the remaining olive oil over medium. The onions go in first, a slow sauté until they’re just soft and translucent (5–6 minutes), then the rest of the garlic. Next, I add the marinated chicken, letting it brown on all sides (about 8–10 minutes total).

  3. Simmer the Sauce
    I pour in the crushed tomatoes, preserved lemon, the remaining harissa, and saffron, then add the chicken stock. Everything comes up to a gentle simmer, then I cover with a tight-fitting lid (or the tagine top) and turn the heat low. This simmers for 40–50 minutes—just enough time for the chicken to go wonderfully tender and the flavors to meld.

  4. Finish & Serve
    I taste and adjust the salt and pepper, skim off any fat that’s floated up, and let it rest—covered and off the heat—for about 10 minutes before serving. Just before bringing it to the table, I scatter the chopped cilantro and parsley on top.

2. Roasted Mediterranean Vegetables

  1. Prep & Season
    All the veggies are chopped into even, bite-sized pieces so they roast at the same rate. In a big bowl, I toss them with olive oil, minced garlic, oregano, cumin, a generous pinch of salt and a few grinds of black pepper, mixing well so everything gets coated.

  2. Roast
    The vegetables go onto a parchment-lined tray in a single layer—crowding will steam them, so I spread them out. Into the preheated oven they go for about 30–35 minutes. I stir once midway to make sure every piece caramelizes nicely.

  3. Finish
    Once roasted and golden at the edges, I take them out and immediately squeeze over some lemon juice and sprinkle with fresh parsley. They’re best just warm—not too hot, so you taste all the flavor.

3. Couscous

  1. Hydrate
    Into a bowl I pour the couscous and salt. I bring the stock (or water) and olive oil just to a boil, pour it straight over the grains, stir once, and cover tightly. I leave it alone for 10 minutes, resisting the urge to peek.

  2. Fluff & Garnish
    When time’s up, I add a knob of butter and use a fork to fluff up the couscous, breaking apart any clumps until the grains are separate and light. If I’m feeling fancy—or if mint is handy—I fold in some chopped fresh mint before serving.

Cooking Notes & Logistics

I always check the chicken’s temperature in the thickest part—it should be at least 74°C (165°F) for safety.

Timing is everything in this meal: as soon as I get the tagine on the stove, I start cutting the vegetables and prepping the couscous, so the meal all comes together at once and nothing’s cold.

Letting the tagine rest, covered, for a few minutes after cooking really does deepen the flavor and makes serving easier since the sauce thickens just a bit more.

How I Served It & Atmosphere

Plating & Presentation

Tonight, I brought the tagine straight to the table, herbs scattered over the bubbling sauce, so everyone could help themselves. The roasted vegetables went onto a big, white platter for color—one of those dishes that looks almost too pretty to eat. Couscous got piled high in its own bowl, flecked with green from the fresh mint.

A small bowl of extra harissa and a few slices of preserved lemon made the rounds, for anyone who wanted an extra punch of heat or tang.

Adapting & Personalizing the Meal

I always document my recipes for four, but it’s easy to scale—just multiply everything per extra person (or halve for two). The mix of vegetables in the roast changes with the market and seasons: sweet potato in fall, asparagus in spring, whatever’s freshest.

Harissa is a deeply personal choice—tonight I used Les Moulins Mahjoub, which is both spicy and complex. Some friends like much more heat, others less. I always taste and tweak.

Whenever there’s leftover chicken or vegetables, I love turning them into salads or wraps the next day—no food wasted.

Homemade stock does make a difference, but a good low-sodium boxed version works well. Sometimes, for elegance, I add toasted almonds or more fresh mint just before serving for crunch and freshness.

Notes & Reflections

This dinner is as much about bringing people together as about the food itself. The tagine’s aroma fills the whole house and draws everyone into the kitchen, and the sound of spoons scraping dishes and laughter over music is its own kind of comfort.

Having a clear, repeatable workflow—ingredients prepped and cooking centralized—frees me up to enjoy the process and the company. It’s a meal I’ll happily return to all winter long.
